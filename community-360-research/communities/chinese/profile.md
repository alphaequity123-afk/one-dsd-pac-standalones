# Chinese in Minnesota

## 1. People and place

**How many.** The answer depends on who is counted and how. Minnesota Compass, using the American Community Survey for 2020 to 2024, counts 45,330 Chinese Minnesotans, give or take 3,937. It places 81.3 percent in the Twin Cities and 18.7 percent in Greater Minnesota ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/chinese)). The state demographer's June 2026 presentation, using the single-year 2024 survey, lists 48,592 Chinese Minnesotans and, separately, 2,545 Taiwanese. In that list Chinese Minnesotans rank third among Asian groups, after Hmong (111,741) and Asian Indian (50,093) residents ([Minnesota State Demographer, June 27, 2026](https://mn.gov/capm/assets/SusanBrower_StateOfAsianPacificMinnesotans_June2026_tcm1051-759113.pdf)). The state's January 2026 economic chartbook uses a narrower rule. It puts people who belong to more than one cultural group in a separate "multiple groups" category, so it counts only about 30,500 Chinese Minnesotans for 2019 to 2023, give or take 3,700, in about 13,100 households ([State Demographic Center, Economic Status of Minnesotans 2026](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). CBS Minnesota, citing census figures in 2023, put the number at 39,622, again third after Hmong and Asian Indian residents, and noted that no other state has the same top three ([CBS Minnesota, May 15, 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/whats-minnesotas-asian-american-and-pacific-islander-history/)). These are survey estimates with wide margins. Read them as a range, not a headcount. As national background only, Pew Research Center counts about 5.5 million people of Chinese origin in the United States in 2023, the country's largest Asian origin group ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/race-and-ethnicity/fact-sheet/asian-americans-chinese-in-the-u-s/)). Compass also notes that "thousands" of Minnesotans adopted as children from China live here, and they appear in these counts only if they or their families report Chinese ancestry ([Minnesota Compass, May 30, 2024](https://www.mncompass.org/data-insights/articles/importance-data-disaggregation-minnesotas-asian-communities)).

**Taiwanese Minnesotans.** A history by the Taiwanese Association of America, Minnesota chapter, says about 32 students from Taiwan were here in 1960, and that the Formosan American Club formed in 1966 to 1967 with about 114 members. It reports 747 people identifying as Taiwanese in the 2000 Census and 1,078 in 2010 (1,375 counting people of more than one ancestry) ([Taiwanese American Historical Archive](https://taiwaneseamericanhistory.org/ourjourneys123-eng/)). Some Taiwanese Minnesotans count themselves as Chinese and some do not; the figures above treat them separately.

**Age and birthplace.** Compass shows 25.9 percent of Chinese Minnesotans are 17 or younger, 12.1 percent are 18 to 24, and 9.5 percent are 65 or older. Foreign-born residents are 56.3 percent, give or take 3.2 points. Women are 53.4 percent ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/chinese)). The state chartbook gives a median age of 35 ([Economic Status of Minnesotans 2026](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). Compass explains that Chinese, Indian and Burmese Minnesotans are more often foreign-born than Hmong Minnesotans because political and economic conditions brought many of them more recently ([Minnesota Compass, May 30, 2024](https://www.mncompass.org/data-insights/articles/importance-data-disaggregation-minnesotas-asian-communities)).

**Where.** Beyond the 81 percent metro share, published Minnesota sources give little detail on neighborhoods. Named places in daily life include the University of Minnesota area (Dinkytown and Stadium Village restaurants), University Avenue in St. Paul, Nicollet Avenue's Eat Street in Minneapolis, Roseville (home of the largest Chinese church), Eden Prairie (Asia Mall and the Chinese Community Center), Bloomington, Plymouth, Woodbury, and Rochester, where a Chinese congregation dates to graduate students working at IBM and Mayo in the 1970s (sections 4, 5 and 10) ([Post-Bulletin, Feb. 17, 2006](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/autumn-ridge-church-tab-chinese-church)). No Minnesota source found gives a current county-by-county count for Chinese residents.

**How people came.** The Minnesota Historical Society's MNopedia dates the first documented Chinese immigrants to 1876; they opened laundries and restaurants in St. Paul and Minneapolis, many having left racial violence on the West Coast ([MNopedia, Immigrants and Refugees in Minnesota, last modified July 24, 2026](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/immigrants-and-refugees-minnesota-connecting-past-and-present)). The society's library guide says "mid-1870s," more than 100 Chinese men by the late 1880s, mostly in St. Paul, Minneapolis and Duluth, and at least six families before 1910 ([MNHS Library](https://libguides.mnhs.org/chinese-americans)). St. Olaf College's Asia in Minnesota project names Wang See Ling, arriving in 1875, as the first recorded Asian immigrant ([St. Olaf College](https://pages.stolaf.edu/aim/wp-json/wp/v2/pages/1607)). The sources disagree by a year or two. MNopedia adds that Chinese immigrants on the Iron Range, in towns such as Eveleth, ran hotels and restaurants serving European immigrant workers, and that as late as the mid-twentieth century Chinese buyers were kept out of affluent suburbs ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/immigrants-and-refugees-minnesota-connecting-past-and-present)).

The federal Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 shaped this early community; it was repealed in 1943, and the 1965 Hart-Celler Act ended the national origins quotas ([MNopedia, Liang May Seen, last modified July 24, 2026](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/liang-may-seen-c1871-1946)). Liang May Seen, the first woman of Chinese descent to live in Minnesota, joined her husband, the restaurateur Woo Yee Sing, in Minneapolis in 1892 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/liang-may-seen-c1871-1946)).

Students make a second, long stream. The first three students from China enrolled at the University of Minnesota in 1914; one of them, Pan Wen Huen, settled in northern Minnesota and worked for a mining company for 40 years ([University of Minnesota China Center](https://chinacenter.umn.edu/umn-china/history/students)). The Chinese American Association of Minnesota (CAAM) describes how kinship and merchant associations gave way after World War II to student and professional networks, then to students from Taiwan and Hong Kong, then, after 1978, to a large migration from mainland China; it counted more than 40 Chinese community organizations by 2008 ([CAAM, History](https://caam.org/history/)). Other doors exist too. Chef Tammy Wong of Rainbow Chinese was born in the Chinese quarter of Saigon and came to the United States with her family in 1979 ([National Trust for Historic Preservation, 2023](https://savingplaces.org/stories/chef-tammy-wong-on-keeping-up-her-minneapolis-restaurant)). A Shakopee family profiled by the city's heritage society came from a village of about 200 people in rural Guangdong, by way of Guangzhou and Hong Kong ([Shakopee Heritage Society](https://www.shakopeeheritage.org/historic-articles/people/yu-heng/)). And Minnesota families adopted children from China from the 1990s until China ended international adoption in September 2024 ([MPR News, Sept. 16, 2024](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2024/09/12/questions-linger-after-china-ends-international-adoptions)).

**Languages.** Among Chinese Minnesotans 5 and older, 62.4 percent speak a language other than English at home and 29.9 percent speak English less than "very well," against 4.6 percent of all Minnesotans ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/chinese); [Minnesota Compass, all Minnesotans](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/minnesota)). The surveys do not split Mandarin, Cantonese and other Chinese languages for Minnesota in any source found. Both Mandarin and Cantonese are in daily use: the largest Chinese church holds separate Mandarin, Cantonese and English services ([Twin City Chinese Christian Church](https://tcccc.org/en/)), and the Chinese Community Center offers tutoring in both ([Chinese Community Center](https://cccminnesota.org/about/)). The Secretary of State publishes voter materials in Chinese among the state's top languages ([Sahan Journal, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/minnesota-non-english-languages-census-data/)).

## 2. All walks of life

No single figure describes this community. Unless noted, numbers below come from the Compass 2020 to 2024 profile, in 2024 dollars, with statewide figures from the same source for comparison ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/chinese); [Minnesota Compass, all Minnesotans](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/minnesota)).

- **Well-off and high-earning households.** Median household income is $109,309, give or take $13,183, against $86,893 for all Minnesota households. More than half of households, 53.2 percent, take in $100,000 or more. The state chartbook puts the median at $102,800 for 2019 to 2023, and median earnings of full-time workers at $78,100, against $61,900 statewide ([Economic Status of Minnesotans 2026](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)).
- **Professionals, scholars and owners.** Among adults 25 and older, 61.2 percent hold a bachelor's degree or more and 32.4 percent hold a graduate or professional degree; statewide, 39.3 percent hold a bachelor's or more. Public careers include Tony Leung, born in Hong Kong in 1959, who in 1994 became Minnesota's first Asian Pacific American judge and in 2011 a federal magistrate judge, serving until May 4, 2025 ([Federal Bar Association, Summer 2025](https://www.fedbar.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Leung-profile_Summer25.pdf)); the urban planner Weiming Lu, who worked on Nicollet Mall and the skyways and led St. Paul's Lowertown Redevelopment Corporation ([UMN China Center](https://chinacenter.umn.edu/umn-china/history/alumni/distinguished-alumni/weiming-lu)); the pipa master Gao Hong of Carleton College ([Carleton College, April 5, 2022](https://www.carleton.edu/news/stories/gao-hong-celebrates-50-years-of-performing/)); and restaurant families such as the Fongs, whose business began as a carryout in 1958 and whose Bloomington restaurant closed in 2022 ([FOX 9, 2022](https://www.fox9.com/news/popular-bloomington-restaurant-david-fongs-set-to-close)). CAAM's founding members in 1967 included a Honeywell research scientist, a Univac scientist, a Minneapolis city planner and a University of Minnesota engineering professor ([CAAM, History](https://caam.org/history/)).
- **Middle-income and working families.** Owner-occupied homes are 69.6 percent of households, close to the state's 72.4 percent. Among adults 18 to 64, 82.7 percent are employed. Another 9.9 percent of people live between the poverty line and twice that line. Restaurant work runs through the family stories found: the Shakopee family above began with parents working long hours in other people's Chinese restaurants before buying their own eight years after arriving ([Shakopee Heritage Society](https://www.shakopeeheritage.org/historic-articles/people/yu-heng/)), and Judge Leung's father, from a poor family in Taishan, gave up a tailoring business in Hong Kong to cook at the Nankin in Minneapolis, while his mother cooked at a restaurant in the St. Paul Hilton ([Federal Bar Association](https://www.fedbar.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Leung-profile_Summer25.pdf)).
- **People in poverty and under strain.** Overall, 9.1 percent of people live below the poverty line (give or take 2.8 points), against 8.1 percent statewide. The rate is 22.9 percent for people 18 to 24, a group that includes many students, and 15.7 percent for people 65 and older, though that figure has a margin of 10.1 points. One household in six (16.1 percent) takes in less than $35,000. Among renters, 36.4 percent spend too much of their income on housing; 11.2 percent of households have no vehicle; 24.2 percent are people living alone. And 14.1 percent of adults 25 and older did not finish high school, almost the same share as hold only a high school diploma (12.1 percent). The Chinese Social Service Center in Minneapolis runs a program for Chinese seniors and says it is open to "citizens and non-citizens" ([United Way 211, updated Nov. 20, 2025](https://search.211unitedway.org/search/eaecc1f2-c03c-5fc8-8d06-0e1147d36001)).

## 3. Work and the economy

**Who works.** Labor force participation among Chinese Minnesotans 16 to 64 is 80 percent, close to the state's 82 percent ([State Demographer, June 2026](https://mn.gov/capm/assets/SusanBrower_StateOfAsianPacificMinnesotans_June2026_tcm1051-759113.pdf)). In the state chartbook, about 77 percent of women and 84 percent of men 16 to 64 are in the labor force ([Economic Status of Minnesotans 2026](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). The state demographer's chart of foreign-born Asian workers 16 to 64 who have been in the United States less than 10 years includes Chinese Minnesotans among the groups shown, and notes that nearly 30 percent of all recent foreign-born labor force growth came from these Asian Pacific groups ([State Demographer, June 2026](https://mn.gov/capm/assets/SusanBrower_StateOfAsianPacificMinnesotans_June2026_tcm1051-759113.pdf)).

**Skilled visas and research.** Compass notes that nearly half of employment-based immigrant visas in recent years have gone to workers from China and India, and that the largest Minnesota sponsors of skilled-worker visas include Optum, U.S. Bank, Mayo Clinic, Medtronic and the University of Minnesota ([Minnesota Compass, May 30, 2024](https://www.mncompass.org/data-insights/articles/importance-data-disaggregation-minnesotas-asian-communities)). China is the second-largest place of origin for the University of Minnesota's international scholars ([UMN Global Snapshot](https://mglobal.umn.edu/global-snapshot)). Rochester's Chinese congregation began in 1974 among graduate students "often associated with IBM or Mayo" ([Post-Bulletin, Feb. 17, 2006](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/autumn-ridge-church-tab-chinese-church)).

**Restaurants and small business.** Food has been a Chinese Minnesotan livelihood from the start. MNopedia identifies Woo Yee Sing and his brother Woo Du Sing's Canton Cafe, opened in 1883, as the first Chinese restaurant in Minneapolis ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/liang-may-seen-c1871-1946)). Walter James opened the Nankin Cafe in 1919 ([CAAM, Walter James](https://caam.org/walter-james/)). Leeann Chin, born in Guangzhou in 1933, came to Minnesota in 1957, worked as a seamstress while raising five children, opened her first restaurant in Minnetonka in 1980, sold her name and restaurants to General Mills in 1985, and left a chain of more than 40 locations at her death in 2010 ([MPR News, March 12, 2010](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2010/03/12/leeann-chin)). Today's restaurants range from family chow mein shops to regional Sichuan, Shandong and Hunan cooking (section 4).

**Business organizing.** CAAM says its business members split off in the early 1970s to form a Chinese Chamber of Commerce, because merchants and professionals had different priorities ([CAAM, History](https://caam.org/history/)). No current Minnesota Chinese chamber of commerce was confirmed. The Chinese Community Center in Eden Prairie lists "creating business opportunities for minority entrepreneurs" among its aims ([Chinese Community Center](https://cccminnesota.org/about/)). Asia Mall in Eden Prairie, a pan-Asian mall opened in late 2022, gave Chinese restaurants such as Home Taste and Legendary Spice a suburban storefront alongside Vietnamese and Korean tenants ([Sahan Journal, Nov. 1, 2022](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/asia-mall-pan-asian-mall-eden-prairie-soft-opening-minnesota/)).

No Minnesota source was found on Chinese workers in unions, on Chinese labor history in Minnesota beyond laundries and restaurants, or on the occupations Chinese Minnesotans hold today.

## 4. Businesses and commercial life

No Chinatown appears in any Minnesota source found. Chinese commercial life shows up in a few clusters: University Avenue in St. Paul, the University of Minnesota's Dinkytown and Stadium Village, Nicollet Avenue's Eat Street, and suburban hubs such as Asia Mall in Eden Prairie. The full list, with the evidence for each, is in the accompanying business file. Highlights:

- **Asia Mall**, 12160 Technology Drive, Eden Prairie: a two-story, roughly 116,000-square-foot pan-Asian mall with the Asia Mart grocery; Chinese tenants at opening included Home Taste and Legendary Spice ([Sahan Journal, Nov. 1, 2022](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/asia-mall-pan-asian-mall-eden-prairie-soft-opening-minnesota/)). Its owners announced a second complex, Asia Village, at Blaine's Northtown Mall ([Eden Prairie Local News, May 2, 2024](https://eplocalnews.org/2024/05/02/developers-of-popular-asia-mall-are-launching-a-second-asian-themed-complex-in-blaine/)).
- **Keefer Court**, a Chinese bakery founded by Sunny and Paulina Kwan on the West Bank in 1983 as a fortune cookie business, closed in December 2022 and reopened at Asia Mall in March 2024 under new owners who bought the recipes and brand ([MPR News, March 15, 2024](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2024/03/15/popular-keefer-court-reopens-in-eden-prairies-asia-mall)).
- **United Noodles**, an Asian grocery that calls itself "Minnesota's Asian Market Since 1972," with stores in Minneapolis and Woodbury ([United Noodles](https://www.unitednoodles.com/)).
- **Rainbow Chinese**, 2739 Nicollet Ave., run by Tammy Wong for more than 36 years; she reopened the dining room in July 2023 ([National Trust, 2023](https://savingplaces.org/stories/chef-tammy-wong-on-keeping-up-her-minneapolis-restaurant)), and its site lists Wednesday-to-Sunday hours ([Rainbow](https://rainbowrestaurant.com/)).
- **Restaurants named in February 2026 Lunar New Year guides**, which show they were operating then: Shuang Cheng (Cantonese, Dinkytown), Tea House (Sichuan and Anhui, Stadium Village), Legendary Spice (Dinkytown), Yangtze (dim sum, St. Louis Park) and JUN Szechuan Kitchen (North Loop) ([Meet Minneapolis, 2026](https://www.minneapolis.org/calendar/holidays/lunar-new-year/); [Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, Feb. 10, 2026](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/where-to-celebrate-lunar-new-year-around-the-twin-cities/)). Peking Garden on University Avenue hosted the Twin Cities Dumpling Feast in February 2026 ([Minnesota Monthly, Feb. 10, 2026](https://www.minnesotamonthly.com/general/celebrate-lunar-new-year-across-minnesota/)).
- **Grand Szechuan**, 10602 France Ave. S., Bloomington, serving Sichuan food since 2008 ([Grand Szechuan](https://grandszechuanmn.com/)).
- **Leeann Chin**, still operating as a regional chain with a 2026 site ([Leeann Chin](https://www.leeannchin.com/)).
- **Closed landmarks:** David Fong's in Bloomington closed at the end of August 2022 after 64 years, though family members' locations in Prior Lake and Savage were to stay open ([FOX 9, 2022](https://www.fox9.com/news/popular-bloomington-restaurant-david-fongs-set-to-close)). Earlier landmarks live on mainly in memory, such as the Nankin, which Walter James opened in Minneapolis in 1919 and where CAAM began ([CAAM, Walter James](https://caam.org/walter-james/)).

Professional firms are the thinnest part of this picture. No Chinese-owned law, accounting, insurance, travel or real estate firm in Minnesota was confirmed open from a current source, and no current Chinese-language newspaper or broadcaster in Minnesota was found. CAAM's bilingual newsletter, News & Views, is listed by the Minnesota Historical Society as published from 1994 ([MNHS Library](https://libguides.mnhs.org/chinese-americans/newspapers)).

## 5. Religion and spiritual life

No Minnesota source gives the religious makeup of Chinese Minnesotans, including how many follow no religion. What follows is what congregations and organizations show about themselves.

**Christian congregations.** Chinese Christianity in Minnesota is as old as the community. Westminster Presbyterian Church in Minneapolis began English lessons for Chinese men in 1882, and its Chinese Sunday School was a central institution for early immigrants; Liang May Seen and Minnie Wong taught its first English classes for women ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/liang-may-seen-c1871-1946)). Walter James and Stanley Chong later partnered with Westminster on language classes run from the Nankin's clubrooms ([CAAM, Walter James](https://caam.org/walter-james/)).

Today the largest Chinese congregation is the **Twin City Chinese Christian Church** at 1752 Terrace Drive in Roseville, a non-denominational church that holds Mandarin, Cantonese and English worship each Sunday at 9:30 a.m. ([Twin City Chinese Christian Church](https://tcccc.org/en/)). A May 2026 job posting puts attendance at about 500 across the three congregations, about 150 of them in the Mandarin congregation ([Twin City Chinese Christian Church posting, 2026](https://swbts.edu/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/Twin-City-Chinese-Christian-Church.pdf)). In Rochester, the **Rochester Chinese Christian (Baptist) Church** says it has served since 1997 ([RCCC](https://www.rcccmn.org/)). A 2006 Post-Bulletin story traced Rochester's Chinese Christian fellowship to two couples in 1974, mostly graduate students, and said Mandarin worship began because elderly parents with limited English had come to live with their children; members came from China, Taiwan, Hong Kong and Malaysia ([Post-Bulletin, Feb. 17, 2006](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/autumn-ridge-church-tab-chinese-church)).

**Buddhism, folk practice and others.** No Chinese Buddhist temple in Minnesota was found, and no Minnesota source describes home ancestor altars, Daoist practice or other Chinese folk religion here. A Mandarin-language Catholic Mass in the Twin Cities was not found either. That does not mean these practices are absent; it means no Minnesota source found documents them.

**Remembering the dead.** Lakewood Cemetery in Minneapolis has a Chinese Community Memorial in Section 39, erected around the mid-1900s, which it describes as the resting place of many members of the Chinese American community ([Lakewood Cemetery, 2021](https://www.lakewoodcemetery.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Desktop-Lakewood-Community-Burial-Cemetery-Stroll-8.12.21.pdf)). Walter James, founder of the Nankin, is buried at Lakewood ([CAAM, Walter James](https://caam.org/walter-james/)).

## 6. Celebrations and observances

**Lunar New Year (Spring Festival).** In 2026 the Year of the Horse began on Tuesday, February 17 ([Minnesota Monthly, Feb. 10, 2026](https://www.minnesotamonthly.com/general/celebrate-lunar-new-year-across-minnesota/); [GovHK](https://www.gov.hk/en/about/abouthk/holiday/2026.htm)). Public celebration in Minnesota stretches over several weeks:
- CAAM's Chinese Dance Theater stages an annual production at the O'Shaughnessy at St. Catherine University; in 2026 it was "The Tale of the Peacock," January 17 and 18 ([CAAM, Dec. 15, 2025](https://caam.org/caamcdts-2026-lunar-new-year-production-show/)).
- The Chinese Community Center held what Mpls.St.Paul Magazine called "one of the largest Lunar New Year celebrations in the state" at Asia Mall in Eden Prairie on February 15, 2026 ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, Feb. 10, 2026](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/where-to-celebrate-lunar-new-year-around-the-twin-cities/)).
- The Chinese Heritage Foundation partnered with Luce Line Brewing in Plymouth on February 21, 2026 ([Meet Minneapolis](https://www.minneapolis.org/calendar/holidays/lunar-new-year/)).
- The fifth annual Twin Cities Dumpling Feast, hosted by the Association of Sino-American Neocultural Exchange and the Minnesota China Friendship Garden Society, was held at Peking Garden in St. Paul on February 25, 2026 ([Minnesota Monthly](https://www.minnesotamonthly.com/general/celebrate-lunar-new-year-across-minnesota/)).
- The Minnesota Orchestra's Lunar New Year concert on February 26, 2026, included a pre-concert set curated by Gao Hong with the Carleton College Chinese Music Ensemble ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/where-to-celebrate-lunar-new-year-around-the-twin-cities/)).
- Mia opened "Year of the Horse: Hoofbeats through Time," on the horse in Chinese art, running February 16 to August 30, 2026 ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/where-to-celebrate-lunar-new-year-around-the-twin-cities/)).

The 2026 season was shadowed by federal immigration enforcement. Mpls.St.Paul Magazine reported that Midtown Global Market and Theater Mu postponed or canceled their celebrations "in light of the surge of ICE agents in Minneapolis" ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, Feb. 10, 2026](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/where-to-celebrate-lunar-new-year-around-the-twin-cities/)). Minnesota Monthly notes that the holiday is also marked by Vietnamese, Korean, Hmong, Taiwanese and other Asian communities in Minnesota ([Minnesota Monthly](https://www.minnesotamonthly.com/general/celebrate-lunar-new-year-across-minnesota/)).

**Mid-Autumn (Moon) Festival.** The Minnesota China Friendship Garden Society and the Association of Sino-American Neocultural Exchange have held a Mid-Autumn Moon Festival at Liu Ming Yuan, St. Paul's China Friendship Garden in Phalen Regional Park. The society announced that there would be no festival there in 2026 and to "watch for details in 2027" ([Minnesota China Friendship Garden Society](https://mnchinagarden.org/upcoming-events/)). The Chinese Community Center posted a call for 2026 Mid-Autumn volunteers on September 8, 2026, though event details were not on the page ([Chinese Community Center](https://cccminnesota.org/programs/)). By the lunar calendar the festival fell on September 25 in 2026; Hong Kong's government listed the following day, September 26, as its public holiday ([GovHK](https://www.gov.hk/en/about/abouthk/holiday/2026.htm)).

**Dragon Boat Festival (Duanwu).** No Chinese-organized Duanwu observance in Minnesota was found. St. Paul's Dragon Festival at Lake Phalen, founded in 2000 to bring together the region's Asian immigrant communities and known for dragon boat races, returned on July 13, 2024, after a four-year pause blamed on board turnover, money problems and the anti-Asian climate of the pandemic ([Sahan Journal, July 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/dragon-fest-returns-lake-phalen-saint-paul/)). It is pan-Asian and held in July, not on the lunar date. No 2025 or 2026 date was confirmed.

**Qingming (Tomb-Sweeping Day).** No Minnesota public Qingming observance was found. In 2026 it fell on Sunday, April 5; Hong Kong's government observed it the next day ([GovHK](https://www.gov.hk/en/about/abouthk/holiday/2026.htm)). Lakewood Cemetery's Chinese Community Memorial (section 5) is one Minnesota place tied to the community's dead, but no Qingming gathering there was documented.

**Community anniversaries.** CAAM's annual celebration on May 9, 2026, combined its scholarship awards with a volunteer banquet and marked 59 years of the organization ([CAAM, July 1, 2026](https://caam.org/https-caam-org-legacy-of-honor-cultural-synergy-and-youth-empowerment-caams-2025-2026-grand-gala-concludes-with-historic-success-2/)). The garden society plans its third St. Paul Peony Festival for May 2027 ([Minnesota China Friendship Garden Society](https://mnchinagarden.org/upcoming-events/)).

## 7. Family, home and daily life

**Households.** Compass counts 16,242 Chinese Minnesotan households with an average size of 2.56 people. Married couples make up just over half: 26.7 percent without children under 18 and 24.2 percent with them. Another 24.2 percent are people living alone, and 9.6 percent are unmarried couples. Single mothers with children head 3.2 percent ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/chinese)). In the state chartbook, about 600 Chinese children, give or take 500, live below the poverty line out of about 5,700 ([Economic Status of Minnesotans 2026](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)).

**Generations under one roof.** First-person accounts describe family-run restaurants as multigenerational homes and workplaces. In Shakopee, three generations worked at the Dragon Cafe, which the parents bought eight years after arriving and later sold to an older daughter ([Shakopee Heritage Society](https://www.shakopeeheritage.org/historic-articles/people/yu-heng/)). In Rochester, aging parents who moved here to live with their adult children drove the need for Mandarin worship ([Post-Bulletin, 2006](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/autumn-ridge-church-tab-chinese-church)).

**Food.** Lunar New Year guides describe dishes chosen for their meaning: dumplings shaped like ingots for wealth, long noodles for long life, whole fish for abundance, and turnip and rice cakes ([Meet Minneapolis, 2026](https://www.minneapolis.org/calendar/holidays/lunar-new-year/)). The Twin Cities Dumpling Feast includes hands-on dumpling wrapping ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, 2026](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/where-to-celebrate-lunar-new-year-around-the-twin-cities/)).

**Adoptees and their families.** Grace Shu Gerloff, adopted from China by a Minnesota couple in 1997 and raised in Linden Hills, told MPR News that many adoptees "have felt kind of forgotten" after China closed its program, and worried about access to birth records ([MPR News, Sept. 16, 2024](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2024/09/12/questions-linger-after-china-ends-international-adoptions)). The University of Minnesota's Immigration History Research Center keeps adoptee stories, including one from a woman born in Wuhan who came to Macalester College ([IHRC](https://cla.umn.edu/ihrc/immigrant-stories/story-collections/transnational-adoption)).

**Aging.** Compass shows 9.5 percent of Chinese Minnesotans are 65 or older, half the state share of 17.3 percent ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/chinese); [all Minnesotans](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/minnesota)). The Chinese Social Service Center's senior program meets every other month, with seminars on health, immigration and social services ([United Way 211](https://search.211unitedway.org/search/eaecc1f2-c03c-5fc8-8d06-0e1147d36001)). New oral histories from Chinese Americans for Social Justice, a South St. Paul group, record adjustment to new jobs, cold winters and learning English ([Minnesota Digital Library, June 2026](https://minitex.umn.edu/news/minnesota-digital-library/2026-06/new-contributor-minnesota-digital-library-chinese-americans)).

No Minnesota source was found on marriage customs, dress or childhood practices specific to Chinese Minnesotans.

## 8. Arts, media, sports and culture

**Dance and music.** CAAM's Chinese Dance Theater, founded in 1992, celebrated its 30th anniversary in 2022 to 2023 and stages an annual Lunar New Year production ([CAAM, History](https://caam.org/history/); [CAAM, 2025](https://caam.org/caamcdts-2026-lunar-new-year-production-show/)). CAAM also runs a Heritage Music Project ([CAAM](https://caam.org/language-school/)). Gao Hong, a pipa player who founded and directs Carleton College's Chinese Music Ensemble, was honored when St. Paul Mayor Melvin Carter proclaimed April 3, 2022, "Gao Hong Day" ([Carleton College, April 5, 2022](https://www.carleton.edu/news/stories/gao-hong-celebrates-50-years-of-performing/)).

**Festivals and gardens.** CAAM lists its Festival of Nations participation from 1977 to 2019 and in 2026 recruited volunteers for a Chinese Cafe at IFest-MN ([CAAM](https://caam.org/history/); [CAAM, March 24, 2026](https://caam.org/language-school/)). Liu Ming Yuan, the St. Paul–Changsha China Friendship Garden at Phalen Regional Park, calls itself the first sister-city China friendship garden in Minnesota; it includes Hmong design elements, released a virtual story-map tour in 2026, and has given docent tours to more than 500 people in 2025 and 2026 ([Minnesota China Friendship Garden Society](https://mnchinagarden.org/)).

**Museums and history.** Mia's 2026 exhibition on the horse in Chinese art ran from February to August ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, 2026](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/where-to-celebrate-lunar-new-year-around-the-twin-cities/)). The Minnesota Digital Library added the Chinese Americans for Social Justice oral histories in 2026 ([Minnesota Digital Library](https://mndigital.org/about/contributing-organizations/chinese-americans-social-justice-0)).

**Media.** No current Chinese-language newspaper, radio or TV outlet based in Minnesota was confirmed. Organizations reach people through their own sites and, in the Chinese Community Center's case, WeChat ([Chinese Community Center](https://cccminnesota.org/)).

**Sports.** No Minnesota source was found on Chinese Minnesotan athletes or sports clubs.

## 9. Civic and political life

**Organizations.** CAAM calls itself the state's oldest and largest Chinese American organization, tracing its roots to Chinese American Club gatherings at the Nankin in the 1930s; it was founded in 1951 and incorporated in 1967 ([CAAM, History](https://caam.org/history/); [MNHS Library](https://libguides.mnhs.org/chinese-americans/more)). Its own pages give slightly different founding stories, naming both Di Chen and Stanley Chong as first president ([CAAM, History](https://caam.org/history/)). Ping Wang has led it since 2019 ([CAAM, History](https://caam.org/history/)). Each November it serves a free pre-Thanksgiving meal at Dorothy Day Place in St. Paul, nearly 300 guests in 2025, a tradition of more than 30 years ([CAAM, Dec. 11, 2025](https://caam.org/2025-pre-thanksgiving-free-lunch/)). Newer groups include the Chinese Community Center in Eden Prairie, which lists public policy, government relations and connecting Chinese and Asian American residents to public resources among its work ([Chinese Community Center](https://cccminnesota.org/about/)); Chinese Americans for Social Justice, which focuses on civil rights and fighting anti-Asian hate ([Minnesota Digital Library](https://mndigital.org/about/contributing-organizations/chinese-americans-social-justice-0)); the Chinese Heritage Foundation; the Association of Sino-American Neocultural Exchange; and the Minnesota China Friendship Garden Society.

**Public office.** Lydia Lee was described in 2005 as the first Chinese American elected to the Minneapolis School Board ([TPT Almanac, Jan. 14, 2005](https://www.pbs.org/video/new-school-board-members-6785/)). Tony Leung was the state's first Asian Pacific American judge, appointed by Gov. Arne Carlson in 1994, and its first Asian Pacific American federal judge, in 2011 ([Federal Bar Association, 2025](https://www.fedbar.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Leung-profile_Summer25.pdf)). No Chinese American member of the current Minnesota Legislature or of a large city council was confirmed.

**Hate and safety.** In March 2020 the state's human rights commissioner reported Asian Minnesotans being yelled at in public and refused service, and a Minnesota Department of Health coordinator said the state COVID hotline received so many calls blaming Chinese Americans and other Asian Americans that staff needed guidance to respond ([MPR News, March 25, 2020](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2020/03/25/asian-americans-in-minnesota-confront-a-covid19-backlash)). Hundreds gathered at the State Capitol for a "Stop Asian Hate" rally on March 28, 2021 ([MPR News, March 28, 2021](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2021/03/28/photos-hundreds-gather-for-stop-asian-hate-rally-in-st-paul)). Asian Media Access said in January 2026 that Asian Minnesotans had faced harassment and profiling from 2020 through 2025, from pandemic scapegoating to deportation actions ([Asian Media Access, Jan. 24, 2026](https://ww1.amamedia.org/2026/01/24/)).

**Debates.** Concern about China in U.S. politics touches Chinese Minnesotans directly. In 2024 a Minnesota congressman co-sponsored a federal bill to bar farmland purchases by people tied to the Chinese government; MinnPost reported that the U.S. Department of Agriculture says no Chinese entity or individual owns farmland in Minnesota ([MinnPost, April 2024](https://www.minnpost.com/national/2024/04/are-chinese-companies-really-trying-to-buy-minnesota-farmland/)). CAAM's own history records an older internal divide between merchants and professionals ([CAAM, History](https://caam.org/history/)). No Minnesota source found describes community debate over politics in China, Taiwan or Hong Kong.

## 10. Education

**Attainment.** Among Chinese Minnesotans 25 and older, 85.9 percent finished high school and 61.2 percent hold a bachelor's degree or more, while 14.1 percent did not finish high school ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/chinese)). Among adults 25 to 64, the state chartbook puts the bachelor's share at 66 percent, against 27 percent for Hmong Minnesotans ([Economic Status of Minnesotans 2026](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). Both high attainment and a real group without a diploma are part of the picture.

**Immersion and heritage schools.** Yinghua Academy in Minneapolis opened in 2006 as the first public charter Chinese immersion school in the United States; it reports 872 students, 59 percent of them students of color ([Yinghua Academy](https://www.yinghuaacademy.org/)). Public district programs include Jie Ming Mandarin Immersion Academy in St. Paul ([SPPS](https://jieming.spps.org/)) and Hopkins' Chinese immersion at Eisenhower Elementary ([Hopkins Public Schools](https://eisenhower.hopkinsschools.org/immersion/chinese-immersion)). Weekend heritage schools include the Twin Cities Chinese Language School, which CAAM says began in January 1975 with nearly 200 students ([CAAM](https://caam.org/language-school/)) and which gives its own founding year as 1976. It describes itself as the only Twin Cities school teaching traditional (正體) characters, teaches both Zhuyin and Pinyin, hosts a Taiwan Center for Mandarin Learning, and holds Moon Festival and Lunar New Year celebrations ([Twin Cities Chinese Language School](https://twincitiescls.org/)). Others include the Minnesota International Chinese School ([MICS](https://www.mnchinese.org/)) and Minnesota Yucai Chinese School's Saturday program ([Yucai](https://www.yucaimn.org/)), among others listed by the friendship garden society ([Minnesota China Friendship Garden Society](https://mnchinagarden.org/connections/)). CAAM awarded 11 scholarships of $2,000 to $5,000 to Chinese American high school seniors in 2026 ([CAAM, July 1, 2026](https://caam.org/https-caam-org-legacy-of-honor-cultural-synergy-and-youth-empowerment-caams-2025-2026-grand-gala-concludes-with-historic-success-2/)).

**College students.** China was the second-leading place of origin, about 18 percent, among Minnesota's 15,560 international students in 2024 to 2025, after India ([Open Doors, 2025](https://opendoorsdata.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/11/OpenDoors_FactSheet_Minnesota_-2025.pdf)). The University of Minnesota enrolled 5,617 international students in fall 2025 but its public summary does not break them down by country ([UMN Global Snapshot](https://mglobal.umn.edu/global-snapshot)). Its Chinese Students' Club dates to 1915, and its Chinese Students and Scholars Association, formed in 1984, was among the largest student groups on campus by 2013 ([UMN China Center](https://chinacenter.umn.edu/umn-china/history/students)). The university opened a Confucius Institute in 2008 ([MPR News, Sept. 18, 2008](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2008/09/18/confucius)).

## 11. Health and wellbeing, broadly

**Coverage.** Only 2.5 percent of Chinese Minnesotans lack health insurance ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/chinese)).

**Community services.** The Chinese Social Service Center in Minneapolis describes itself as supporting the cultural and social adjustment of Chinese Americans, citizens and noncitizens. Its services include health counseling and education, translation, help with Medical Assistance, MinnesotaCare and Medicare forms, rides to health appointments, and a seniors' program with health seminars ([United Way 211, updated Nov. 20, 2025](https://search.211unitedway.org/search/eaecc1f2-c03c-5fc8-8d06-0e1147d36001)). The Chinese Community Center lists health services and a community wellness clinic among its programs ([Chinese Community Center](https://cccminnesota.org/)).

**Language as a health issue.** With 29.9 percent of Chinese Minnesotans 5 and older reporting limited English, and with Mandarin and Cantonese both in use (section 1), interpreter matching matters. No Minnesota source found reports how often Chinese patients get an interpreter in the right language.

**What was not found.** No Minnesota clinic or provider group specifically serving Chinese patients was confirmed, and no Minnesota source was found on traditional Chinese medicine use, mental health, or chronic disease among Chinese Minnesotans.

## 12. Disability

**What the numbers show.** Compass reports 7.7 percent of Chinese Minnesotans have a disability (give or take 1.9 points) and 4.4 percent of those under 65, compared with 11.7 percent and 8.3 percent of all Minnesotans ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/chinese); [all Minnesotans](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/minnesota)). The state chartbook counts about 900 Chinese Minnesotans 18 to 64 with a disability, give or take 400 ([Economic Status of Minnesotans 2026](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)).

**Questions the numbers raise, not answers.** Why is the reported rate lower? Possible factors, none established for Minnesota by any source found, include a younger age profile (only 9.5 percent are 65 or older), the large share of recent student and skilled-worker arrivals, differences in how people answer survey questions about difficulty seeing, hearing, remembering or living independently, and reluctance to report. The survey asks about functional difficulty, not diagnosis, so it does not show who has an autism, intellectual disability or mental health diagnosis.

**Adoptees.** Thousands of Minnesotans were adopted as children from China ([Minnesota Compass, 2024](https://www.mncompass.org/data-insights/articles/importance-data-disaggregation-minnesotas-asian-communities)). No Minnesota source was found that counts how many have disabilities, describes what services they use, or explains how adoptive families and adult adoptees here think about disability.

**Services and language.** No Minnesota organization focused on Chinese families with disabled members was found, and no Minnesota Department of Human Services, Department of Health, University of Minnesota or Institute on Community Integration report breaking out Chinese Minnesotans was found. Who in these families names a condition a disability, whether a label opens doors to services or closes doors socially, and whether a Mandarin- or Cantonese-speaking parent can work through an English-only individualized education program are open questions here, without Minnesota evidence either way. The closest community channel found is the Chinese Social Service Center in Minneapolis. Its United Way 211 listing, last confirmed November 20, 2025, says it helps with Supplemental Security Income and Social Security Disability Insurance applications, offers health and disability-related counseling, arranges transportation to health and benefits appointments, and provides homemaking and personal care assistant (PCA) services, under contract with Medica, to older adults and people living with a disability ([United Way 211](https://search.211unitedway.org/search/eaecc1f2-c03c-5fc8-8d06-0e1147d36001)). The listing does not say how many Chinese families use these services.

## 13. What is changing now

- **Immigration enforcement, 2026.** In February 2026, Midtown Global Market and Theater Mu postponed or canceled Lunar New Year events because of a surge of ICE agents in Minneapolis, and other organizers framed their events around care for neighbors ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, Feb. 10, 2026](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/where-to-celebrate-lunar-new-year-around-the-twin-cities/)). In March 2025, a University of Minnesota graduate student's detention by ICE, off campus, raised fears among the university's more than 5,000 international students; the report did not give the student's nationality ([CBS Minnesota, March 31, 2025](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/concerns-among-university-of-minnesota-international-students/)).
- **The end of China adoption.** China ended international adoption in September 2024, leaving adoptees concerned about access to their records ([MPR News, Sept. 16, 2024](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2024/09/12/questions-linger-after-china-ends-international-adoptions)).
- **Suburban centers and new organizations.** Asia Mall (2022), Keefer Court's revival there (2024), the planned Asia Village in Blaine, and the Chinese Community Center's large Asia Mall New Year festival show commercial and civic life moving to the suburbs ([Sahan Journal, 2022](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/asia-mall-pan-asian-mall-eden-prairie-soft-opening-minnesota/); [Eden Prairie Local News, 2024](https://eplocalnews.org/2024/05/02/developers-of-popular-asia-mall-are-launching-a-second-asian-themed-complex-in-blaine/); [MPR News, 2024](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2024/03/15/popular-keefer-court-reopens-in-eden-prairies-asia-mall)).
- **Restaurant generations.** Longtime family restaurants close as owners retire, as David Fong's did in 2022, while newer places such as JUN, opened by a Shandong-born chef and her son who started Szechuan in Roseville in 2009, bring regional cooking ([FOX 9](https://www.fox9.com/news/popular-bloomington-restaurant-david-fongs-set-to-close); [North Loop Neighborhood Association](https://northloop.org/directory/business/jun/)).
- **Events in flux.** The Mid-Autumn Moon Festival at Phalen was not held in 2026, and the next Twin Cities Dumpling Feast is listed only as "coming in 2027" ([Minnesota China Friendship Garden Society](https://mnchinagarden.org/upcoming-events/)).
- **Recording history.** In 2026 the Minnesota Digital Library added oral histories from Chinese Americans for Social Justice ([Minitex, June 2026](https://minitex.umn.edu/news/minnesota-digital-library/2026-06/new-contributor-minnesota-digital-library-chinese-americans)).

## 14. Strengths and contributions

- **Long roots.** Chinese Minnesotans have been part of the state for 150 years, from Canton Cafe in 1883 to Liang May Seen's leadership among Chinese women in Minneapolis ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/liang-may-seen-c1871-1946)).
- **Building the cities.** Weiming Lu's work shaped Nicollet Mall, the skyways and Lowertown, where the redevelopment corporation he led drew $10 million from the McKnight Foundation and leveraged $750 million more and fought to save Union Depot ([Wakan Tipi Awanyankapi, Nov. 7, 2022](https://www.wakantipi.org/wta-blog/2022/11/4/in-memory-of-weiming-lu-7da2t-rljd3-476gt)).
- **Science and medicine.** University scientists, Honeywell and Univac researchers, and Mayo and IBM staff appear throughout this community's history ([CAAM](https://caam.org/history/); [Post-Bulletin](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/autumn-ridge-church-tab-chinese-church)).
- **Justice.** Judge Tony Leung opened the state and federal bench to Asian Pacific Americans in Minnesota ([Federal Bar Association](https://www.fedbar.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Leung-profile_Summer25.pdf)).
- **Language and schools.** A heritage school running since 1975 and the nation's first public charter Chinese immersion school ([CAAM](https://caam.org/language-school/); [Yinghua Academy](https://www.yinghuaacademy.org/)).
- **Service to neighbors.** CAAM's three decades of Thanksgiving meals for people experiencing homelessness, and Rainbow's free Family Meal during Lunar New Year 2026 ([CAAM](https://caam.org/2025-pre-thanksgiving-free-lunch/); [Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/where-to-celebrate-lunar-new-year-around-the-twin-cities/)).
- **Food culture.** From Leeann Chin's chain to dim sum, hot pot and hand-pulled noodles, Chinese cooks have shaped how Minnesota eats ([MPR News, 2010](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2010/03/12/leeann-chin)).
