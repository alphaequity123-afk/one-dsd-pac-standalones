# Colombians in Minnesota
Checked: October 31, 2026

## 1. People and place

**How many.** The Colombian community in Minnesota is small, and every count has a wide margin of error. Minnesota Compass, using the American Community Survey (ACS) for 2020 to 2024, counts 9,853 Colombian Minnesotans, give or take 1,784 ([Minnesota Compass, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/colombian)). Compass builds its cultural-community counts from a mix of self-reported race, ancestry, birthplace and parents' characteristics, so its number can include people who would not show up in a narrower question ([Minnesota Compass, cultural communities](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities)).

Other Census tables give different numbers because they ask different questions:

- People who named Colombian as their Hispanic origin: 10,494, give or take 2,420, in the single-year 2024 survey. For scale, the same table counts 388,435 Hispanic or Latino Minnesotans, 221,876 Mexican, 17,722 Ecuadorian and 50,771 South American in all ([ACS 2024, Table B03001, via Census Reporter](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B03001&geo_ids=04000US27)).
- The five-year 2020 to 2024 estimate for the same question is lower and steadier: 8,332, give or take 959 ([ACS 2020–2024, Tables B03001 and B05006](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B03001,B05006&geo_ids=04000US27,05000US27053,05000US27123,05000US27037,05000US27003,05000US27139,05000US27163,05000US27109,16000US2743000,16000US2758000,16000US2754880)).
- People living in Minnesota who were born in Colombia: 6,139, give or take 1,302, out of 523,532 foreign-born Minnesotans in 2024 ([ACS 2024, Table B05006](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B05006&geo_ids=04000US27)). The five-year figure is 4,774, give or take 666 ([ACS 2020–2024](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B03001,B05006&geo_ids=04000US27,05000US27053,05000US27123,05000US27037,05000US27003,05000US27139,05000US27163,05000US27109,16000US2743000,16000US2758000,16000US2754880)).

Taken together, a fair summary is "roughly 8,000 to 10,500 people, about half of them born in Colombia." In the same Census table, Ecuadorian Minnesotans outnumber Colombians by a wide margin, which matters because the two are often grouped together, including in restaurants (section 4). MNopedia's overview of Latino Minnesota, citing 2013 figures, lists Mexican, Puerto Rican, Ecuadorian, Salvadoran and Guatemalan Minnesotans as the largest groups and does not name Colombians ([MinnPost / MNopedia, Sept. 18, 2023](https://www.minnpost.com/mnopedia/2023/09/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-in-minnesota/)).

**Where.** Compass places 65.1 percent in the Twin Cities and 34.9 percent in Greater Minnesota, each give or take 7.2 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/colombian)). County and city estimates for 2020 to 2024 are small and uncertain, but they show the pattern ([ACS 2020–2024](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B03001,B05006&geo_ids=04000US27,05000US27053,05000US27123,05000US27037,05000US27003,05000US27139,05000US27163,05000US27109,16000US2743000,16000US2758000,16000US2754880)):

Hennepin County holds the largest share, and much of it lies outside Minneapolis. Olmsted County and Rochester stand out for a community this size. Local reporting also places Colombian Minnesotans in Northfield, Worthington and Faribault (sections 5, 6 and 8). There is no Colombian neighborhood. The places most associated with Colombian food are Northeast Minneapolis's Central Avenue, Uptown, Seward and East Franklin Avenue (section 4).

**How people came.** Minnesota's records show a Colombian official presence for at least seven decades. Secretary of State registration cards list a Colombian consul in Chicago in 1951 and a consul named Fernando Torres Restrepo in Minnesota in 1960 ([Minnesota Secretary of State consul card, via Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov//archive/sos/cards/pdf/foreign_consul_registrations_0001713246.pdf)). A 1978 letter from the Colombian consulate in St. Paul told the state that its consulate in Rochester had moved to St. Paul, with jurisdiction over Minnesota, North Dakota, South Dakota, Iowa and Wisconsin, while an honorary consulate in Minneapolis continued ([letter filed Dec. 14, 1978](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/archive/sos/film/pdf/31876.pdf)). By 2024, the consulate in Chicago was the office organizing services for Colombians in Minneapolis ([Colombia.com, Oct. 31, 2024](https://www.colombia.com/colombianos/estados-unidos/mesa-nacional-de-la-sociedad-civil-para-las-migraciones-tendra-su-espacio-en-minnesota-491739)). No current honorary consul in Minnesota was found.

Several streams of arrival appear in the sources:

- **Physicians and scientists, from the 1950s.** Dr. Fernando Torres Restrepo, a neurologist, joined the University of Minnesota Medical School in 1956, became a full professor of neurology in 1964 and died in Minneapolis in January 2007 at 82, according to Colombia's National Academy of Medicine. He was the brother of Camilo Torres Restrepo, the priest who joined the ELN guerrillas and was killed in 1966 ([Academia Nacional de Medicina obituary](https://encolombia.com/medicina/revistas-medicas/academedicina/va-76/obituariodrtorres/)). The obituary does not mention the consular post held by a man of the same name. Dr. Raúl Eduardo Espinosa, born in Guaduas, Colombia, came to Mayo Clinic in Rochester as a neurology fellow in 1957, joined the staff in 1961 and retired in 1989 ([Mayo Clinic Alumni Association](https://alumniassociation.mayo.edu/obituaries/raul-eduardo-espinosa-m-d-n-61/)).
- **Children adopted from Colombia.** Children's Home, now Children's Home & Lutheran Social Service, says it has worked with children in Colombia for more than 40 years and still runs an open Colombia program ([CHLSS](https://chlss.org/adoption/international-adoption/colombia/)). Family accounts collected by American Public Media include a 1970s adoptee who became a family medicine resident in Minnesota ([American RadioWorks](https://americanradioworks.publicradio.org/features/adoption/view_story_128174.html)). Colombia Live's director told MPR News there is "a huge community of adopted children from Colombia" in Minnesota ([MPR News, Dec. 21, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/12/21/colombia-live-bringing-the-rhythm-and-culture-of-colombia-to-minnesota)). No count of Colombian adoptees in Minnesota was found.
- **Family, study and work.** Patricia Torres Ray, born in Pasto, moved to Minnesota in November 1987 ([Minnesota Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/legdb/fulldetail?id=15249)). Coffee entrepreneur Leo Carvajalino first came on summer work-travel visas and met his wife in Minnesota ([Plymouth Magazine, April 2023](https://plymouthmag.com/something-new-is-brewing/)). Author Anika Fajardo was born in Colombia and raised in Minnesota ([The Loft](https://loft.org/artists/anika-fajardo)).
- **Conflict and asylum.** MinnPost, describing Minnesota's immigrant history, noted that many Colombians came to the United States to escape civil war and drug conflict ([MinnPost, April 4, 2018](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans-greater-minnesota/2018/04/state-immigration-where-new-minnesotans-have-come-statehood/)). In November 2024 the Chicago consulate held a session on asylum in Minneapolis (section 13). In January 2024, Colombia was not among the five most common origins of pending asylum cases in Minnesota's immigration court ([Sahan Journal chart data, from Syracuse University TRAC](https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/3ZICm/4/dataset.csv)).

**Generations and languages.** Compass puts 48.8 percent of Colombian Minnesotans as foreign-born, give or take 7.7 points, so roughly half were born in the United States or elsewhere. Among those 5 and older, 56.5 percent speak only English at home, 43.5 percent speak another language and 18.6 percent (give or take 5.9) speak English less than "very well" ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/colombian)). Compass does not name the other language. Spanish is the obvious one, but Colombia also has Indigenous and Creole languages, and no Minnesota count of their speakers was found. The community is young: 26.5 percent are 17 or younger and 6.1 percent are 65 or older. Women outnumber men, 54.5 percent to 45.5 percent ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/colombian)).

## 2. All walks of life

Compass figures for 2020 to 2024 show a community spread across classes rather than clustered in one. Margins of error are wide, so read these as rough shapes ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/colombian)):

- **Income.** Median household income is $74,087, but the margin of error is ±$28,936. Of 3,605 households, 38.3 percent earn $100,000 or more, while 26.1 percent earn under $35,000.
- **Poverty.** 6.9 percent live below the poverty line, and another 17.8 percent live between 100 and 199 percent of it. That leaves 75.3 percent at twice the poverty line or more.
- **Education.** 54.8 percent of adults 25 and older hold a bachelor's degree or higher, including 24.2 percent with a graduate or professional degree. Another 16.0 percent stopped at a high school diploma or GED.
- **Housing.** 57.3 percent of households own their home. 30.1 percent of all households are cost-burdened. Among renters the figure is 41.4 percent, give or take 20.5 points. Median rent is $1,437.

**Wealth, professions and public life.** The record includes senior physicians and academics, like Dr. Torres Restrepo and Dr. Espinosa (section 1), and a state senator who served 16 years, Patricia Torres Ray (section 9). In sports, it includes Gersson Rosas, a native of Colombia, hired in 2019 to lead the Minnesota Timberwolves' basketball operations. MPR News reported that he would be the highest-ranking Latino in an NBA front office ([MPR News, May 4, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/05/04/talking-timberwolves-new-president-of-basketball-operations)). It includes an award-winning author who teaches in a graduate writing program, Anika Fajardo ([The Loft](https://loft.org/artists/anika-fajardo)).

**Business owners and working families.** Restaurant owners like María Hoyos, who arrived from Medellín in 1980 and has run her own breakfast places since 1993 ([Maria's Café](https://mariascafe.com/)), and Luis Patiño, a former paralegal who started a food truck before opening Cafe Racer Kitchen ([Heavy Table, Dec. 20, 2021](https://heavytable.com/a-conversation-in-the-cafe/)), built businesses over decades. A coffee farmer's son from Caldas sells his family's beans at Twin Cities farmers markets ([Norte Café](https://nortecafe.shop/)).

**Hardship.** About a quarter of households live on under $35,000 a year, and more than four in ten renters are cost-burdened ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/colombian)). In 2024 the Colombian consulate held a session in Minneapolis on asylum, which Colombia.com described as one of the most requested paths to staying in the United States and also one of the most often denied ([Colombia.com, Nov. 7, 2024](https://www.colombia.com/colombianos/estados-unidos/consulado-colombiano-de-chicago-dictara-en-minnesota-charla-sobre-asuntos-de-asilo-en-eeuu-492675)). No Minnesota-specific reporting on Colombian families in shelters, low-wage industries or detention was found. That is not evidence that such hardship is absent.

## 3. Work and the economy

Among Colombian Minnesotans ages 18 to 64, 82.4 percent are employed: 87.6 percent of men and 78.1 percent of women. Of 5,759 workers, 80.3 percent drive to work. Most commute under 30 minutes ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/colombian)). Compass does not publish industry or occupation for this community, and no Minnesota source was found that does.

The sources point to a few areas of work:

- **Medicine and research.** Colombian-born physicians at the University of Minnesota and Mayo Clinic reach back to the 1950s (section 1).
- **Public service and human services.** Patricia Torres Ray listed her occupation, when first elected, as a consultant in social services at the Minnesota Department of Human Services ([Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/legdb/fulldetail?id=15249)). After leaving the Senate she opened PTR Associates, a public policy firm ([Carleton College, Oct. 2, 2024](https://www.carleton.edu/news/stories/senator-patricia-torres-ray-to-deliver-convocation-on-immigration-as-a-statement-of-conscience/)).
- **Food and coffee.** Restaurants, catering, a farm-to-cup coffee brand, a Colombian coffee stand, and at least one Colombian hot sauce maker in recent years (section 4).
- **Craft and design.** Laura Merino-Franco designs filigree jewelry that artisans in Colombia make, and in May 2022 she opened an Uptown boutique for women artisans ([KSTP, 2022](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/latino-businesses-uplifting-entrepreneurs-in-minneapolis-community/)).
- **Professional sports.** Colombian athletes have worked in Minnesota on contracts with Minnesota United and the Twins (section 8).

No union, labor history or Colombian chamber of commerce in Minnesota was found.

## 4. Businesses and commercial life

This is a short list, and it is meant to be. A community of about 10,000 people supports only a handful of businesses that name themselves as Colombian. Several Colombian dishes appear on menus run by Ecuadorian or pan-Latin owners. Meet Minneapolis calls Central Avenue in Northeast Minneapolis "a Little Ecuador of sorts" ([Meet Minneapolis](https://www.minneapolis.org/food-drink/international/latin-american/)). The full list, with status, is in businesses.json.

**Restaurants.**
- **Maria's Café**, 1113 East Franklin Avenue in the Ancient Traders Market, Minneapolis. María Hoyos came from Medellín in 1980. She opened Maria's Breakfast Club in 1993 and later moved to this space at the invitation of the American Indian Business Development Corporation. The menu includes arepas, plantains, Colombian hot chocolate and weekend specials, with live music on weekends ([Maria's Café](https://mariascafe.com/)). Meet Minneapolis calls it "Minneapolis's favorite Colombian brunch" ([Meet Minneapolis](https://www.minneapolis.org/food-drink/international/latin-american/)).
- **Cafe Racer Kitchen**, 2929 East 25th Street, Seward neighborhood of Minneapolis. It serves Latin American food, including Colombian street dogs, and does catering. Its site lists hours Tuesday through Sunday ([Cafe Racer Kitchen](https://www.caferacermn.com/)). The truck became a restaurant in 2015, and its Colombian-born owner, Luis Patiño, has served free meals to the community ([Heavy Table, Dec. 20, 2021](https://heavytable.com/a-conversation-in-the-cafe/)).
- **Macondo**, 2943 Girard Avenue South, Uptown, Minneapolis. It serves parrillada colombiana, salchipapas, weekend bandeja paisa and sancocho, and becomes a dance spot on weekend nights ([Meet Minneapolis](https://www.minneapolis.org/food-drink/international/latin-american/); [Macondo](https://macondomn.com/)).
- **La Colonia**, Central Avenue, Northeast Minneapolis. It serves Colombian and Ecuadorian food ([Meet Minneapolis, Central Avenue district](https://minneapolis.org/cultural-districts/districts/central-avenue)). Minnesota United described it as family-owned when a Colombian player visited ([Minnesota United](https://www.mnufc.com/news/angelo-rodriguez-visits-northeast-restaurant-la-colonia)). Its own website did not load, so its current status could not be confirmed.

**Coffee and food products.**
- **Norte Café** is grown on the Carvajalino family's farm, Finca Paramitos, in Chinchiná, Caldas, and roasted in Buffalo, Minnesota. It is sold online and at Twin Cities farmers markets ([Norte Café](https://nortecafe.shop/)). The brand launched on Dec. 11, 2020 ([Plymouth Magazine](https://plymouthmag.com/something-new-is-brewing/)).
- **Mi Cartagena Café** is a Colombian coffee stand whose owner-barista, Kerlis, writes that she was born and raised in Cartagena. Its site lists daily hours and an ordering address on West Lake Drive ([Mi Cartagena Café](https://micartagenacafe.com/)). Its city could not be confirmed from its own pages.
- **Uncle Simon's Traditions** was a Minnesota hot-sauce company making Colombian picante, founded by a brother and sister from Barranquilla ([Heavy Table, July 17, 2017](https://heavytable.com/picantes-uncle-simons-traditions/)). Its current status could not be confirmed.

**Retail.** Primitiva Collective, an Uptown boutique, was opened in May 2022 by Laura Merino-Franco, who designs jewelry made by Colombian artisans ([KSTP, 2022](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/latino-businesses-uplifting-entrepreneurs-in-minneapolis-community/)). Its current status could not be confirmed.

**Gaps.** No Colombian-owned grocery, bakery, law firm, accounting firm, clinic, travel agency, money-transfer office or media outlet in Minnesota was confirmed. No Colombian chamber of commerce or business association was found. A Carleton College community page lists Los Andes, in Uptown, as serving Colombian and Ecuadorian food, but the page is undated and the restaurant's own site did not load ([Carleton College](https://www.carleton.edu/spanish/resources/community/)).

## 5. Religion and spiritual life

No Minnesota source surveys Colombian Minnesotans' religion, and no congregation that calls itself Colombian was found. What the sources show:

- **Catholic tradition in family life.** A Colombian-born Worthington resident told the Worthington Globe in 2014 that Colombia's Christmas customs are "deeply rooted in the Catholic faith." She described Día de las Velitas on Dec. 7 and the nine-night Novena de Aguinaldos, and said she sometimes felt lonely celebrating far from home ([The Globe, Dec. 21, 2014](https://www.dglobe.com/news/in-colombia-catholic-faith-runs-deep-at-christmas)).
- **A church as gathering place.** In 2016 an adoptee and her Colombian birth mother planned to share their story and cook a Colombian dinner at Nueva Jerusalem Church in Faribault ([FOX 9, Dec. 1, 2016](https://www.fox9.com/news/minnesota-woman-recently-reunited-with-birth-family-killed-in-crash)). This shows a church serving as a gathering place. It does not show the community's overall religious makeup.
- **Faith in public speech.** When he signed with Minnesota United in 2026, winger Mauricio González said he hoped to celebrate goals with fans, "God willing" ([MLSSoccer.com, January 2026](https://www.mlssoccer.com/news/minnesota-united-acquire-colombian-winger-mauricio-gonzalez)).

Spanish-language Catholic Masses, evangelical and Pentecostal congregations, Afro-Colombian spiritual practices, and people of no faith are all plausible parts of Colombian life in Minnesota. None was documented specifically for Colombians here.

## 6. Celebrations and observances

- **Independence Day, July 20.** This marks the 1810 revolt in Bogotá that began Colombia's independence process ([Calendario Colombia](https://www.calendario-colombia.com/dia/2026-07-20); [La FM](https://www.lafm.com.co/sociedad/festivos-colombia-julio-2026-fecha-origen-404388)). In Minnesota, a mother told MPR News she first saw the Colombian dance group now called Colombia Live at a Colombian Independence Day celebration in 2010 ([MPR News, Dec. 21, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/12/21/colombia-live-bringing-the-rhythm-and-culture-of-colombia-to-minnesota)). No announcement of a 2025 or 2026 Minnesota Independence Day event was found.
- **Battle of Boyacá, Aug. 7.** This national holiday marks the 1819 battle that secured New Granada's independence ([Calendario Colombia, 2026 holidays](https://www.calendario-colombia.com/festivos/)). No Minnesota observance was found.
- **Christmas season.** The season runs from Día de las Velitas (Dec. 7) through the Novena de Aguinaldos (Dec. 16 to 24) to Christmas ([Calendario Colombia, Dec. 7, 2026](https://www.calendario-colombia.com/dia/2026-12-07); [Dec. 16, 2026](https://www.calendario-colombia.com/dia/2026-12-16)). One Worthington resident described keeping these customs at home, with buñuelos ([The Globe, 2014](https://www.dglobe.com/news/in-colombia-catholic-faith-runs-deep-at-christmas)). Colombia Live staged a sold-out Christmas-season family show, *La Cucharita*, at the Wellstone Center in St. Paul on Dec. 9, 2023. Former Sen. Patricia Torres Ray, who attended, said the Colombian community had lacked a family show at Christmastime ([MPR News, Dec. 21, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/12/21/colombia-live-bringing-the-rhythm-and-culture-of-colombia-to-minnesota)).
- **Holy Week and Easter.** Holy Thursday and Good Friday are public holidays in Colombia ([Calendario Colombia](https://www.calendario-colombia.com/festivos/)). No Colombian Holy Week observance in Minnesota was documented.
- **Epiphany (Reyes Magos).** In Colombia the public holiday falls on the Monday after Jan. 6, which was Jan. 12 in 2026 ([Calendario Colombia](https://www.calendario-colombia.com/festivos/)). No Minnesota observance was found.
- **Hispanic Heritage Month, Sept. 15 to Oct. 15** ([hispanicheritagemonth.gov](https://hispanicheritagemonth.gov/)). Colombia Live performs at Latin-themed events across Minnesota, and the Minnesota State Fair lists its pop-up performances in its Arts A'Fair program ([Minnesota State Fair](https://www.mnstatefair.org/schedule/colombia-live)). The group has also danced at the Festival of Nations, the Minneapolis Aquatennial and Valleyfair's Latin Days ([MPR News](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/12/21/colombia-live-bringing-the-rhythm-and-culture-of-colombia-to-minnesota)).
- **World Cup 2026.** Colombia reached the round of 16 and lost to Switzerland on penalties ([Excélsior, July 21, 2026](https://www.excelsior.com.mx/deportes/minnesota-united-oficializa-salida-james-rodriguez-futuro-colombiano-aire)). No Minnesota Colombian watch party was documented.

## 7. Family, home and daily life

**Households.** Compass counts 3,605 Colombian households with an average size of 2.26. Of these, 21.9 percent are married couples without children under 18, 18.3 percent are married couples with children, 16.4 percent are unmarried couples and 32.8 percent are people living alone. The margins are 8 to 12 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/colombian)). Young adults are a large share of the community: 14.0 percent are 18 to 24 and 17.2 percent are 25 to 34. 

**Families across borders.** Norte Café's founder writes that his father and cousin run the family farm in Colombia while he roasts and sells in Minnesota ([Norte Café](https://nortecafe.shop/)). Colombia Live describes intergenerational dances in which children partner with adults, sometimes their own relatives. A mother told MPR News she kept Spanish at home and joined the group so her daughter could be "bilingual and bicultural" ([MPR News](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/12/21/colombia-live-bringing-the-rhythm-and-culture-of-colombia-to-minnesota)).

**Adoptive families.** Colombia Live's director describes "a huge community of adopted children from Colombia" in Minnesota ([MPR News](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/12/21/colombia-live-bringing-the-rhythm-and-culture-of-colombia-to-minnesota)). Some of these families were formed through Children's Home & Lutheran Social Service. Others came through a "Camp of Dreams" run by Friends of FANA, a Colombian orphanage, which brought older children to Minnesota to meet possible adoptive parents. Eleven children who attended in 2013 were adopted ([Better Care Network summary of Star Tribune, Dec. 3, 2014](https://bettercarenetwork.org/news-updates/news/from-colombia-to-minnesota-program-seeks-homes-for-older-orphans)). CHLSS now partners with Kidsave to host Colombian children ages about 10 to 14 for summer visits ([CHLSS](https://chlss.org/adoption/international-adoption/colombia/)). Adult adoptees seeking birth family must start the request themselves with Colombia's family welfare agency, ICBF, which holds all adoption files ([CHLSS post-adoption](https://chlss.org/colombia-post-adoption/)). In 2016, FOX 9 reported that a Minnesota woman adopted from Colombia found her birth mother in Bogotá through a Facebook group called "Adopted in Colombia" and a private investigator ([FOX 9, Dec. 1, 2016](https://www.fox9.com/news/minnesota-woman-recently-reunited-with-birth-family-killed-in-crash)). Anika Fajardo, raised in Minnesota apart from her Colombian father, has written about meeting him and a half-brother as an adult ([Las Musas Books, 2022](https://www.lasmusasbooks.com/blog/anika-fajardo-meet-me-halfway)).

**Food.** Menus and accounts mention arepas, huevos pericos, Colombian chorizo, plantains, yuca, bandeja paisa, sancocho, buñuelos and hot chocolate with arepa ([Maria's Café](https://mariascafe.com/); [Meet Minneapolis](https://www.minneapolis.org/food-drink/international/latin-american/); [The Globe](https://www.dglobe.com/news/in-colombia-catholic-faith-runs-deep-at-christmas)). Maria's Café's menu notes that "as good paisas" her family drinks hot chocolate at breakfast with arepa ([Maria's Café](https://mariascafe.com/)).

**Aging.** Only 6.1 percent are 65 or older, and Compass suppresses age detail above 65 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/colombian)). No source on Colombian elders in Minnesota was found.

## 8. Arts, media, sports and culture

**Dance.** Colombia Live is the community's best-documented cultural institution. It grew out of Fuego Latino, an adult Latin dance group, and became Grupo de Danzas Colombianas in 2007 under Martha Ordóñez Daniels, who was born in Barranquilla ([GiveMN](https://www.givemn.org/organization/Colombia-Live)). The State Fair dates the group to 2001 ([Minnesota State Fair](https://www.mnstatefair.org/schedule/colombia-live)). In September 2023 it took the name Colombia Live and became a nonprofit. It teaches four age groups, from preschoolers to adults, and MPR News reported that many of its young dancers are second-generation Colombian Americans or adoptees ([MPR News](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/12/21/colombia-live-bringing-the-rhythm-and-culture-of-colombia-to-minnesota)). Its 2023 production *La Cucharita* used 17 dances, 36 dancers and 180 costumes. As of October 2026, its GiveMN page carried a campaign for 2026 workshops with Colombian guest teachers ([GiveMN](https://www.givemn.org/organization/Colombia-Live)). Its own site listed no upcoming events ([Colombia Live](https://www.colombialivemn.com/)).

**Literature.** Anika Fajardo, born in Colombia, raised in Minnesota and living in Minneapolis, teaches in Augsburg University's MFA program. She was a finalist for the 2026 Minnesota Book Award and is an International Latino Book Award gold medalist ([The Loft](https://loft.org/artists/anika-fajardo)). Her middle-grade novel *Meet Me Halfway* draws on an Arhuaco story from Colombia's Caribbean coast ([Las Musas Books](https://www.lasmusasbooks.com/blog/anika-fajardo-meet-me-halfway)).

**Film and broadcasting.** Twin Cities PBS carries *Día a Día, 2020: One Day At a Time*, in which Colombian immigrants in Minnesota reflect on 2020 ([TPT / Minnesota PBS](https://video.tpt.org/show/dia-dia-2020-one-day-time/)). KYMN Radio in Northfield aired a Spanish-language interview series, funded by the Minnesota Humanities Center, that included a Colombian-born resident talking about moving there and visiting Colombia ([KYMN, Sept. 3, 2023](https://kymnradio.net/2023/09/03/entrevista-con-diana-camargo/)). No Colombian-run media outlet in Minnesota was found.

**Sports.**
- *Soccer.* In February 2026, Minnesota United signed James Rodríguez, Colombia's national team captain and the 2014 World Cup's top scorer ([MLSSoccer.com, Feb. 6, 2026](https://www.mlssoccer.com/news/minnesota-united-sign-colombian-superstar-james-rodriguez)). - *Baseball.* Gio Urshela, born in Cartagena, played the 2022 season for the Minnesota Twins and set career highs in hits and games ([MLB.com](https://www.mlb.com/player/gio-urshela-570482)).
- *Basketball.* Gersson Rosas led Timberwolves basketball operations from 2019 (section 2).

These athletes came on contracts. Their presence matters to fans, but it is not the same as a settled Colombian community.

## 9. Civic and political life

**Elected office.** Patricia Torres Ray, born in Pasto, Colombia, served in the Minnesota Senate from 2007 to 2022, first for District 62 and then District 63 in Minneapolis, as a DFLer. The Legislative Reference Library, citing the Star Tribune, calls her the first known Hispanic woman in the Minnesota Senate. She ran in the 2018 primary for the 5th Congressional District and did not seek re-election in 2022 ([Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/legdb/fulldetail?id=15249)). Carleton College adds that she ran for lieutenant governor with John Marty in 2010 and chaired the Education Policy, State and Local Government, and Parks and Trails Legacy committees, among other roles. In 2024 she spoke at Carleton on "Immigration as Statement of Conscience" ([Carleton College, Oct. 2, 2024](https://www.carleton.edu/news/stories/senator-patricia-torres-ray-to-deliver-convocation-on-immigration-as-a-statement-of-conscience/)). No other Colombian-born state legislator, mayor or city council member in Minnesota was found.

**Consular and transnational life.** On Nov. 9, 2024, the Colombian consulate in Chicago brought its Mesa Nacional de la Sociedad Civil para las Migraciones to 1915 Chicago Avenue in Minneapolis. The meeting was meant to gather Colombians' input on migration policy and included an asylum session led by Emperatriz Blanco ([Colombia.com, Oct. 31, 2024](https://www.colombia.com/colombianos/estados-unidos/mesa-nacional-de-la-sociedad-civil-para-las-migraciones-tendra-su-espacio-en-minnesota-491739); [Colombia.com, Nov. 7, 2024](https://www.colombia.com/colombianos/estados-unidos/consulado-colombiano-de-chicago-dictara-en-minnesota-charla-sobre-asuntos-de-asilo-en-eeuu-492675)). 

**Organizations.** Colombia Live is the only active Colombian-identified nonprofit confirmed in Minnesota. For broader Latino civic life, the Minnesota Council on Latino Affairs advises state government and reports launching MCLA Informa, a monthly Spanish-language Facebook Live series ([MCLA 2026 Legislative Session Report](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/other/261121.pdf)).

**Debates.** In 2021, Heavy Table reported that a food writer withdrew a recommendation of Cafe Racer after learning that a co-owner is a Minneapolis police sergeant. Owner Luis Patiño, a Colombian-born former paralegal, told the outlet he understood the question and had opened the restaurant to give people access to food ([Heavy Table, Dec. 20, 2021](https://heavytable.com/a-conversation-in-the-cafe/)). This is one documented local debate. It does not represent the community's views on policing, and no survey of Colombian Minnesotans' political views was found.

## 10. Education

Colombian Minnesotans 25 and older are highly educated as a group: 93.9 percent finished high school and 54.8 percent hold a bachelor's degree or more, give or take 8.4 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/colombian)). That average includes graduate-degree holders and people with a high school diploma or less. It also sits alongside 18.6 percent who speak English less than "very well." A degree does not tell you which language a parent needs for school meetings.

**Institutions and scholars.** The University of Minnesota has employed Colombian-born faculty since 1956, notably Dr. Torres Restrepo in neurology ([Academia Nacional de Medicina](https://encolombia.com/medicina/revistas-medicas/academedicina/va-76/obituariodrtorres/)). Mayo Clinic trained and employed Colombian physicians from 1957 ([Mayo Clinic Alumni Association](https://alumniassociation.mayo.edu/obituaries/raul-eduardo-espinosa-m-d-n-61/)). Patricia Torres Ray earned a University of Minnesota bachelor's degree in urban studies after studying law in Pasto ([Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/legdb/fulldetail?id=15249)), and a public affairs degree from the Humphrey School ([Carleton College](https://www.carleton.edu/news/stories/senator-patricia-torres-ray-to-deliver-convocation-on-immigration-as-a-statement-of-conscience/)). Anika Fajardo teaches at Augsburg University ([The Loft](https://loft.org/artists/anika-fajardo)).

**Heritage learning.** Colombia Live functions as a heritage school for children, including adoptees ([MPR News](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/12/21/colombia-live-bringing-the-rhythm-and-culture-of-colombia-to-minnesota)). No Colombian student association at a Minnesota college, no Colombian Saturday school and no count of Colombian international students in Minnesota was found.

## 11. Health and wellbeing, broadly

Compass estimates that 8.9 percent of Colombian Minnesotans lack health insurance, give or take 5.6 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/colombian)). With a margin that wide, the true rate could be anywhere from about 3 to 15 percent.

**Providers.** The documented link between Colombia and Minnesota medicine runs mostly through clinicians rather than clinics for the community: Colombian-born neurologists at the University of Minnesota and Mayo Clinic (section 1), and a Colombian adoptee who became a family medicine resident here ([American RadioWorks](https://americanradioworks.publicradio.org/features/adoption/view_story_128174.html)). Mayo Clinic's pages about its office in Colombia could not be opened. No clinic, therapist practice or community health worker program aimed specifically at Colombian Minnesotans was found.

**Wellbeing.** Sources describe Colombia Live as "a second family" to some dancers and a place where adoptees "feel they belong" ([MPR News](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/12/21/colombia-live-bringing-the-rhythm-and-culture-of-colombia-to-minnesota)). A Colombian-born woman in Worthington spoke of loneliness at holiday time ([The Globe](https://www.dglobe.com/news/in-colombia-catholic-faith-runs-deep-at-christmas)). No Minnesota research on Colombian mental health, traditional healing or conflict-related trauma was found, and no such rate should be assumed.

## 12. Disability

**Minnesota numbers.** Compass estimates that 5.4 percent of Colombian Minnesotans have a disability, give or take 2.3 points, and 4.9 percent of those under 65 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/colombian)). These are survey estimates of functional difficulty, not counts of diagnoses or program eligibility. The community is young, which may matter, but the margin is wide and the reasons for the level have not been studied.

**Questions the evidence raises:**

- *Who defines disability?* The ACS counts functional difficulty. Schools count eligibility for special education, and county programs count assessed need. No Minnesota source breaks any of these down for Colombians, so the community is folded into "Hispanic" or "Spanish-speaking" in state data.
- *What does a label open or close?* For families who came through international adoption, disability can be part of the adoption itself. One Minnesota family adopted a deaf infant from Colombia in 2004 through Lutheran Social Service as a "special-needs adoption," choosing him partly because the father is deaf ([American RadioWorks](https://americanradioworks.publicradio.org/features/adoption/view_story_128263.html)). CHLSS says Colombian children waiting for families include those "living with identified needs ranging from minor/correctable to lifelong" ([CHLSS](https://chlss.org/adoption/international-adoption/colombia/)). For these Minnesotans, a diagnosis made before adoption may follow them into school and health records here.
- *What produced an impairment?* No Minnesota source addresses this for Colombians. Nothing found ties any cause, including Colombia's armed conflict, to Colombian Minnesotans with disabilities, and no cause should be assumed.
- *Language and access.* With 18.6 percent of Colombian Minnesotans speaking English less than "very well," Spanish-language supports matter. In 2024, PACER Center and Family Voices of Minnesota, with a Minnesota Department of Human Services grant, began Spanish-language parent support groups for Latino families of children with autism and other disabilities ([PACER Center, July 17, 2024](https://www.pacer.org/press-release/new-pacer-center-project-will-support-latino-and-somali-families-with-children-on-the-autism-spectrum/)). The University of Minnesota's Institute on Community Integration works with Latina mothers of children with developmental disabilities as community connectors for early screening ([ICI, March 2024](https://ici.umn.edu/news/connecting-communities)). Neither program is specific to Colombians.
- *Background, not Minnesota evidence.* A 2022 study of parents in Colombia found they faced systemic challenges and limited services for children with autism ([Tejero Hughes, Magaña et al., 2022](https://neiudc.neiu.edu/sped-pub/10/)). It describes conditions in Colombia. It does not describe families in Minnesota.

No Colombian-focused disability organization, Colombian disability advocate or DHS report with Colombian-specific data was found in Minnesota.

## 13. What is changing now

- **Current size and spread (dated).** The current Compass profile, for 2020 to 2024, gives 9,853 people, 34.9 percent outside the Twin Cities and 48.8 percent foreign-born ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/colombian)). The 2024 single-year Hispanic-origin estimate of 10,494 is higher than the five-year 8,332, which may reflect recent arrivals but is within the margins of error ([ACS 2024](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B03001&geo_ids=04000US27)).
- **Enforcement climate.** Operation Metro Surge, a federal immigration enforcement operation in the Twin Cities, was a central issue in the 2026 legislative session, according to the Minnesota Council on Latino Affairs. The council reports estimates of about $600 million in lost business revenue and more than $240 million in lost wages in Minneapolis and St. Paul, from University of San Diego researchers. The City of Minneapolis later put its own losses near $700 million ([MCLA 2026 report](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/other/261121.pdf)). No reporting specific to Colombian Minnesotans during the surge was found. Nationally, Vanguardia, citing the Deportation Data Project, reported 7,157 ICE arrests of Colombians from January to July 2026, already 74 percent more than in all of 2024 ([Vanguardia, Sept. 27, 2026](https://www.vanguardia.com/mundo/2026/09/27/ice-arresto-7157-colombianos-en-tan-solo-siete-meses/)). That is national background, not a Minnesota count.
- **Consular outreach.** The Chicago consulate's November 2024 migration meeting and asylum session in Minneapolis shows that a Colombian government office treats Minnesota as a place worth visiting ([Colombia.com, Nov. 7, 2024](https://www.colombia.com/colombianos/estados-unidos/consulado-colombiano-de-chicago-dictara-en-minnesota-charla-sobre-asuntos-de-asilo-en-eeuu-492675)).
- **A brief star turn.** James Rodríguez's half-season with Minnesota United in 2026 put Colombia in local sports news, and the club's decision not to keep him drew coverage in Colombia and Mexico ([Excélsior](https://www.excelsior.com.mx/deportes/minnesota-united-oficializa-salida-james-rodriguez-futuro-colombiano-aire)).
- **Adoption continues, in a new form.** International adoption from Colombia to Minnesota continues, now focused on older children, sibling groups and children with identified needs, partly through summer hosting ([CHLSS](https://chlss.org/adoption/international-adoption/colombia/)).
- **Second generation.** Younger Colombian Minnesotans, including adoptees and children of mixed families, describe needing to seek out culture deliberately, through dance and family trips ([MPR News](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/12/21/colombia-live-bringing-the-rhythm-and-culture-of-colombia-to-minnesota); [Las Musas Books](https://www.lasmusasbooks.com/blog/anika-fajardo-meet-me-halfway)).

## 14. Strengths and contributions

- **Long roots in Minnesota medicine.** Colombian physicians have taught and practiced at the University of Minnesota and Mayo Clinic since the 1950s ([Academia Nacional de Medicina](https://encolombia.com/medicina/revistas-medicas/academedicina/va-76/obituariodrtorres/); [Mayo Clinic Alumni Association](https://alumniassociation.mayo.edu/obituaries/raul-eduardo-espinosa-m-d-n-61/)).
- **Public leadership.** A first-known Hispanic woman in the state Senate, who served 16 years and came from work at the Department of Human Services ([Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/legdb/fulldetail?id=15249)).
- **Small businesses with long staying power.** One owner has run breakfast restaurants since 1993 and was invited into the Ancient Traders Market on Franklin Avenue ([Maria's Café](https://mariascafe.com/)). Another restaurant grew out of a food truck and has served free meals ([Heavy Table](https://heavytable.com/a-conversation-in-the-cafe/)).
- **Culture-keeping.** A community dance school teaches Colombian regional dances across generations and gives adoptees a place to belong ([MPR News](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/12/21/colombia-live-bringing-the-rhythm-and-culture-of-colombia-to-minnesota)).
- **Literature.** Anika Fajardo's memoir and novels, honored in Minnesota and nationally, give Minnesota readers Colombian stories ([The Loft](https://loft.org/artists/anika-fajardo)).
- **Work and education.** Employment above 80 percent among working-age adults, and more than half of adults with a four-year degree ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/colombian)).

## What could not be established

- **No Minnesota survey or study of Colombian Minnesotans** was found: nothing on occupations, religion, political views, health, mental health or disability specific to this group. All rates above come from small ACS samples with wide margins.
- **Organizations.** No active Colombian community association, chamber of commerce, student association, media outlet or congregation was confirmed. A Colombian consulate flyer about a mobile consulate day in Minnesota could not be opened, so any partner group named there could not be checked.
- **Celebrations.** No 2025 or 2026 Minnesota Colombian Independence Day festival, Boyacá Day observance, Christmas novena, Holy Week event or World Cup watch party was found announced. No Minnesota polling site for Colombia's 2026 elections was confirmed. No Minnesota observance of Afro-Colombian or Indigenous days was found.
- **Businesses.** No Colombian-owned grocery, bakery, law or accounting firm, clinic, salon, insurance, tax or travel agency was confirmed. The status of La Colonia, Los Andes, Primitiva Collective, Uncle Simon's Traditions and Mi Cartagena Café could not be confirmed.
- **People.** No current honorary consul of Colombia in Minnesota was identified. The number of Colombian adoptees in Minnesota, and the size of any Afro-Colombian or Indigenous Colombian presence, could not be found.
- **Immigration.** No Minnesota-specific count of Colombian asylum cases, detentions or deportations was found, beyond the January 2024 note that Colombia was not among the top five origins of pending asylum cases.
- **Pages that could not be reached.** Sahan Journal (including a profile of Gersson Rosas and a story on a State Fair naturalization ceremony), Explore Minnesota, Mayo Clinic, KARE 11, the Colombian consulate's Minnesota flyer, the Minnesota Women's Press, MinnPost's archive, the websites of La Colonia and Los Andes, and a Carleton College history of Spanish-language parishes. The same facts were sought from other outlets where possible.
