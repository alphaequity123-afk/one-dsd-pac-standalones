# Ukrainians in Minnesota

## 1. People and place

**How many.** Minnesota Compass, using American Community Survey data for 2020 to 2024, counts 22,254 Ukrainian Minnesotans, give or take 3,082. It places 71.9 percent in the Twin Cities and 28.1 percent in Greater Minnesota, each give or take 6.4 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ukrainian)). The Census Bureau's 2024 one-year survey counts 19,849 Minnesotans who name Ukrainian ancestry, give or take 3,043 ([ACS 2024 1-year, table B04006, via Census Reporter](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B04006&geo_ids=04000US27)). The Ukrainian American Community Center, writing to the Legislature in June 2025, put the community at "approximately 25,000 residents across the state," and its website says "20,000+" ([UACC letter, June 26, 2025](https://www.house.mn.gov/comm/docs/DhB1hP9jLk2PakdiFQu2CA.pdf); [UACC home page](https://uaccmn.org/)). These are different measures and all are estimates. In 2022 Compass counted "around 16,000," so the number has grown since the full-scale invasion, though the margins of error are wide ([Minnesota Compass, March 9, 2022](https://www.mncompass.org/data-insights/articles/minnesotas-ukrainian-population)).

**Born in Ukraine.** People born in Ukraine are a smaller group inside this count. The 2024 one-year survey estimates 6,647 Minnesota residents born in Ukraine, give or take 2,021; the five-year estimate for 2020 to 2024 is 5,269, give or take 720 ([ACS 2024 1-year, table B05006](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B05006&geo_ids=04000US27); [ACS 2020–2024 5-year, table B05006](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B05006&geo_ids=04000US27)). Compass puts the foreign-born share of the Ukrainian community at 31.3 percent, give or take 4.9 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ukrainian)). So roughly two in three Ukrainian Minnesotans were born in the United States.

**Recent arrivals.** The Minnesota Department of Health tracks newcomers who resettle here first and are eligible for its refugee health screening, a group that includes refugees and humanitarian parolees. Its public data list 1,049 arrivals from Ukraine in 2022, 1,030 in 2023 and 739 in 2024, plus a few small counts the department hides for privacy. Ukraine was the top country of origin in that data for both 2022 and 2023 ([MDH refugee arrival data by country and county, data through 2024](https://www.health.mn.gov/communities/rih/stats/country.csv); [MDH refugee health statistics page, updated Nov. 7, 2025](https://www.health.mn.gov/communities/rih/stats/index.html)). Hennepin County received the most (367, 516 and 399 in those three years), followed by Anoka and Ramsey; the data group most other counties together. These figures leave out people who never connected with screening. MPR News reported in February 2023 that more than 2,700 humanitarian parole applications had been filed by people in Minnesota and that more than 900 Ukrainians had used federally funded services, and that the federal government does not notify states when Ukrainians arrive ([MPR News, Feb. 23, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/02/23/ukrainians-in-minnesota-pay-it-forward-one-year-after-fleeing-war)). NPR, citing the Minnesota Department of Human Services, reported in March 2025 that more than 2,000 Ukrainians had come to Minneapolis through Uniting for Ukraine ([NPR, March 28, 2025](https://www.npr.org/2025/03/28/nx-s1-5318049/as-protections-expire-ukrainians-war-uncertain-future-uniting-for-ukraine)). A CBS Minnesota story in March 2023 said "more than 15,000 Ukrainian refugees" were in Minnesota but did not give a source ([CBS Minnesota, March 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/ukrainian-community-thanks-minnesotans-for-a-year-of-support/)); no other source found supports a number that high.

**Where.** Northeast Minneapolis, especially the riverfront area once called Old St. Anthony (city wards 1 and 3), has been the center of the Twin Cities' Ukrainian community since the late 1800s ([MNopedia, last modified April 11, 2025](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis)). The anchors are still there: St. Constantine Ukrainian Catholic Church at 515 University Ave. NE, St. Michael's and St. George's Ukrainian Orthodox Church at 505 Fourth St. NE, the Ukrainian American Community Center at 301 Main St. NE, and Kramarczuk's at 215 E. Hennepin Ave. (sections 4 and 5). Many Ukrainian Minnesotans live elsewhere. Ukrainian Minnesotans named in news stories live in Edina, Brooklyn Park and Vadnais Heights, among other places, and a Ukrainian Orthodox parish meets in Arden Hills and a Ukrainian Baptist congregation in St. Louis Park ([NPR, March 2025](https://www.npr.org/2025/03/28/nx-s1-5318049/as-protections-expire-ukrainians-war-uncertain-future-uniting-for-ukraine); [KSTP topic page](https://kstp.com/tag/ukrainian-refugees/); [MinnPost via Twin Cities Business, March 3, 2022](https://tcbmag.com/how-northeast-minneapolis-became-a-center-for-ukrainian-americans/); [St. Louis Park Historical Society](https://slphistory.org/firstukrainian/)). On the Iron Range, Chisholm's Saints Peter and Paul church, built in 1916, was described in its 1979 National Register nomination as the religious and social center of the Range's Ukrainian community and "the only Ukrainian church on the Iron Range" ([National Register nomination, 1979](https://npgallery.nps.gov/NRHP/GetAsset/NRHP/80004340_text)). A Rusyn heritage society says its founders came from the Sanok and Lesko districts of Galicia, that it closed in 1976, and that the building now holds apartments ([Rusin Association of Minnesota, undated](https://rusinmn.org/pdf/topics-interest/churches/minnesota-wisconsin-rusyn-churches.pdf)). In 2022 MPR interviewed a Ukrainian-born chemistry professor teaching at the College of St. Scholastica and Lake Superior College in Duluth ([MPR News, Feb. 24, 2022](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2022/02/24/for-minnesotans-with-ties-to-ukraine-anger-and-anxiety-in-wake-of-russian-attack)), and MDH counted a handful of Ukrainian arrivals in Olmsted County (Rochester) in 2022 to 2024 ([MDH](https://www.health.mn.gov/communities/rih/stats/country.csv)). Beyond that, no source was found that maps where in Greater Minnesota Ukrainian families live today.

**How people came.** Arrival came in waves, each tied to a crisis.
- *Late 1800s to World War I.* MNopedia dates the Minneapolis community to the late 1870s, when men, mostly from western Ukraine, came for mill and labor jobs and settled where rents were low ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis); [MinnPost via TCB](https://tcbmag.com/how-northeast-minneapolis-became-a-center-for-ukrainian-americans/)). St. Constantine's own timeline counts about 50 Ukrainian families in the area in 1912 ([St. Constantine history](https://stconstantine.org/history.html)). Many came from Galicia, then in Austria-Hungary, and some families identified as Rusyn or Ruthenian rather than Ukrainian; the first local Eastern-rite parish, St. John the Baptist (1906), was Ruthenian ([St. Constantine history](https://stconstantine.org/history.html)).
- *After World War II.* University of Minnesota entomologist Alexander Granovsky visited displaced persons camps and recruited Ukrainians for Minnesota jobs; MNopedia says hundreds came, especially skilled workers such as scientists and carpenters. Filmmaker Zina Poletz Gutmanis told MinnPost that St. Paul's Brown and Bigelow sponsored his trips with lists of job openings from Minnesota companies ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis); [MinnPost via TCB](https://tcbmag.com/how-northeast-minneapolis-became-a-center-for-ukrainian-americans/)).
- *1990s and 2000s.* After 1991, political uncertainty brought more people ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis)). MDH's data show a steady stream of refugee arrivals from Ukraine from 1999 to 2007, often 50 to 100 a year, about 780 from 1999 through 2017 in all ([MDH arrival data](https://www.health.mn.gov/communities/rih/stats/country.csv)).
- *2014 to now.* Russia's seizure of Crimea and the war in the east in 2014 pushed some families to move earlier ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis); [MinnPost via TCB](https://tcbmag.com/how-northeast-minneapolis-became-a-center-for-ukrainian-americans/)). After February 24, 2022, most newcomers came on humanitarian parole, many through Uniting for Ukraine, which let U.S. sponsors bring Ukrainians for two years ([MDH Ukrainian clinical guidance](https://www.health.mn.gov/communities/rih/coe/clinical/ukrainian.html); [Ukraine Immigration Task Force, May 2026](https://ukrainetaskforce.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/U4U-Four-Year-Retrospective.pdf)). In 2022 the Minneapolis-based aid group Alight welcomed its first Ukrainian refugee family, from Donetsk, with local sponsors, and aimed to bring 99 more ([FOX 9, 2022](https://www.fox9.com/news/ukrainian-refugees-building-new-life-in-minnesota)). MPR News reported in 2023, after a correction, that the Twin Cities was the nation's top metro area for Ukrainian sponsorships through the Welcome.US program, according to the aid group Alight ([MPR News, Feb. 23, 2023, corrected March 1, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/02/23/ukrainians-in-minnesota-pay-it-forward-one-year-after-fleeing-war)).

**Generations and language.** Compass's age profile looks like Minnesota as a whole: 28.8 percent are 17 or younger and 15.2 percent are 65 or older ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ukrainian)). Among Ukrainian Minnesotans 5 and older, 70.0 percent speak only English at home, 30.0 percent speak another language, and 13.7 percent speak English less than "very well." The Census Bureau does not count Ukrainian alone; it groups "Ukrainian or other Slavic languages," which 7,180 Minnesotans 5 and older spoke at home in 2024, give or take 2,209, and 3,169 of them spoke English less than "very well" ([ACS 2024 1-year, table B16001](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B16001&geo_ids=04000US27)). Gutmanis, who grew up in Minneapolis's Ukrainian community during the Soviet era, told MinnPost her family spoke Ukrainian at home and sent her to Ukrainian Saturday school to "preserve that spark of Ukraine" ([MinnPost via TCB](https://tcbmag.com/how-northeast-minneapolis-became-a-center-for-ukrainian-americans/)). A newcomer working for the community center told MPR in 2023 that "most people do not speak English, especially elder generations" among the new arrivals ([MPR News, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/02/23/ukrainians-in-minnesota-pay-it-forward-one-year-after-fleeing-war)).

## 2. All walks of life

No single description fits. All figures below are from the Compass profile for 2020 to 2024, in 2024 dollars, unless noted ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ukrainian)).

- **Well-off households.** Median household income is $94,322, give or take $13,367. Nearly half of households (47.9 percent) take in $100,000 or more. Among adults 25 and older, 24.9 percent hold a graduate or professional degree and 53.2 percent hold at least a bachelor's degree. In 2022, Compass noted that Ukrainian Minnesotans' median income and degree rates were above the state average ([Compass, March 2022](https://www.mncompass.org/data-insights/articles/minnesotas-ukrainian-population)).
- **Business owners and professionals.** Family businesses have lasted for generations. Kramarczuk's, bought in 1954 by refugees Wasyl and Anna Kramarczuk and now owned by their son Orest, won a James Beard America's Classic award in 2013 ([James Beard Foundation](https://www.jamesbeard.org/stories/americas-classic-kramarczuks)). The Ukrainian Gift Shop, opened in a living room in 1947, calls itself the world's largest family-owned source of pysanky supplies ([Ukrainian Gift Shop](https://ukrainiangiftshop.com/pages/our-story)). Ukrainian-born prosthetist Yakov Gradinar co-founded the Protez Foundation in Minnesota in 2022 ([FOX 9, Dec. 2, 2022](https://www.fox9.com/news/ukrainian-soldiers-come-to-minnesota-for-prosthetics-and-then-return-to-the-fight)). Newcomers in the news include an IT worker and an aromatherapist from Kyiv who started a gluten-free bakery, and newcomers who now work in community outreach and refugee case management ([Heaven Gluten Free](https://heavenglutenfree.com/); [MPR News, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/02/23/ukrainians-in-minnesota-pay-it-forward-one-year-after-fleeing-war)).
- **Middle-income and working families.** About a quarter of households (26.6 percent) earn $50,000 to $99,999. Owner-occupied homes are 68.0 percent of households. Among adults 18 to 64, 82.5 percent are employed; 88.7 percent of men and 77.5 percent of women. Recent arrivals profiled by NPR worked as a cabinet maker, a FedEx driver, a retail worker and in restaurants, cafes and bars ([NPR, March 2025](https://www.npr.org/2025/03/28/nx-s1-5318049/as-protections-expire-ukrainians-war-uncertain-future-uniting-for-ukraine)).
- **Households under strain.** About 17.3 percent of households earn under $35,000. Overall, 7.7 percent of people live below the poverty line, give or take 2.9, and another 10.8 percent are just above it (100 to 149 percent of poverty). Among people 65 and older, 11.3 percent are in poverty. One in three households (33.5 percent) is cost-burdened by housing, rising to 54.5 percent of renter households, give or take 15.3. And 5.3 percent lack health insurance.
- **People whose status is in limbo.** Some newcomers have lost the right to work. NPR described a Ukrainian woman in Minneapolis whose legal status had expired and who could no longer legally work at her three jobs, living off savings while a renewal sat unprocessed ([NPR, March 2025](https://www.npr.org/2025/03/28/nx-s1-5318049/as-protections-expire-ukrainians-war-uncertain-future-uniting-for-ukraine)). In 2023 a community center worker told MPR, "We are feeling a lack of places for people to live" ([MPR News, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/02/23/ukrainians-in-minnesota-pay-it-forward-one-year-after-fleeing-war)). These stories are individual; no source was found that counts how many Ukrainian Minnesotans are in this position.

These figures blend fourth-generation Northeast Minneapolis families, 1990s refugees and 2022 arrivals. No source was found that breaks income or poverty out by when people came.

## 3. Work and the economy

**Labor history.** The first Ukrainian men in Minneapolis worked in the mills of Northeast and lived near them ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis)). On the Iron Range, Ukrainians were the last immigrant group to settle during the wave of immigration that ended in 1914, according to the 1979 National Register nomination for the Chisholm church ([NPS](https://npgallery.nps.gov/NRHP/GetAsset/NRHP/80004340_text)). MNopedia points readers to a 1988 Minnesota Historical Society oral history with Fred M. Fine, a second-generation Ukrainian Minnesotan active in socialist and labor organizing in the 1940s ([MNopedia, related resources](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis)). After World War II, recruits from displaced persons camps were chosen for skills; Gutmanis told MinnPost they included scientists, carpenters and an artist who went to work at Brown and Bigelow ([MinnPost via TCB](https://tcbmag.com/how-northeast-minneapolis-became-a-center-for-ukrainian-americans/)).

**Today.** Compass counts 10,640 Ukrainian Minnesotan workers 16 and older; 73.6 percent drive to work and 25.3 percent walk, bike or work at home ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ukrainian)). No Minnesota source was found that breaks Ukrainian workers out by industry or occupation.

**Newcomers and work.** The community center says finding stable work is "one of the most pressing challenges" for new arrivals. It runs a long-term care workforce program with English classes built around care vocabulary, vocational training, mentors and job placement, and names Fairview Health Services and Ebenezer Senior Living among the employers it works with ([UACC, Long-Term Care Workforce](https://uaccmn.org/services/long-term-care-workforce/)). The James Beard Foundation wrote that a job at Kramarczuk's "has long been a first step for new immigrants" ([James Beard Foundation](https://www.jamesbeard.org/stories/americas-classic-kramarczuks)). A community center worker told MPR it took her "several months" to get work eligibility ([MPR News, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/02/23/ukrainians-in-minnesota-pay-it-forward-one-year-after-fleeing-war)). Work permits for parolees and people with Temporary Protected Status have become uncertain (section 13).

**Entrepreneurship.** Long-standing businesses include Kramarczuk's and the Ukrainian Gift Shop. Dmytro Mandybur, from the same village as the Kramarczuks, founded a Ukrainian credit union, according to MNopedia ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis)); no current sign of it operating was found. New businesses include Heaven Gluten Free, founded in 2023 by Polina and Vlad Lahoda, who came from Kyiv in 2022 ([Heaven Gluten Free](https://heavenglutenfree.com/); [Lahoda fundraiser page](https://www.gofundme.com/f/save-heaven-gluten-free-bakery-from-shutting-its-doors)).

**Unions.** No source was found on Ukrainian Minnesotans' role in unions today.

## 4. Businesses and commercial life

Ukrainian commercial life in Minnesota is small and centered in Northeast Minneapolis. There is no Ukrainian shopping district, chamber of commerce or business directory that could be found. The list in businesses.json is short for that reason.

- **Kramarczuk's** (215 E. Hennepin Ave., Minneapolis): deli, bakery, restaurant and sausage maker. Founded when Wasyl and Anna Kramarczuk bought a butcher shop in 1954; at its current address since 1967. Orest Kramarczuk told KSTP his parents arrived in 1949 with "a wicker basket" of belongings ([James Beard Foundation](https://www.jamesbeard.org/stories/americas-classic-kramarczuks); [KSTP, Feb. 2022](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/standing-with-ukraine/)). Its site lists hours (Tuesday to Saturday, 10 to 8) and 2026 holiday closings, and its sausages are sold at Target Field ([Kramarczuk's](https://kramarczuks.com/about)). MNopedia credits Kramarczuk's, with the churches and the community center, for making Ukrainians one of the city's most visible European groups ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis)).
- **Ukrainian Gift Shop** (North Loop, Minneapolis): founded in 1947 by Marie Procai and her daughter Luba Perchyshyn; sells pysanky dyes, kits, books and embroidery, mostly by mail. It has no walk-in showroom; pickup can be arranged ([Ukrainian Gift Shop](https://ukrainiangiftshop.com/pages/our-story)).
- **Heaven Gluten Free** (Minneapolis): gluten-free sourdough bakery founded in 2023 by a couple from Kyiv; ships nationwide and sells through stores. Customer reviews on its site are dated as recently as October 2026, and a 2024 fundraiser said its breads were sold at Eastside Food Co-op in Northeast Minneapolis ([Heaven Gluten Free](https://heavenglutenfree.com/); [fundraiser](https://www.gofundme.com/f/save-heaven-gluten-free-bakery-from-shutting-its-doors); [CBS Minnesota, May 13, 2024](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/video/couple-that-fled-from-ukraine-opens-bakery-in-minnesota/)).
- **Ukrainian Village Band** (Twin Cities): a "zabava" (party) band founded in 2007, which played The Cedar Cultural Center on Aug. 20, 2025 and is on the Minnesota State Fair's schedule ([The Cedar](https://www.thecedar.org/events/summer-at-the-cedar-ukrainian-village-band); [Minnesota State Fair](https://www.mnstatefair.org/schedule/ukrainian-village-band)).
- **Community center sales.** The center's Lenten fish fry, held every Friday before Easter, draws lines down the block, NPR reported, with tips going to aid for Ukraine and to newcomers ([NPR, March 2025](https://www.npr.org/2025/03/28/nx-s1-5318049/as-protections-expire-ukrainians-war-uncertain-future-uniting-for-ukraine); [UACC](https://uaccmn.org/)). St. Constantine sells pyrohy (dumplings) ([St. Constantine](https://stconstantine.org/)).
- **Media.** No Ukrainian-language newspaper, radio show or magazine published in Minnesota could be found. Community news moves through the community center's site and social media pages and the "Ukrainians in Minnesota" Facebook community named by Ukraine's Holodomor Museum ([UACC](https://uaccmn.org/); [Holodomor Museum](https://holodomormuseum.org.ua/en/news-museji/minnesota-commemorated-the-holodomor-victims/)).

## 5. Religion and spiritual life

Ukrainian Minnesotans belong to several churches, and some to none. No source was found that counts how many attend any church.

**Ukrainian Greek Catholic.** St. Constantine Ukrainian Catholic Church, 515 University Ave. NE, Minneapolis, was founded in 1913. Its first Divine Liturgy was held on Jan. 1, 1913, in a rented hall, and Archbishop John Ireland blessed the cornerstone that August. The current Byzantine-style church was blessed in 1972. Its pastor since 2019 is Fr. Ivan Shkumbatyuk ([St. Constantine history](https://stconstantine.org/history.html)). Greek Catholics follow Eastern (Byzantine) worship but are in communion with Rome; the parish says any Catholic may meet the Sunday obligation at its liturgy, which it also streams ([St. Constantine](https://stconstantine.org/)). Its timeline notes that St. Constantine adopted the Gregorian calendar in 1943, so it keeps Christmas on Dec. 25 ([St. Constantine history](https://stconstantine.org/history.html)). The same timeline says that in 1925 "many left the church to join the Ukrainian Autocephalous Orthodox church," a split rooted in politics back home. It also records that a priest organized a St. Stephen parish in St. Paul in 1954; whether that parish still meets could not be confirmed.

**Ukrainian Orthodox.** St. Michael's Ukrainian Orthodox Church formed in 1925 and moved into 505 Fourth St. NE in 1926 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis)). Now St. Michael's and St. George's, it belongs to the Ukrainian Orthodox Church of the USA. Its website lists Divine Liturgy every Sunday at 10 a.m., names Fr. Myron Korostil as priest, and says longtime members, "some descended from our church's founders," worship alongside recent immigrants from Ukraine. Its 2025–26 notices, some in both Ukrainian and English, include beginner pysanka classes run by the parish Sisterhood, a Palm Sunday Pysanka Festival (March 29, 2026), a borscht cook-off, and a June 2025 multifaith prayer gathering for Ukrainian children taken to Russia ([St. Michael's and St. George's](https://www.stmichaelsandstgeorgesuoc.org/); [services](https://www.stmichaelsandstgeorgesuoc.org/services); [events](https://www.stmichaelsandstgeorgesuoc.org/events)). In 2016 the parish had a Ukrainian school for children, and the two Twin Cities Orthodox parishes sang as a joint choir ([UOC of the USA, Nov. 2016](https://uocofusa.org/news_161122_1)). St. Katherine Ukrainian Orthodox Church in Arden Hills began about 1951 as St. Volodymyr and Olha Ukrainian Orthodox Church; at its 60th anniversary in December 2011, services were held in both English and Ukrainian ([UOC of the USA, Dec. 2011](https://uocofusa.org/news_111219_2)). In May 2026 Archbishop Daniel marked the retirement of the Very Rev. Petro Siwko after 28 years as its pastor and installed Rev. Pavlo Vysotskyi ([UOC of the USA, May 2026](https://www.uocofusa.org/news_260511_1)). St. Katherine's own website could not be found, so its service times are not confirmed here.

**Calendar differences.** In 2023 the Orthodox Church of Ukraine moved to the Revised Julian calendar, which puts Christmas on Dec. 25, while letting parishes keep the old date; the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church's bishops voted for Dec. 25 while letting parishes change gradually; and Ukraine moved its official Christmas holiday to Dec. 25 ([Catholic Review/OSV News, 2023](https://catholicreview.org/ukraine-officially-moves-christmas-observance-to-dec-25-in-huge-cultural-shift/print/)). Which date each Minnesota Orthodox parish now uses for Christmas could not be confirmed from their own pages. In 2016, St. Michael's patronal feast fell in late November, matching the older Julian calendar ([UOC of the USA, 2016](https://uocofusa.org/news_161122_1)); in 2026, its website listed the St. Demetrius memorial Saturday on Oct. 24, a date that fits the newer calendar ([St. Michael's and St. George's](https://www.stmichaelsandstgeorgesuoc.org/services)). Families should be asked which dates they keep.

**Evangelical Protestant.** First Ukrainian Evangelical Baptist Church meets in the former Park Baptist Church building in St. Louis Park, where it moved from Minneapolis in 1996. The St. Louis Park Historical Society describes services in Ukrainian and a congregation about 90 percent Ukrainian, with Russian and Moldovan members ([St. Louis Park Historical Society, undated](https://slphistory.org/firstukrainian/)). Baptist Press reported in 2022 that it had about 150 members, belonged to the Minnesota-Wisconsin Baptist Convention, housed wounded soldiers in Minnesota for prosthetics, and sent money to churches in Ukraine its members came from ([Baptist Press via ChurchLeaders, Oct. 14, 2022](https://churchleaders.com/news/436226-minnesota-ukrainian-church-prays-over-injured-soldier-thanks-god-bp.html)). The community center's former advocacy page also lists a "Ukrainian Baptist Church" among the groups in the Minnesota Ukrainian American Advocacy Committee ([UACC, archived site](https://old.uaccmn.org/advocate-for-ukraine/)). No other Ukrainian Pentecostal or evangelical congregations in Minnesota could be confirmed.

**Jewish families from Ukraine.** Some Minnesotans born in Ukraine are Jewish. No Minnesota source was found that counts them or describes how they identify.

**Practice and change.** Parish life mixes worship and community: St. Constantine's calendar includes a Palm Sunday dinner, a blessing of vehicles and its own festival; St. Michael's held a Ukrainian school in 2016 ([St. Constantine](https://stconstantine.org/); [UOC of the USA, 2016](https://uocofusa.org/news_161122_1)).

**No faith.** No source was found that describes or counts Ukrainian Minnesotans who are not religious.

## 6. Celebrations and observances

- **Christmas (Rizdvo) and Christmas Eve (Sviat Vechir).** St. Constantine keeps Dec. 25 ([St. Constantine history](https://stconstantine.org/history.html)); some Orthodox families may keep Jan. 7. No 2026 Minnesota event listing was found.
- **Easter (Velykden) and pysanky.** Easter falls on the Western date for Greek Catholics using the Gregorian calendar and the Orthodox date for those on the Julian calendar; in 2026 these were April 5 and April 12. The community center lists a Ukrainian Pysanka Festival and Easter Bazaar, pysanky classes and a Lenten fish fry every Friday before Easter ([UACC About](https://uaccmn.org/about/); [NPR, March 2025](https://www.npr.org/2025/03/28/nx-s1-5318049/as-protections-expire-ukrainians-war-uncertain-future-uniting-for-ukraine)). Pysanky, wax-resist decorated eggs, are a Minneapolis specialty: Gutmanis told MinnPost that people in Ukraine, where the tradition faded in the Soviet era, learned designs from Minneapolis ([MinnPost via TCB](https://tcbmag.com/how-northeast-minneapolis-became-a-center-for-ukrainian-americans/)).
- **Vyshyvanka Day (third Thursday in May).** A day for wearing embroidered shirts. In 2022 Ukrainian Minnesotans gathered at Boom Island Park with the Ukrainian Village Band and arkan dancing ([MPR News, May 20, 2022](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2022/05/20/ukrainian-minnesotans-celebrate-international-vyshyvankas-day)).
- **Ivana Kupala (midsummer).** The Ukrainian Village Band organizes a summertime ethno-festival named for Ivana Kupala ([The Cedar](https://www.thecedar.org/events/summer-at-the-cedar-ukrainian-village-band)); no dated 2026 listing was found.
- **Independence Day (Aug. 24).** Marks the 1991 declaration of independence. In 2025 the Ukrainian Community of Minnesota held a celebration at Boom Island Park with music, food and traditional dress ([FOX 9, Aug. 24, 2025](https://www.fox9.com/news/minnesotans-join-ukraines-independence-day-celebrations-conflict-rages)). In 2021 the governor proclaimed Ukrainian Independence Commemoration Day, the Lowry Avenue Bridge was lit blue and yellow, and the community unfurled a 280-foot flag ([UACC, archived site](https://old.uaccmn.org/30-years-of-ukrainian-independence/)).
- **Ukrainian Festival of Minnesota.** Held at the community center since 2006, normally on the third weekend of September, with free admission, food, dance and music ([UACC](https://uaccmn.org/arts-and-culture/ukrainian-festival-of-minnesota/)). The 2026 festival was postponed during a major renovation of the center; organizers plan the next one for Sept. 17 and 18, 2027 ([Ukrainian Festival MN](https://ukrainianfestivalmn.com/)).
- **Holodomor remembrance (late November).** Remembers the 1932–33 famine in Soviet Ukraine. In 2023 the Minnesota House adopted a resolution naming the last week of November Holodomor Memorial Week; the community center's two pages give the memorial day as Nov. 25 and Nov. 28, so the exact day is unclear ([UACC Holodomor page](https://old.uaccmn.org/holodomor/); [UACC advocacy page](https://old.uaccmn.org/advocate-for-ukraine/)). Commemorations were held at the community center in 2018, by Minnesota's Ukrainian community on Nov. 30, 2022, and at the State Capitol on Nov. 8, 2023 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis); [Holodomor Museum](https://holodomormuseum.org.ua/en/news-museji/minnesota-commemorated-the-holodomor-victims/); [UACC](https://old.uaccmn.org/holodomor/)).
- **International Holiday Bazaar (Sunday after Thanksgiving)** at the community center ([UACC About](https://uaccmn.org/about/)).
- **Anniversary of the full-scale invasion (Feb. 24).** A remembrance, not a celebration. The governor proclaimed Feb. 24, 2024, Solidarity with Ukraine Day at the community's request ([UACC, archived site](https://old.uaccmn.org/advocate-for-ukraine/)).
- **New Year (Jan. 1).** No Minnesota source describing how Ukrainian families here keep it was found.

Dates and sources are in calendar-entries.json.

## 7. Family, home and daily life

**Households.** Compass counts 8,250 Ukrainian Minnesotan households, averaging 2.46 people. Married couples without children under 18 make up 32.3 percent, married couples with children 21.0 percent, and people living alone 28.9 percent ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ukrainian)).

**Families split by war.** Some newcomers arrived without a spouse. An Edina mother told NPR in 2025 that her husband was fighting at the front while she raised two sons here and worked retail ([NPR, March 2025](https://www.npr.org/2025/03/28/nx-s1-5318049/as-protections-expire-ukrainians-war-uncertain-future-uniting-for-ukraine)). Others came as whole families; one St. Paul case manager and her four children lived rent-free in a house an older Minnesota couple had left empty ([MPR News, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/02/23/ukrainians-in-minnesota-pay-it-forward-one-year-after-fleeing-war)).

**Sponsors and hosts.** Private Minnesotans sponsored and housed many newcomers. The First Unitarian Society of Minneapolis, for example, welcomed its fourth sponsored Ukrainian family, a mother and two sons whose father serves in Ukraine's army, in July 2024 ([First Unitarian Society, July 5, 2024](https://firstunitarian.org/blog/welcoming-a-fourth-ukrainian-refugee-family-to-mn/)). First Ukrainian Evangelical Baptist Church has housed wounded soldiers who came for prosthetics ([Baptist Press via ChurchLeaders](https://churchleaders.com/news/436226-minnesota-ukrainian-church-prays-over-injured-soldier-thanks-god-bp.html)).

**Food.** Kramarczuk's menu shows the dishes long served here: cabbage rolls, potato-and-cheese dumplings, sausages with sauerkraut and piroshky, according to the James Beard Foundation ([James Beard Foundation](https://www.jamesbeard.org/stories/americas-classic-kramarczuks)). St. Constantine sells pyrohy (also called varenyky) ([St. Constantine](https://stconstantine.org/)), and the festival has held a varenyky-eating contest ([Ukrainian Festival MN](https://ukrainianfestivalmn.com/)).

**Dress.** The vyshyvanka, an embroidered shirt or blouse, is worn on church days, at festivals and on Vyshyvanka Day; MPR described it as "a form of spiritual armor" in Ukrainian culture ([MPR News, May 2022](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2022/05/20/ukrainian-minnesotans-celebrate-international-vyshyvankas-day)).

**Childhood.** Children's lives combine Minnesota routines and Ukrainian ones. MPR described a newcomer mother spending her evenings at her son's hockey games, "like many Minnesota moms" ([MPR News, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/02/23/ukrainians-in-minnesota-pay-it-forward-one-year-after-fleeing-war)). Cheremosh dance, Plast scouting and the Ukrainian American Youth Association give children cultural programs (section 8).

**Aging.** Among people 65 and older, 11.3 percent are in poverty ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ukrainian)). Luba Perchyshyn, the pysanky artist, spent her last months at Catholic Eldercare in Northeast Minneapolis, which her family described warmly ([Ukrainian Gift Shop](https://ukrainiangiftshop.com/pages/our-story)). No source was found that describes elder care patterns across the community.

## 8. Arts, media, sports and culture

**Dance.** Cheremosh Ukrainian Dance Ensemble, part of the Ukrainian American Youth Association and based at the community center, has about 60 dancers aged 4 to 24, most of them children or grandchildren of immigrants and some born in Ukraine. It performs at the Festival of Nations in St. Paul, the Minnesota State Fair and the Lake Harriet Band Shell. A group called Rosynky was started for young adults who came after the war began, and adult groups (Bureviy) have danced at Canada's National Ukrainian Festival. Registration for 2026–27 is open ([Cheremosh](https://www.cheremoshmn.com/)).

**Music.** Singer-songwriter Yev Rosso (YEV Music), who writes about life as a Ukrainian immigrant in the Midwest, played with the Ukrainian Village Band at Shoreview's first Culture in the Commons event in October 2023; the city chose a Ukrainian theme because Ukrainian residents were the most responsive ([Shoreview Press, 2023](https://www.presspubs.com/shoreview/news/ukrainian-culture-highlighted-in-shoreview-this-october/article_3866c7ea-5cc6-11ee-8b9c-6bcc6b7d78b0.html)). The Ukrainian Village Band, founded in 2007, plays traditional and modern Ukrainian music and rock covers; it organizes concerts by touring Ukrainian performers and an Ivana Kupala festival ([The Cedar](https://www.thecedar.org/events/summer-at-the-cedar-ukrainian-village-band); [Minnesota State Fair](https://www.mnstatefair.org/schedule/ukrainian-village-band)). The Ukrainian National Chorus of the Twin Cities gave benefit concerts during the Holodomor years ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis)), and local choirs sang at the 2022 Holodomor commemoration ([Holodomor Museum](https://holodomormuseum.org.ua/en/news-museji/minnesota-commemorated-the-holodomor-victims/)).

**Folk art.** Pysanky define Ukrainian Minneapolis. Marie Procai made her first Minnesota pysanky with a shoelace tip and crepe-paper dye; her daughter Luba Perchyshyn created an egg-decorating kit and was featured in Slavko Nowytski's 1975 film "Pysanky." National Geographic featured the shop and the Minneapolis community in April 1972 ([Ukrainian Gift Shop](https://ukrainiangiftshop.com/pages/our-story); [MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis)). The community center offers pysanky classes ([UACC About](https://uaccmn.org/about/)).

**Film and history.** Zina Poletz Gutmanis, born in Minneapolis in 1966, recorded oral histories with relatives of Holodomor survivors after the 2018 commemoration; the interviews are held by the University of Minnesota's Immigration History Research Center ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis)). The center also holds the papers of Alexander Granovsky, of Mary Siemon, whose papers document St. Michael's parish, of John Wakiriak of St. Constantine, and the records of the Minnesota branch of the Ukrainian Congress Committee of America, 1949–1986 ([MNopedia bibliography](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis)).

**Sports.** KSTP has profiled young Ukrainian refugees playing hockey in Minneapolis and Minnetonka and a Ukrainian wrestler welcomed in Minnesota ([KSTP topic page](https://kstp.com/tag/ukrainian-refugees/)). The Protez Foundation helped a member of Ukraine's national amputee football team return to competition ([Protez Foundation](https://www.protezfoundation.org/)).

**Media.** See section 4. Twin Cities TV stations and MPR News have covered the community steadily since 2022.

## 9. Civic and political life

**Organizations.** The Ukrainian American Community Center, founded in 1964 as the Ukrainian-American Home by Dmytro Mandybur, calls itself a "fraternal, cultural, and community center." Its board, as listed in October 2026, is led by president Dima Blyshchak ([UACC About](https://uaccmn.org/about/); [UACC board](https://uaccmn.org/board-of-directors/); [MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis)). It runs refugee services with help from a Minnesota Department of Human Services refugee program grant, and says it has served over 2,000 newly arrived families ([UACC letter, June 2025](https://www.house.mn.gov/comm/docs/DhB1hP9jLk2PakdiFQu2CA.pdf)). Its website reports "$7m+" raised in humanitarian aid and "1000+" refugees served in 2023 ([UACC](https://uaccmn.org/)). Its aid page itemizes purchases as of March 2023, including more than $250,000 in emergency medical supplies and about $207,000 for 18 medical evacuation vehicles ([UACC Aid for Ukraine](https://uaccmn.org/aid-for-ukraine/)).

Plast, the Ukrainian scouting organization, started its Twin Cities branch in 1949 among post-war immigrants and had 77 members at its 1958 peak; it dwindled by the 1980s and was revived in 2018, meeting at the community center, according to the Ukrainian Weekly ([Ukrainian Weekly, Feb. 28, 2021](https://plastusa.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/02/The_Ukrainian_Weekly_Plast_Twin_Cities.pdf)). Stand With Ukraine MN, a community crisis committee directed by board member Mykola Sarazhynskyy, raises money and medical supplies and lobbies officials ([Stand With Ukraine MN](https://www.standwithukrainemn.com/about); [UACC board](https://uaccmn.org/board-of-directors/)). The MN Ukrainian American Advocacy Committee says it represents parishes, the Baptist church, the center and the youth association ([UACC, archived site](https://old.uaccmn.org/advocate-for-ukraine/)). The Minnesota branch of the Ukrainian Congress Committee of America has records going back to 1949 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis)). The center lists Plast Minneapolis among its partners ([UACC](https://uaccmn.org/)).

**Engagement with government.** The community asked for, and got, gubernatorial proclamations for Ukraine's 30th Independence Day (2021), Holodomor remembrance and Solidarity with Ukraine Day (Feb. 24, 2024), and a 2023 House resolution on the Holodomor ([UACC, archived pages](https://old.uaccmn.org/advocate-for-ukraine/); [UACC](https://old.uaccmn.org/30-years-of-ukrainian-independence/)). The center thanked the Legislature for a 2022 law directing the State Board of Investment to divest from Russian and Belarusian assets, and in June 2025 backed a proposed state Council on Central and Eastern European Affairs ([UACC letter, June 2025](https://www.house.mn.gov/comm/docs/DhB1hP9jLk2PakdiFQu2CA.pdf)). Committee members joined national Ukraine Action Summits in Washington ([UACC, archived site](https://old.uaccmn.org/advocate-for-ukraine/)). Orest Kramarczuk told KSTP he brought the idea of lighting the Lowry Avenue Bridge in Ukraine's colors to city leaders in February 2022 ([KSTP, Feb. 2022](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/standing-with-ukraine/)).

**Elected officials.** MPR News described Minneapolis's mayor as "of Ukrainian descent" when he spoke at a rally outside St. Constantine on Feb. 24, 2022, and WCCO reported he attended the community's third-anniversary commemoration in February 2025 ([MPR News, Feb. 24, 2022](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2022/02/24/for-minnesotans-with-ties-to-ukraine-anger-and-anxiety-in-wake-of-russian-attack); [CBS Minnesota, Feb. 2025](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/the-ukrainian-american-center-honoring-those-three-years-on/)). No current state legislator or member of Congress who publicly identifies as Ukrainian American could be confirmed. A U.S. senator from Minnesota met wounded Ukrainian soldiers at that 2025 event and called Minnesota one of the leading places for Ukrainian immigrants and refugees to settle ([CBS Minnesota, Feb. 2025](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/the-ukrainian-american-center-honoring-those-three-years-on/)).

**Debates.** Historic splits include the 1925 break between Greek Catholics and Orthodox at St. Constantine ([St. Constantine history](https://stconstantine.org/history.html)). Recent news coverage focuses on U.S. immigration policy and aid to Ukraine; no source was found documenting internal disputes over language, church jurisdiction or politics in Minnesota.

## 10. Education

**Attainment.** Among Ukrainian Minnesotans 25 and older, 94.5 percent finished high school and 53.2 percent hold a bachelor's degree or higher, including 24.9 percent with a graduate or professional degree; 5.5 percent did not finish high school ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ukrainian)).

**Newcomer students.** The community center's Family Education Support Program serves Ukrainian-speaking children and young people from pre-K through age 24 who came on humanitarian parole, helping with schools, tutoring, after-school programs, emotional support and college enrollment, free and in Ukrainian or English ([UACC](https://uaccmn.org/services/family-coaching-and-assistance/)). Its earlier Ukrainian-language guide explains Minnesota school enrollment, English learner testing and special education ([UACC, archived site](https://old.uaccmn.org/education-%d0%be%d1%81%d0%b2%d1%96%d1%82%d0%b0/)). The International Institute of Minnesota taught orientation and money-management classes for newcomers at the center ([MPR News, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/02/23/ukrainians-in-minnesota-pay-it-forward-one-year-after-fleeing-war)).

**Language data.** Ukrainian is not among the 12 most common home languages of Minnesota English learners in 2024–25; the smallest on that list had 649 students. Russian is, with 1,245 English learners in 2025, up from 895 in 2020; the report does not say how many of them are from Ukraine ([MDE, English Learners in Minnesota Report 2024-25, via Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/other/261381.pdf)).

**Heritage schooling.** Saturday Ukrainian school was part of growing up for children of the post-war wave ([MinnPost via TCB](https://tcbmag.com/how-northeast-minneapolis-became-a-center-for-ukrainian-americans/)), and St. Michael's ran a parish Ukrainian school in 2016 ([UOC of the USA, 2016](https://uocofusa.org/news_161122_1)). St. Constantine had a parochial school, begun in 1956 ([St. Constantine history](https://stconstantine.org/history.html)). No current Ukrainian Saturday school in Minnesota could be confirmed.

**University ties.** Alexander Granovsky, who recruited displaced Ukrainians after World War II, was a University of Minnesota professor ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis)). The university's Immigration History Research Center holds major Ukrainian American collections (section 8). Its Center for Holocaust and Genocide Studies keeps a Holodomor resource guide, and the Humphrey School hosted a 2023 panel organized by a Ukrainian Humphrey Fellow ([CHGS](https://cla.umn.edu/chgs/holocaust-genocide-education/resource-guides/holodomor); [UACC](https://old.uaccmn.org/holodomor/)). The Protez Foundation runs Protez Academy, a training project with Century College and contributors from the University of Minnesota and Concordia University ([Protez Foundation](https://www.protezfoundation.org/)).

**Curriculum.** The advocacy committee says the 2023 education law requiring Holocaust and genocide education in social studies means the Holodomor will be taught in Minnesota schools, and that it testified for it ([UACC, archived site](https://old.uaccmn.org/advocate-for-ukraine/)). This is the committee's reading of the law.

## 11. Health and wellbeing, broadly

**Coverage.** 5.3 percent of Ukrainian Minnesotans lack health insurance ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ukrainian)).

**Newcomer health.** The Minnesota Department of Health's Center of Excellence in Newcomer Health publishes a clinicians' guide to Ukrainian newcomers, guidance on translating Ukrainian vaccination records, and a course on vaccine conversations with Russian- and Ukrainian-speaking patients ([MDH Ukrainian clinical guidance](https://www.health.mn.gov/communities/rih/coe/clinical/ukrainian.html)). The community center's earlier site, in Ukrainian and English, explained the free refugee health screening, free TB testing in Ramsey, Hennepin and Olmsted counties, and sliding-fee clinics from Minneapolis to Duluth ([UACC, archived site](https://old.uaccmn.org/useful-links/)). Its seminars cover health insurance and finding doctors ([UACC Seminars](https://uaccmn.org/services/friday-seminars/)).

**War trauma.** A community center worker told MPR that each newcomer brings "a unique set of traumatic experiences" ([MPR News, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/02/23/ukrainians-in-minnesota-pay-it-forward-one-year-after-fleeing-war)). No Minnesota study measures trauma or mental health in this community.

**Prosthetics.** The Protez Foundation, founded in Minnesota in 2022 and now with programs in Ukraine, gives free prosthetic care to Ukrainian soldiers and civilians who lost limbs in the war, with rehabilitation in the United States and mental health support in Ukraine ([Protez Foundation](https://www.protezfoundation.org/)). By December 2022 it had fitted 22 soldiers; WCCO reported in March 2023 that it had fitted 43 soldiers and had more than 700 people on its waiting list ([FOX 9, Dec. 2022](https://www.fox9.com/news/ukrainian-soldiers-come-to-minnesota-for-prosthetics-and-then-return-to-the-fight); [CBS Minnesota, March 6, 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/wounded-soldiers-from-ukraine-fitted-with-prosthetics-thanks-to-minnesota-nonprofit/)). Ukraine's president awarded co-founder and CEO Yura Aroshidze the Order of Merit, Third Class, in September 2026 ([Protez Foundation](https://www.protezfoundation.org/)).

**Traditional practice.** No Minnesota source on traditional healing or home remedies among Ukrainians was found.

## 12. Disability

**Minnesota numbers.** Compass reports that 10.9 percent of Ukrainian Minnesotans have a disability, give or take 3.1, and 8.0 percent of those under 65, give or take 3.3 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ukrainian)). These are survey measures of difficulty with hearing, seeing, thinking, walking, self-care or living independently; they do not show who uses services. The margins are wide, so comparisons with other groups should be careful. No MDH or DHS report breaking out Ukrainian Minnesotans' disability service use was found.

**Disability produced by war.** The clearest Minnesota evidence is about limb loss. Since 2022, Minnesota has become a place where Ukrainians wounded in the war come for prosthetics, through the Protez Foundation (section 11). Patients have included soldiers hurt by landmines and drones and civilians, among them a person hurt in an attack on a stadium in March 2022 and a 10-year-old boy born with atrophied legs who took his first steps on prosthetics in Minneapolis ([Protez Foundation](https://www.protezfoundation.org/); [FOX 9, Dec. 2022](https://www.fox9.com/news/ukrainian-soldiers-come-to-minnesota-for-prosthetics-and-then-return-to-the-fight)). Most soldiers FOX 9 met in 2022 planned to go back to Ukraine, and at least one had returned to the front ([FOX 9, Dec. 2022](https://www.fox9.com/news/ukrainian-soldiers-come-to-minnesota-for-prosthetics-and-then-return-to-the-fight)). The community center and churches hosted them; WCCO described the center as a "healing zone" for more than 50 wounded soldiers in March 2023 ([CBS Minnesota, March 18, 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/ukrainian-community-thanks-minnesotans-for-a-year-of-support/); [Baptist Press via ChurchLeaders](https://churchleaders.com/news/436226-minnesota-ukrainian-church-prays-over-injured-soldier-thanks-god-bp.html)).

**Questions the evidence raises.**
- *Who defines disability?* In Minnesota, a person applying for Medical Assistance on the basis of disability needs either a Social Security disability determination or a certification from the State Medical Review Team, which a county or tribal eligibility worker requests from DHS ([Disability Hub MN](https://disabilityhubmn.org/top-topics/other/state-medical-review-team-smrt/common-questions/)). Whether a disability status or diagnosis from Ukraine carries over is an open question. The community center's Ukrainian-language school guide explains special education evaluation to parents ([UACC, archived site](https://old.uaccmn.org/education-%d0%be%d1%81%d0%b2%d1%96%d1%82%d0%b0/)). No source was found showing how often Ukrainian records are accepted or redone here.
- *What does a label open or close?* For people on parole or TPS, eligibility for some benefits depends on status (the community center's guide notes that TPS holders are not eligible for federal refugee benefits, while pregnant women and children under 18 can apply for Medicaid) ([UACC, archived site](https://old.uaccmn.org/useful-links/)). Families may weigh services against immigration worries. No study measures this among Ukrainian Minnesotans.
- *What produced an impairment?* War injuries and trauma are documented for some newcomers. No source links the overall Compass rate to any particular cause, and the reasons for it remain open.
- *Trauma or disability?* In October 2022, the University of Minnesota's Institute on Community Integration described a Ukrainian American student who volunteered at a Minneapolis elementary school with a newly arrived kindergartener whose father was fighting in Ukraine. The child did not have a disability, but the student began writing a handbook to help school staff tell cultural norms apart from trauma responses that need mental health care ([ICI, Oct. 2022](https://ici.umn.edu/news/immediate-impact-article-sparks-ukrainian-connection)). No later version of that handbook was found.
- *Caregiving.* The community center's long-term care workforce program trains newcomers to work as caregivers for older and disabled Minnesotans ([UACC](https://uaccmn.org/services/long-term-care-workforce/)). Ukrainian newcomers are thus part of the disability workforce as well as, sometimes, its clients.

**Organizations.** No Minnesota organization specifically for disabled Ukrainians or their families was found. The community center's Family Resource Connector helps newcomers find services generally ([UACC](https://uaccmn.org/)). Apart from the 2022 ICI story above, no Ukrainian-specific disability material from the Institute on Community Integration or DHS was found.

## 13. What is changing now

**Legal status (as of Oct. 31, 2026).** Uniting for Ukraine stopped taking new applications in January 2025, when the federal government suspended humanitarian parole programs; almost 240,000 Ukrainians had been paroled through it nationally, and many now face expiring parole with few ways to renew ([Ukraine Immigration Task Force, May 2026](https://ukrainetaskforce.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/U4U-Four-Year-Retrospective.pdf)). NPR reported in March 2025 that USCIS had paused processing for parolees' requests, and profiled Minneapolis-area families facing loss of work permits ([NPR, March 2025](https://www.npr.org/2025/03/28/nx-s1-5318049/as-protections-expire-ukrainians-war-uncertain-future-uniting-for-ukraine)). Temporary Protected Status for Ukraine was set to end Oct. 19, 2026. On Oct. 18, 2026, the administration said Ukrainians with TPS "retain protection including work authorization" until an announcement is made, but no new end date has been published, and the Ukraine Immigration Task Force warned that federal verification systems still show Oct. 19 as the expiration ([Ukraine Immigration Task Force, Oct. 2026](https://ukrainetaskforce.org/ukraine-s-tps-is-theoretically-extended-for-6-months-but-dhs-has-yet-to-publish-updates-or-implement-extension/); [Jackson Lewis, Oct. 2026](https://www.globalimmigrationblog.com/2026/10/uscis-ukraine-tps-continues-for-now/)). These are national rules; no source was found that counts how many Minnesotans are affected.

**Fewer new arrivals.** MDH's data show arrivals from Ukraine falling from 1,049 in 2022 to 739 in 2024, as other groups grew ([MDH](https://www.health.mn.gov/communities/rih/stats/country.csv)). Data for 2025 were not yet published.

**The community center is rebuilding.** The 2026 Ukrainian Festival was postponed while the center "undergoes a major renovation as we build for the future" ([Ukrainian Festival MN](https://ukrainianfestivalmn.com/)). Its calendar page listed no events in late October 2026 and pointed to Facebook ([UACC calendar](https://uaccmn.org/calendar/)).

**Parish leadership.** St. Katherine's in Arden Hills changed pastors in May 2026 after 28 years ([UOC of the USA](https://www.uocofusa.org/news_260511_1)).

**Generations meeting.** Cheremosh's newest dance group was formed for young adults who came after the war began, alongside dancers who are children or grandchildren of earlier immigrants ([Cheremosh](https://www.cheremoshmn.com/)). Newcomers have joined the staff: a woman who arrived in 2022 was working as the community center's outreach manager by 2023 ([MPR News, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/02/23/ukrainians-in-minnesota-pay-it-forward-one-year-after-fleeing-war)).

**Grief and worry.** At the third-anniversary commemoration in February 2025, a family who had moved to Champlin two years earlier told WCCO, "Our family is still there and we're really worried about them" ([CBS Minnesota, Feb. 2025](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/the-ukrainian-american-center-honoring-those-three-years-on/)).

**War fatigue.** Community leaders have urged Minnesotans not to look away. "When we start to feel like, well it's this far-off thing," a Stand With Ukraine MN volunteer told WCCO in 2023, people should remember Ukrainians are "fighting and dying every day" ([CBS Minnesota, 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/ukrainian-community-thanks-minnesotans-for-a-year-of-support/)). At the 2025 Independence Day gathering, a board member said, "Ukraine stays strong" ([FOX 9, Aug. 2025](https://www.fox9.com/news/minnesotans-join-ukraines-independence-day-celebrations-conflict-rages)).

## 14. Strengths and contributions

- **Institutions that last.** A parish founded in 1913, an Orthodox parish since 1925, a community center since 1964, a deli since 1954 and a pysanky shop since 1947 all still operate ([St. Constantine](https://stconstantine.org/history.html); [MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis); [James Beard Foundation](https://www.jamesbeard.org/stories/americas-classic-kramarczuks); [Ukrainian Gift Shop](https://ukrainiangiftshop.com/pages/our-story)).
- **A welcome built by the community.** The federal government did not notify states when Ukrainians arrived; the community center, churches, sponsors and volunteers filled much of the gap, later with DHS grant support ([MPR News, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/02/23/ukrainians-in-minnesota-pay-it-forward-one-year-after-fleeing-war); [UACC letter, 2025](https://www.house.mn.gov/comm/docs/DhB1hP9jLk2PakdiFQu2CA.pdf)).
- **Care for the wounded.** The Protez Foundation made Minnesota a destination for war amputees and is training prosthetic specialists with Century College ([Protez Foundation](https://www.protezfoundation.org/)).
- **Keeping an art alive.** Minneapolis families helped preserve pysanky when the art faded in Soviet Ukraine ([MinnPost via TCB](https://tcbmag.com/how-northeast-minneapolis-became-a-center-for-ukrainian-americans/)).
- **Workforce.** Newcomers are being trained for Minnesota's long-term care jobs ([UACC](https://uaccmn.org/services/long-term-care-workforce/)).
- **Memory and education.** Oral histories of the Holodomor, archives at the University of Minnesota, and advocacy for genocide education add to what all Minnesotans can learn ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/ukrainian-american-community-northeast-minneapolis); [UACC](https://old.uaccmn.org/advocate-for-ukraine/)).

## What could not be established

- **A current count of newcomers in Minnesota.** No source gives how many Ukrainians who came since 2022 live here now. State and federal figures cover different things and different dates.
- **Status of affected Minnesotans.** How many Ukrainian Minnesotans hold parole, TPS or other status, and how many have lost work authorization, could not be found.
- **Occupations and industries.** No Minnesota breakdown of Ukrainian workers by industry or occupation, or by arrival period, was found.
- **Greater Minnesota.** Compass says 28 percent live outside the Twin Cities, but no source showed where, apart from the closed Chisholm church and a small number of arrivals in Olmsted County.
- **Businesses.** No Ukrainian chamber of commerce, business directory, Ukrainian grocery, restaurant (other than Kramarczuk's), law, accounting, medical, insurance, travel or salon business owned by Ukrainian Minnesotans could be confirmed.
- **Minnesota media.** No Ukrainian-language Minnesota newspaper, radio or online outlet could be found.
- **Church calendars and websites.** St. Katherine's own site could not be found, and neither Orthodox parish's pages state which date it uses for Christmas. Whether St. Stephen's in St. Paul still meets could not be confirmed.
- **A current Saturday school.** No current Ukrainian heritage language school in Minnesota could be confirmed.
- **Disability service use.** No DHS, MDH or University of Minnesota data on Ukrainian Minnesotans' use of disability services, and no study of how Ukrainian families here understand disability, were found.
- **Elected officials.** Apart from MPR's 2022 description of the Minneapolis mayor's Ukrainian descent, no current Minnesota elected official who publicly identifies as Ukrainian American could be confirmed.
- **Jewish and Russian-speaking Ukrainians.** How Jewish and Russian-speaking Minnesotans born in Ukraine identify, and how they relate to Ukrainian institutions here, was not documented in any Minnesota source found.
- **Blocked sites.** The Minnesota Department of Human Services' "Ukrainians in Minnesota" page, Sahan Journal, MinnPost's original page, KARE 11, Baptist Press, the Festival of Nations and the Minnesota Orchestra site blocked or rate-limited access. MinnPost's story was read through its Twin Cities Business reprint, and the Baptist Press story through ChurchLeaders.
