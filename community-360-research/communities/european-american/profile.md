# European Americans in Minnesota

This page is for Minnesotans of German, Norwegian, Irish, Swedish, English, Polish, Finnish, Czech, Dutch, Danish, Italian, Scottish, Greek and other European heritage who want to see their own culture described with evidence. It does not repeat the separate pages on Ukrainian, Russian-speaking, Bosnian, French-speaking, American and Minnesota culture. Most people described here have families that arrived generations ago, and many carry several European ancestries at once.

## 1. People and place

**How many, and how the count works.** The Census Bureau's 2024 American Community Survey (ACS) asks people to write in their ancestry, and a person may give up to two. Those answers show German as by far the most common ancestry in Minnesota, at about 1,609,000 (give or take 22,400). Next come Norwegian (about 662,000), Irish (569,000), English (361,000), Swedish (354,000) and Polish (214,000). Smaller but sizable groups are French (145,000), Italian (132,000), Finnish (93,000), Dutch (79,000), Czech (75,000, plus 8,100 who wrote "Czechoslovakian"), Scottish (73,000) and Danish (59,000). Another 94,000 wrote simply "Scandinavian" and 105,000 "European." All of these are one-year survey estimates with margins of error ([ACS 2024 table B04006 via Census Reporter](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B04006&geo_ids=04000US27)). Because one person can list two ancestries, the groups overlap and cannot be added up. About 1.29 million answers were unclassified or not reported, and about 206,000 people wrote "American," so these counts understate how many Minnesotans have European roots ([same table](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B04006&geo_ids=04000US27)).

Smaller European ancestries also appear in the 2024 table: Welsh (about 20,000), Swiss (16,000), Austrian (16,000), Belgian (13,000), Greek (13,000), Hungarian (11,000), Croatian (9,700), Slovene (8,700), Slovak (6,400), Romanian (5,900), Lithuanian (5,300), Luxembourger (5,000) and Icelander (2,900, give or take 900) ([ACS B04006](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B04006&geo_ids=04000US27)).

**Race is a different question.** In the same survey, about 4,355,000 Minnesotans, roughly 76 percent of 5.74 million, identified as white alone and not Hispanic ([ACS 2024 table B03002](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B01003,B02001,B03002&geo_ids=04000US27)). Minnesota Compass rounds this to 4.3 million, or 74 percent, and notes the share was 83 percent in 2010. It also shows the change by age: people of color are 7 percent of Minnesotans 80 and older but 42 percent of children under 5 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/race-ethnicity)). Not every white Minnesotan has European roots, and not every person of European descent marks only "white," so race and ancestry should not be treated as the same thing.

**Born here, mostly.** Very few European-heritage Minnesotans are immigrants now. The 2024 ACS counts about 48,000 Minnesota residents born anywhere in Europe, out of 524,000 foreign-born. Many of those were born in Ukraine, Russia, Belarus or Bosnia, which have their own pages. For the older streams the numbers are small: about 5,800 born in Germany, 6,400 in the United Kingdom, 1,300 in Italy, 1,300 in Poland, 1,000 in Sweden and fewer than 1,000 each in Ireland, the Netherlands, Norway and Denmark, most with wide margins of error ([ACS 2024 table B05006](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B05006&geo_ids=04000US27)). The median age of white non-Hispanic Minnesotans is 43.6 ([ACS 2024 table B01002H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B01002H&geo_ids=04000US27)).

**How people came.** Settlement came in layers. Treaties signed in 1851 moved Dakota lands in southern Minnesota into federal hands, and European and American settlers moved onto them right away ([MNopedia, Immigration to Goodhue County](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/immigration-goodhue-county)). The Homestead Act of 1862 offered 160 acres, and railroads advertised land in Norway for less than two dollars an acre ([MNopedia, Norwegian Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/norwegian-immigration-minnesota)).

- **Norwegians** came in waves from 1851 to 1920. There were about 7,000 in 1857, 50,000 in 1870, more than 120,000 in 1880 and more than 250,000 by 1905, when about 45,000 lived in Minneapolis. MNopedia calls the Twin Cities "the unofficial capital of Norwegian America." By 1870, 1,135 of Spring Grove's roughly 1,200 residents were Norwegian, most from Hallingdal ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/norwegian-immigration-minnesota)).
- **Swedes**: of about 1.25 million who came to the United States from 1845 to 1930, more settled in Minnesota than in any other state, nearly 300,000 in all. They started in Chisago County in 1851 and came in five waves, with nearly 100,000 in the state by 1890 ([MNopedia, Swedish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/swedish-immigration-minnesota)).
- **Germans** were the single largest ethnic group in Minnesota when the United States entered World War I in 1917 ([MNopedia, Anti-German Nativism](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/anti-german-nativism-1917-1919)).
- **Irish** families such as the Irelands and O'Gormans reached St. Paul in May 1852, during the years of Ireland's Great Famine. In 1876 Bishop John Ireland founded the Catholic Colonization Bureau to settle Catholic families on railroad land in western Minnesota ([MNopedia, John Ireland](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/ireland-john-1838-1918)).
- **Poles** formed the state's first Polish community at Winona in 1858, with Kashubians from the Baltic coast. Silesians followed in Wright, Morrison and McLeod counties. Later came displaced persons after World War II, Solidarity-era refugees in the 1980s, and economic migrants after 1989 ([MNopedia, Polish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/polish-immigration-minnesota)).
- **The Iron Range** grew from fewer than 5,000 people in 1885 to more than 100,000 by 1920. Immigrants were more than half of the Range's population and 85 percent of mine workers. After 1910, Finns were the largest immigrant group, about a quarter of the foreign-born, followed by Slovenes and Croats (over 20 percent together) and then Italians and Swedes (near 10 percent each) ([MNopedia, Immigration to the Iron Range](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/immigration-iron-range-1880-1930)).
- **Italians**: Minnesota's Italian-born population peaked at 9,688 in 1910 ([MNopedia, Columbus Memorial](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/christopher-columbus-memorial-st-paul)). About 80 percent of Italians bound for Minnesota between 1899 and 1914 settled in the Twin Cities and Duluth ([Heavy Table](https://heavytable.com/timeline-italian-food-minnesota/)).
- **Danes** at Tyler (1885) and Clarks Grove, **Czechs and Slovaks** in St. Paul, Montgomery and New Prague, and **Scots** at Mapleton (from 1856) built their own churches, halls and clubs ([MNopedia, Danebod](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/structure/danebod); [MNopedia, St. Paul Sokol](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/st-paul-sokol); [MNopedia, Scottish organizations](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/scottish-cultural-organizations-minnesota)).

**Where.** Some places still carry one heritage strongly: New Ulm calls itself "Minnesota's most German city" ([Visit New Ulm, Aug. 2026](https://www.newulm.com/2026/08/12/new-ulm-oktoberfest-usa-today-10best-2026/)), and Spring Grove calls itself Minnesota's first Norwegian settlement ([City of Spring Grove](https://www.springgrovemn.com/heritage)). By 1880, Goodhue County held the state's largest Swedish group, a dense Norwegian area in its southwest, a German tract and an Irish colony ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/immigration-goodhue-county)). Montgomery is billed as the "Kolacky Capital of the World" ([Minnesota Prairie Roots, May 8, 2025](https://mnprairieroots.com/2025/05/08/montgomery-kolacky-days-royalty-honor-czech-heritage-culture/)), and New Prague runs a Czech harvest festival ([KEYC, Sept. 20, 2024](https://www.keyc.com/2024/09/20/40th-donky-festival/)). In the cities, old ethnic neighborhoods have mostly turned over: Swede Hollow in St. Paul, Bohemian Flats on the Minneapolis riverbank, Northeast Minneapolis's Polish parishes, and the Scandinavian Cedar-Riverside of "Snoose Boulevard" ([MNopedia, Swede Hollow](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/swede-hollow); [MNopedia, Bohemian Flats](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/bohemian-flats-minneapolis); [MNopedia, Swedish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/swedish-immigration-minnesota)).

**Languages.** English is now the home language for nearly everyone in this group. In 2024, about 14,400 Minnesotans age 5 and older spoke German at home, about 3,000 Italian, 2,200 Polish and 450 Greek. The Scandinavian languages are not counted separately and fall inside "other Indo-European languages" (8,300 people in all) ([ACS 2024 table B16001](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B16001&geo_ids=04000US27)). The switch to English came fast. Minnesota Lutheran services held in English rose from 22 percent in 1915 to 93 percent in 1945, and World War I pressure sped it up ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/norwegian-immigration-minnesota)). In Spring Grove in 2015, a University of Oslo linguist found Norwegian still spoken "only by old people" whose own parents had discouraged passing it on ([Post Bulletin, July 14, 2015](https://www.postbulletin.com/newsmd/video-spring-grove-carries-on-norwegian-heritage)).

## 2. All walks of life

The figures below describe white, non-Hispanic Minnesotans in the 2024 ACS, a group that is mostly but not only of European descent. The survey does not publish income by ancestry. Dollar figures are in 2024 dollars.

- **Comfortable and wealthy households.** Median household income is $91,406 (give or take $862), above the state's $87,117 ([ACS B19013H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B19013H&geo_ids=04000US27); [ACS B19013](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B19013&geo_ids=04000US27)). About 14.5 percent of these households, some 277,000, had incomes of $200,000 or more, and about a quarter had $150,000 or more ([ACS B19001H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B19001H&geo_ids=04000US27)). Wealth in this group also has deep roots. By the late 1800s, Norwegian Americans owned land worth about $650 million (more than $16 billion in 2018 dollars), and a third of them lived in Minnesota ([MNopedia, Norwegian Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/norwegian-immigration-minnesota)). Swan Turnblad, a Swedish immigrant newspaper publisher, built the mansion that became the American Swedish Institute ([MNopedia, Swedish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/swedish-immigration-minnesota)).
- **Professionals and owners.** Among white non-Hispanic adults 25 and older, 41.8 percent hold a bachelor's degree or more, and women (710,000) outnumber men (614,000) at that level ([ACS C15002H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=C15002H&geo_ids=04000US27)). Per-person income averages $52,918 ([ACS B19301H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B19301H&geo_ids=04000US27)).
- **Homeowners.** About 77 percent of white non-Hispanic households own their homes, compared with about 72 percent of all Minnesota households ([ACS B25003H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B25003H&geo_ids=04000US27); [ACS B25003](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B25003&geo_ids=04000US27)).
- **Working families and the middle.** The largest single band is households earning $75,000 to $99,999, about 254,000 households. About 722,000 adults whose schooling ended with high school and about 1 million with some college or an associate degree together make up more than half of adults 25 and older ([ACS B19001H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B19001H&geo_ids=04000US27); [ACS C15002H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=C15002H&geo_ids=04000US27)).
- **People in poverty.** About 312,000 white non-Hispanic Minnesotans, 7.4 percent, lived below the poverty line in 2024. The statewide rate for everyone was 9.3 percent ([ACS B17001H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B17001H&geo_ids=04000US27); [ACS B17001](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B17001&geo_ids=04000US27)). About 209,000 households, 11 percent, had incomes under $25,000, and about 102,000 adults had not finished high school ([ACS B19001H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B19001H&geo_ids=04000US27); [ACS C15002H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=C15002H&geo_ids=04000US27)).

**Place makes a large difference.** Five-year ACS figures for 2020 to 2024 show white non-Hispanic median household income of $127,202 in suburban Carver County and $107,397 in Hennepin County. It was $71,493 in St. Louis County, home to Duluth and much of the Iron Range, $70,301 in Itasca County and $61,368 in Aitkin County. Poverty for this group ranged from 4.1 percent in Carver to 12.0 percent in St. Louis and 12.4 percent in Aitkin. In Stearns County the rate was 10.0 percent. Homeownership was highest in Aitkin (86 percent), where incomes are lowest ([ACS 2020-2024 five-year, tables B19013H, B17001H and B25003H by county](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B19013H,B17001H,B25003H&geo_ids=05000US27053,05000US27137,05000US27061,05000US27001,05000US27015,05000US27055,05000US27145,05000US27019,05000US27123,05000US27119)).

**Hardship has a long history here too.** Swede Hollow gave the poorest immigrants in St. Paul a home for nearly a century, in houses without plumbing, electricity or roads ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/swede-hollow)). On Minneapolis's Bohemian Flats, Slovak, Czech and other families lived on a floodplain without sewer service, suffered diphtheria and typhoid, and resisted eviction in 1923 before the city cleared the area in 1931 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/bohemian-flats-minneapolis)). Irish families from Connemara who could not survive their first winter on western Minnesota farms ended up in a St. Paul shantytown called Connemara Patch ([Visit Saint Paul, Irish history](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/saint-pauls-irish-history/)). County poor farms housed the old, poor and mentally ill together from the 1860s, and the Fillmore County farm ran until 1943 ([MNopedia, Fillmore County Poor Farm](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/fillmore-county-poor-farm)).

## 3. Work and the economy

**Farms and cooperatives.** Danish farmers at Clarks Grove founded one of Minnesota's first cooperative creameries in 1890, after Hans Peter Jensen studied butter-making in Denmark. By 1898 Minnesota had more than 550 cooperative creameries, and by 1928 it held nearly half of all U.S. cooperative creameries. Land O'Lakes, founded in 1921, grew from this movement ([MNopedia, Clarks Grove Cooperative Creamery](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/clarks-grove-cooperative-creamery)). Rural work still carries strain. The state's free, 24-hour Farm and Rural Helpline was taking about 30,000 calls a year, a staff member told CBS News in 2022 ([CBS News, March 31, 2022](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/minnesota-hotline-works-to-improve-mental-health-of-small-farmers/)). Rural systems are covered in depth on the separate rural page.

**Mines and mills.** The Mesabi Range has shipped iron ore since the 1890s ([MNopedia, Mesabi Iron Range](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/mesabi-iron-range)). In 1916 up to 8,000 miners, most of them immigrants, struck under the Industrial Workers of the World for an eight-hour day and an end to contract pay. One of those killed in the violence was a Finnish soda distributor, Tomi Ladvalla. The strike ended Sept. 17 without a settlement, but mining companies soon raised pay and moved to an eight-hour day ([MNopedia, Mesabi Strike of 1916](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/mesabi-iron-range-strike-1916)). In the cities, Swedes and Italians worked in breweries, mills and on the railroads ([MNopedia, Swede Hollow](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/swede-hollow); [Ramsey County Historical Society](https://rchs.com/publishing/catalog/history-revealed-italians-on-st-pauls-east-side/)), and Bohemian Flats residents supplied labor to flour and lumber mills ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/bohemian-flats-minneapolis)). Young Norwegian and Swedish women often worked as domestic servants and cooks in wealthy homes ([MNopedia, Daughters of Norway](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/daughters-norway); [MNopedia, Swedish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/swedish-immigration-minnesota)).

**Labor.** The 1934 Minneapolis Teamsters strike, which won union recognition, was organized by rank-and-file leaders including Carl Skoglund ([MNopedia, Teamsters Strike of 1934](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/minneapolis-teamsters-strike-1934)). In 1985, about 1,500 workers of Local P-9 struck Hormel in Austin over a 23 percent wage cut. The strike lasted 13 months and split the town. Hormel hired about 540 replacement workers, most of them Mexican migrant workers, which is part of how Austin's population changed ([MNopedia, Hormel Strike](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/hormel-strike-1985-1986)).

**Mutual aid as business.** Sons of Norway began in Minneapolis in 1895 as a fraternal insurance society for immigrants ([MNopedia, Sons of Norway in Cottonwood County](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/sons-norway-cottonwood-county)). The Daughters of Norway offered burial insurance to working women ([MNopedia, Daughters of Norway](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/daughters-norway)). In May 2026 the insurer BetterLife announced a definitive agreement to take Sons of Norway in as one of its community organizations, pending regulatory approval ([PR Newswire via WOWK, May 2026](https://digital-release.wowktv.com/business/press-releases/cision/20260506CG52468/betterlife-announces-definitive-agreement-to-partner-with-sons-of-norway-extending-its-presence-to-more-than-400-communities)).

**Professions and industry today.** Polish engineers who came as refugees in the 1980s found jobs at 3M and Honeywell ([MNopedia, Polish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/polish-immigration-minnesota)). Detailed current occupation data by ancestry were not available in the tables opened.

## 4. Businesses and commercial life

Heritage businesses are spread thin now, since most families have been here for generations, but a working core remains. Full details, links and status are in the accompanying business list.

- **Scandinavian.** Ingebretsen's Nordic Marketplace on Lake Street in Minneapolis has sold Nordic foods, lefse and gifts since 1921 ([Ingebretsen's](https://ingebretsens.com/)). Taste of Scandinavia Bakery takes online orders ([Taste of Scandinavia](https://tasteofscandinavia.com/)). Norsland Lefse sells lefse and gifts in Rushford ([Norsland Lefse](https://norslandlefse.com/)). Norway House in Minneapolis runs exhibits, events and a library ([Norway House](https://www.norwayhouse.org/)).
- **German.** Gasthof zur Gemütlichkeit in Northeast Minneapolis ([Gasthof](https://gasthofzg.com/)), Waldmann Brewery in an 1857 building in St. Paul ([Waldmann](https://waldmannbrewery.com/)), Bauhaus Brew Labs in Northeast Minneapolis ([Bauhaus](https://www.bauhausbrewlabs.com/)), and, in New Ulm, August Schell Brewing and Domeier's German Store ([Schell's](https://www.schellsbrewery.com/); [Visit New Ulm](https://www.newulm.com/2026/08/20/coming-to-new-ulm-for-oktoberfest-5/)). The Glockenspiel restaurant's old web address no longer leads to a working site.
- **Italian.** DeGidio's on West Seventh in St. Paul dates to 1933 ([DeGidio's](https://degidios.com/)). Broders' Cucina Italiana in Minneapolis dates to 1982 ([Broders'](https://broders.com/)). Cossetta, which Heavy Table traces to Calabrian immigrant Michael Cossetta's food shop on St. Paul's Upper Levee, still has a working site ([Cossetta](https://cossettas.com/); [Heavy Table](https://heavytable.com/timeline-italian-food-minnesota/)).
- **Irish and British.** Visit Saint Paul's current guide lists eight Irish pubs and coffee shops, including The Dubliner, open since 1983, Half Time Rec and two Claddagh coffee shops. Irish on Grand, which Visit Saint Paul calls the oldest Irish store in the Twin Cities, closed its Grand Avenue storefront and now sells at the State Fair ([Visit Saint Paul, Irish bars](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/irish-bars-restaurants/); [Irish on Grand](https://www.irishongrand.com/)). Minneapolis has Merlins Rest and Brit's Pub ([Merlins Rest](https://www.merlinsrest.com/); [Brit's Pub](https://britspub.com/)).
- **Polish, Czech and Danish.** The Polish American Cultural Institute in downtown St. Paul keeps Sunday hours ([PACIM](https://pacim.org/)). The Danish American Center in Minneapolis posts monthly news ([Danish American Center](https://www.danishamericancenter.org/)). In New Prague, Dožínky is run by the Chamber of Commerce to celebrate both Czech harvest traditions and Main Street businesses ([KEYC](https://www.keyc.com/2024/09/20/40th-donky-festival/)).
- **Media.** Sons of Norway publishes Viking magazine ([Sons of Norway](https://www.sofn.com/)). KCHK radio in New Prague brought a radio tent to Montgomery's Czech May Day in 2025 ([Minnesota Prairie Roots](https://mnprairieroots.com/2025/05/08/montgomery-kolacky-days-royalty-honor-czech-heritage-culture/); [Ingstad Media New Prague](https://ingstadmedia.com/new-prague/)). Earlier generations ran a large foreign-language press. Swan Turnblad published the Swedish-language Svenska Amerikanska Posten from 1885 to 1940 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/swedish-immigration-minnesota)), and German papers were watched and pressured during World War I ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/anti-german-nativism-1917-1919)).
- **Chambers.** No current Minnesota-based Norwegian, Swedish, German, Italian or Polish chamber of commerce website could be confirmed. New Prague's Chamber of Commerce runs a heritage festival ([KEYC](https://www.keyc.com/2024/09/20/40th-donky-festival/)).

## 5. Religion and spiritual life

**Lutherans and Catholics, and many with no religion.** Pew Research Center's 2023-24 survey of Minnesota adults of every background found 22 percent in mainline Protestant churches and 20 percent in evangelical churches. Lutherans in both groups add up to about 23 percent, including 12 percent in the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America (ELCA) and 5 percent in the Lutheran Church-Missouri Synod. Catholics were 18 percent. About 29 percent claimed no religion: 5 percent atheist, 9 percent agnostic and 15 percent "nothing in particular." The margin of error is plus or minus 5.7 points, and these figures cover all Minnesotans, not only those of European descent ([Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/religious-landscape-study/state/minnesota/)).

**How the churches were built.** Lutheran congregations were the main source of both unity and dispute in Norwegian towns. One Spring Grove resident joked in the 1970s that you could tell a Norwegian settlement by "two churches close together, preferably on opposite sides of the road." By 1865 there were five separate Norwegian American Lutheran synods ([MNopedia, Norwegian Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/norwegian-immigration-minnesota)). Swedes built the Augustana Synod, founded in 1860. Other Swedes joined the Mission Covenant, Methodist, Baptist, Pentecostal and Episcopal churches and the Salvation Army ([MNopedia, Swedish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/swedish-immigration-minnesota)). Grundtvigian Danes at Tyler built a folk school before their church and did not hold English services until 1943 ([MNopedia, Danebod](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/structure/danebod)). Slovak Lutherans built St. Emmanuel church on Bohemian Flats in 1889 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/bohemian-flats-minneapolis)).

Catholic life ran through ethnic parishes. Czechs and Poles founded St. Stanislaus in St. Paul in 1872 and then separated. Poles founded St. Adalbert in Frogtown in 1881 and Holy Cross in Northeast Minneapolis in 1886 ([MNopedia, Polish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/polish-immigration-minnesota)). East Side Italians built St. Ambrose in 1915 ([Ramsey County Historical Society](https://rchs.com/publishing/catalog/history-revealed-italians-on-st-pauls-east-side/)). Irish and German Catholics did not always agree. Archbishop John Ireland pushed for English in worship and schools, which alienated German Catholics who held Mass in German, and during World War I this helped Irish Catholics move into leadership ([MNopedia, Anti-German Nativism](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/anti-german-nativism-1917-1919)). Ireland also founded the seminary that grew into the University of St. Thomas and the Saint Paul Seminary ([MNopedia, John Ireland](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/ireland-john-1838-1918)).

**Congregations still keeping a language.** Mindekirken, the Norwegian Memorial Church in Minneapolis, holds a festival worship service and a flag parade every May 17, with its children's Norwegian club and its band ([The Alley, May 2, 2026](https://alleynews.org/2026/05/join-mindekirken-for-syttende-mai/)). In 2015 Mindekirken's pastor led a joint Norwegian and Swedish service at the Scandinavian Summer Fest at Minnehaha Park ([Nordstjernan](https://nordstjernan.com/news/midwest/7000)).

**Shrinking and merging.** In the ELCA's Minneapolis Area Synod, baptized membership fell 16 percent, from 169,192 in 2017 to 142,410 in 2024, and congregations fell from 144 to 135. In-person worship dropped from 39,180 a week in 2017 to 20,913 in 2024, with another 14,398 attending online. Of 96,955 active participants in 2024, 86,814 were counted as white ([ELCA, Minneapolis Area Synod data kit, Dec. 2025](https://cdn.elca.org/cdn/wp-content/uploads/3G_Minneapolis_Area_data_kit.pdf)). Membership fell 20 percent in the Northeastern Minnesota Synod (56,501 to 45,094) and 13 percent in the Southwestern Minnesota Synod (108,365 to 93,946) over the same years ([ELCA Northeastern Minnesota](https://cdn.elca.org/cdn/wp-content/uploads/3E_Northeastern_Minnesota_data_kit.pdf); [ELCA Southwestern Minnesota](https://cdn.elca.org/cdn/wp-content/uploads/3F_Southwestern_Minnesota_data_kit.pdf)). A 2018 report relaying Star Tribune figures said the ELCA in Minnesota had lost almost 200,000 members since 2000, and that a third of its remaining 1,050 churches had fewer than 50 members. Catholic membership had held steady, but the number of Catholic churches fell from 720 to 639 ([Patch, July 8, 2018](https://patch.com/minnesota/edenprairie/more-mainline-churches-decline-minnesota)). Rural Catholic parishes have merged too. When six parishes around Czech-heritage New Prague were combined under St. Wenceslaus in a 2010 plan, one parishioner said "it just feels like we don't have a home" ([The Catholic Spirit](https://thecatholicspirit.com/featured/new-prague-area-catholics-not-surprised-but-many-still-sad-about-merger-announcement/)).

**Old parishes, new members.** In January 2026, the pastor of Ascension Catholic Church in north Minneapolis said his parish was "founded by German and Irish immigrants" and has been "sustained by Mexican and Latin American immigrants." He spoke at an interfaith gathering after a federal immigration agent shot and killed Renee Nicole Macklin Good, 37, in south Minneapolis on Jan. 7 ([America magazine/OSV News, Jan. 9, 2026](https://www.americamagazine.org/news/2026/01/09/minneapolis-ice-shooting-catholic/)). On Feb. 27, 2026, Archbishop Bernard Hebda and three cardinals celebrated a Mass in solidarity with migrants in St. Paul ([AP via KSAT, Feb. 27, 2026](https://www.ksat.com/news/politics/2026/02/27/in-minnesota-us-cardinals-and-popes-ambassador-decry-mass-deportations-and-call-for-reconciliation/)).

**Other faiths and none.** Some European-heritage Minnesotans are Orthodox Christians, Jewish, Mennonite, Baptist or Covenant, and many practice no religion, as the Pew figures show. The Iron Range's Catholic, Orthodox and Jewish immigrants drew suspicion from community leaders, and local Ku Klux Klan chapters formed in the 1920s against Catholic and Jewish immigrants ([MNopedia, Immigration to the Iron Range](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/immigration-iron-range-1880-1930)). A surname or heritage does not tell anyone a person's faith.

## 7. Family, home and daily life

**Households and help.** Immigrant families leaned on mutual aid. The Daughters of Norway, founded in Minneapolis in 1897, served single working women, most of them domestic servants, cooks, seamstresses and laundresses. Lodges let young women "speak their native tongue and enjoy themselves socially," as one St. Paul lodge history put it. The group peaked at 5,553 members in 72 lodges in 1927 and merged into Sons of Norway in 1950 ([MNopedia, Daughters of Norway](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/daughters-norway)). New arrivals in Swede Hollow sometimes came with notes pinned to their shirts to guide neighbors to their relatives ([MNopedia, Swede Hollow](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/swede-hollow)).

**Food.** Lefse, lutefisk dinners, kolacky, Polish cookbooks and Italian "red sauce" cafes all have documented Minnesota histories. The Polanie Club's *Treasured Polish Recipes for Americans* stayed in print for 50 years ([MNopedia, Polish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/polish-immigration-minnesota)). Italians sold fruit, vegetables and ice cream in St. Paul from the 1850s, and the Italian Macaroni and Vermicelli Company was formed in 1889 ([Heavy Table](https://heavytable.com/timeline-italian-food-minnesota/)). At Mindekirken's Syttende Mai, hot dogs and ice cream are free for children ([The Alley](https://alleynews.org/2026/05/join-mindekirken-for-syttende-mai/)). The Norway House brunch serves salmon, eggs, waffles and a cream cake decorated with the Norwegian flag ([St. Olaf calendar](https://wp.stolaf.edu/calendar/event/syttende-mai-celebration-at-the-norway-house/)).

**Dress and handwork.** People wear bunads (Norwegian national costume) and Norwegian sweaters at Syttende Mai ([The Alley](https://alleynews.org/2026/05/join-mindekirken-for-syttende-mai/)), and Czech kroje at Kolacky Days ([Minnesota Prairie Roots](https://mnprairieroots.com/2025/05/08/montgomery-kolacky-days-royalty-honor-czech-heritage-culture/)). Sons of Norway lodges award cultural medals in rosemaling (decorative painting), Hardanger embroidery, knitting, weaving, wood carving, folk dance, genealogy, language and food ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/sons-norway-cottonwood-county)).

**Generations and memory.** Heritage now often lives in objects and travel rather than language. A Spring Grove woman who spoke Norwegian from childhood did not teach it to her children, but she kept a "Norwegian room" of rosemaling, costumes and genealogy binders, and her granddaughter insisted she come along to Norway because "you talk Norwegian" ([Post Bulletin, 2015](https://www.postbulletin.com/newsmd/video-spring-grove-carries-on-norwegian-heritage)). Spring Grove's Giants of the Earth Heritage Center is collecting photographs of Syttende Mai honorees going back to 1972 ([Giants of the Earth](https://giantsoftheearth.org/)).

**Childhood.** Children lead Syttende Mai parades in Minneapolis ([The Alley](https://alleynews.org/2026/05/join-mindekirken-for-syttende-mai/)). Others go to language camps such as Spring Grove's Norwegian Ridge camp ([City of Spring Grove](https://www.springgrovemn.com/heritage)) or Concordia Language Villages ([Salolampi Foundation](https://www.salolampi.org/)). No Minnesota source opened gave current data on marriage, household size or eldercare that is specific to European heritage.

## 8. Arts, media, sports and culture

**Museums and centers.** The American Swedish Institute in the Turnblad mansion opened its 2026 season Feb. 14 with a textile show by Emelie Röndahl and a traveling exhibition, "Nordic Echoes," of 24 Upper Midwest artists. The exhibition says it challenges the "heritage model" of folklore by stressing that "all tradition is change." CEO Molly Steenson leads the institute ([Yahoo News, 2026 season report](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/american-swedish-institute-reveals-2026-233302470.html)). For 2027 it has announced a show on Swedish trolls and folklore, along with a troll space built with its Svenska Skolan students ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/american-swedish-institute-announces-2027-exhibition-schedul/)). Other heritage sites include Norway House ([Norway House](https://www.norwayhouse.org/)); Moorhead's Hjemkomst Center, which holds a Viking ship replica sailed from Duluth to Bergen in 1982 ([MNopedia, Hjemkomst](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/hjemkomst-ship)) and a replica of the Hopperstad stave church built by Guy Paulson from 1996 to 2001 ([MNopedia, Hopperstad replica](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/structure/hopperstad-stave-church-replica-moorhead); [Historical and Cultural Society of Clay County](https://www.hcscconline.org/)); the Minnesota Discovery Center in Chisholm for Iron Range history ([Minnesota Discovery Center](https://www.mndiscoverycenter.org/)); and New Ulm's 102-foot Hermann Monument. The Hermann Monument was dedicated in 1897 by the Sons of Hermann and named by Congress in 2000 as a symbol of German American contributions ([MNopedia, Hermann Monument](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/structure/hermann-german-monument-new-ulm)).

**Music.** St. Olaf's Christmas Festival, built on F. Melius Christiansen's choral tradition, is recorded each year ([St. Olaf College](https://christmas.stolaf.edu/)). Polka and German bands play New Ulm's Oktoberfest, and the Minnesota Music Hall of Fame in New Ulm opens for the festival ([Visit New Ulm](https://www.newulm.com/2026/08/12/new-ulm-oktoberfest-usa-today-10best-2026/); [Visit New Ulm schedule](https://www.newulm.com/2026/08/20/coming-to-new-ulm-for-oktoberfest-5/)). Irish Arts Minnesota teaches traditional Irish music and dance ([Irish Arts Minnesota](https://irishartsmn.org/)). Polish-born conductor Stanisław Skrowaczewski, who died in 2017, led the Minnesota Orchestra ([MNopedia, Polish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/polish-immigration-minnesota)).

**Writing.** Polish American writers Wiktoria Janda and Monika Krawczyk led the Polanie Club's publishing work ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/polish-immigration-minnesota)). Ann Regan's *Irish in Minnesota* is recommended by Visit Saint Paul as the deeper history ([Visit Saint Paul](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/saint-pauls-irish-history/)).

**Sport.** Scots brought curling. It may have been played at Mapleton as early as 1857, and a St. Paul club formed in 1912 from the Nushka and Capital City clubs became the largest in the country. More than 30 curling clubs were active in Minnesota in 2019 ([MNopedia, Scottish organizations](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/scottish-cultural-organizations-minnesota)). Czech and Slovak Sokols combined gymnastics with culture. The St. Paul Sokol, revived in 1900 in the 1887 C.S.P.S. Hall, built a camp at Pine City in 1926 ([MNopedia, St. Paul Sokol](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/st-paul-sokol)). Iron Range mining companies built parks and recreation centers after 1916, and Range towns went on to field strong athletic teams ([MNopedia, Immigration to the Iron Range](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/immigration-iron-range-1880-1930)).

**Public memory.** The 1925 Norse-American Centennial in the Twin Cities drew President Coolidge. MNopedia notes that it celebrated assimilation and a "Protestant work ethic" more than folk traditions, and its children's chorus turned a human Norwegian flag inside out into the Stars and Stripes ([MNopedia, Norwegian Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/norwegian-immigration-minnesota)).

## 9. Civic and political life

**Officeholders.** Knute Nelson, born in Voss, Norway, in 1843, came to Alexandria in 1871, was elected governor in 1892 and 1894, and resigned in 1895 to serve in the U.S. Senate until 1923 ([Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/mngov/govbio?govid=27)). Rudy Perpich, born in 1928 at Carson Lake near Hibbing, was a Hibbing dentist before taking office as governor in December 1976, and again in January 1983 ([Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/mngov/govbio?govid=41)). His father was a first-generation Croatian immigrant, and he insisted on calling rural Minnesota "Greater Minnesota" rather than "Outstate." A Range writer told the paper that Perpich remains the only Iron Ranger elected governor ([Duluth News Tribune](https://www.duluthnewstribune.com/news/local/remembering-rudy-perpich-the-governor-who-turned-outstate-into-greater-minnesota)). In St. Paul, nine of ten mayors between 1932 and 1972 were Irish, even though Germans outnumbered the Irish there ([Visit Saint Paul](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/saint-pauls-irish-history/)). By the 1890s, Norwegians and Swedes together held outsized weight in state politics ([MNopedia, Norwegian Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/norwegian-immigration-minnesota)).

**Being targeted.** During World War I, the Minnesota Commission of Public Safety watched German Americans and German-language newspapers and urged English-only schools. On Aug. 19, 1918, John Meints, a German American farmer near Luverne, was kidnapped, whipped and tarred and feathered on suspicion of disloyalty. A "Germania" statue was taken down from a St. Paul building that April ([MNopedia, Anti-German Nativism](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/anti-german-nativism-1917-1919)). On the Iron Range, leaders looked down on southern and eastern Europeans, and 1920s Klan chapters targeted Catholic and Jewish immigrants ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/immigration-iron-range-1880-1930)). Italian Americans faced a racial ideology that ranked "Nordic" peoples above them ([MNopedia, Columbus Memorial](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/christopher-columbus-memorial-st-paul)).

**Debates inside the community.** Heritage groups have argued over public memory. Italian American clubs raised $50,000 for the Christopher Columbus statue at the State Capitol, unveiled in 1931 before more than 24,000 people, at a time of anti-Italian bigotry. Some people of Scandinavian heritage objected, naming Leif Erikson as the first European to reach America, and put up a Leif Erikson monument on the Capitol grounds in 1949. MNopedia records that the Columbus memorial erased Native Americans from the story and that protesters tore down the statue on June 10, 2020 ([MNopedia, Columbus Memorial](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/christopher-columbus-memorial-st-paul)). Heritage groups have also debated how much to assimilate. The Germanic-American Institute turned itself from a social club into a nonprofit in 1994 partly because of generational change ([MNopedia, Germanic-American Institute](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/germanic-american-institute)).

**Organizations.** Major heritage organizations include Sons of Norway, the American Swedish Institute, Norway House, the Germanic-American Institute, the Polish American Cultural Institute, the Danish American Center, Irish Arts Minnesota, FinnFest USA, Sokol Minnesota, Finlandia Foundation Northland and county historical societies ([Sons of Norway](https://www.sofn.com/); [PACIM](https://pacim.org/); [Danish American Center](https://www.danishamericancenter.org/); [Finlandia Foundation Northland](https://www.finlandiafoundationnorthland.org/)).

## 10. Education

**Schools built by immigrants.** Swedish immigrants founded Gustavus Adolphus College (1862), Bethel University (1871) and Minnehaha Academy (1913) ([MNopedia, Swedish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/swedish-immigration-minnesota)). Norwegian pastors and farmers from Rice, Dakota and Goodhue counties, led by the Rev. Bernt Julius Muus, the Rev. N.A. Quammen and Harald Thorson, laid the groundwork for St. Olaf College in 1874 ([St. Olaf College catalog](https://www.stolaf.edu/catalog/0304/factsandfigures/history-heritage.html)). Archbishop John Ireland's St. Thomas Aquinas Seminary grew into the University of St. Thomas and the Saint Paul Seminary ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/ireland-john-1838-1918)). Danes at Tyler opened the Danebod Folk School in 1888, and its summer folk meeting drew 500 young Danish Americans in 1946 ([MNopedia, Danebod](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/structure/danebod)). On the Iron Range, public schools and night classes were the center of Americanization, and Hibbing built a $4 million high school in 1923 ([MNopedia, Immigration to the Iron Range](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/immigration-iron-range-1880-1930)).

**Losing languages in school.** Many public and parochial schools taught in German until World War I. The Commission of Public Safety urged English-only classrooms and listed banned German textbooks, Archbishop Ireland and Bishop Joseph Busch pressed Catholic schools to drop German, and a Wabasha County teacher lost her certificate over alleged pro-German remarks ([MNopedia, Anti-German Nativism](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/anti-german-nativism-1917-1919)).

**Learning them again.** When many metro public schools dropped German in the 1990s, the Germanic-American Institute founded the Twin Cities German Immersion School in 2005 ([MNopedia, Germanic-American Institute](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/germanic-american-institute)). It is now a free K-8 public charter school in St. Paul that says it is the largest German immersion school in North America, open to all Minnesota families and offering English support for children who speak other languages at home ([Twin Cities German Immersion School](https://www.tcgis.org/)). Concordia Language Villages, run by Concordia College in Moorhead, offers programs in 14 languages ([Concordia Language Villages](https://www.concordialanguagevillages.org/)), and the Salolampi Foundation supports its Finnish village on Turtle River Lake near Bemidji ([Salolampi Foundation](https://www.salolampi.org/)). Polish families have used the Adam Mickiewicz Saturday School in Minneapolis ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/polish-immigration-minnesota)). Spring Grove runs a Norwegian Ridge language and culture camp with its public school ([City of Spring Grove](https://www.springgrovemn.com/heritage)), and the American Swedish Institute has a Svenska Skolan (Swedish school) ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/american-swedish-institute-announces-2027-exhibition-schedul/)).

**Attainment.** In 2024, 41.8 percent of white non-Hispanic Minnesotans 25 and older held at least a bachelor's degree and 3.2 percent had not finished high school ([ACS C15002H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=C15002H&geo_ids=04000US27)).

## 11. Health and wellbeing, broadly

**Hospitals and care homes with immigrant roots.** Swedish immigrants founded Bethesda Hospital in St. Paul (1883), Swedish Hospital in Minneapolis (1898, now part of Hennepin County Medical Center) and Bethany Home for the Aged in Alexandria (1917). Lutheran Social Service of Minnesota traces its roots to the Vasa Children's Home, which Swedish pastor Eric Norelius started in 1865 ([MNopedia, Swedish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/swedish-immigration-minnesota)). The Polish American Medical Society of Minnesota formed in 2017 to connect Polish health workers ([MNopedia, Polish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/polish-immigration-minnesota)).

**Older and rural.** White non-Hispanic Minnesotans have a median age of 43.6, and people of color make up only 7 percent of Minnesotans 80 and older, so most of Minnesota's oldest residents belong to this group ([ACS B01002H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B01002H&geo_ids=04000US27); [Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/race-ethnicity)). In several rural and Iron Range counties, incomes for this group are lower and poverty higher than in the metro (section 2). Farm families can call the state's 24-hour Farm and Rural Helpline, which a staff member said took about 30,000 calls a year as of 2022. Another staff member said that year that farmer suicides in Minnesota had started to drop as more farmers called, though that was his observation, not a published statistic ([CBS News, March 31, 2022](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/minnesota-hotline-works-to-improve-mental-health-of-small-farmers/)).

**What is not known.** No Minnesota source opened reported health outcomes such as chronic disease, life expectancy or substance use by European ancestry. Statewide health data are usually reported by race, which mixes many heritages and cannot describe any one of them.

## 12. Disability

**Numbers.** In the 2024 ACS, about 543,000 white non-Hispanic Minnesotans reported a disability, 12.7 percent. That is close to the statewide 12.2 percent. The rate rises steeply with age: 4.5 percent of children, 9.9 percent of adults 18 to 64, and 27.4 percent of people 65 and older ([ACS B18101H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B18101H&geo_ids=04000US27); [ACS B18101](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B18101&geo_ids=04000US27)). Because this group is older on average, age alone may explain part of the gap with the state rate. That is an open question, not a finding. These figures come from people's own survey answers and are not diagnoses.

> **Content note:** This passage describes forced commitment and sterilization under Minnesota's eugenics laws and a patient's death after reported abuse at Rochester State Hospital. Readers may skip to the next paragraph.

**Who defined disability here, and what a label did.** Minnesota's history shows that a label could close doors as well as open them. Under a 1917 law, county probate judges could commit people they called "feeble minded" without consent. MNopedia notes that an immigrant who spoke English as a second language and failed an English-language IQ test could be given that label, as could an unmarried mother, and most of those committed were working-class women. A 1925 law allowed sterilization, and at least 2,204 people were sterilized between 1925 and 1945, 77 percent of them women. Minnesota kept no central count, so the true number is uncertain ([MNopedia, Eugenics in Minnesota](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/eugenics-minnesota)). In 1897, Anna Salzer, an Austrian-born widow sent to Rochester State Hospital, died after another patient reported seeing her abused. That patient also noted that every other German-speaking patient on the ward was gone ([MNopedia, Death of Anna Salzer](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/death-anna-salzer-rochester-state-hospital-1897)).

**Families who organized.** Parents in Minneapolis began meeting in 1946. On Sept. 28, 1950, they hosted the first national convention of parents at the Radisson Hotel, where The Arc was formed. They won special education classes in Minneapolis schools 11 years before national law required them, and The Arc later helped negotiate the plan to close Minnesota's state institutions ([The Arc Minnesota](https://arcminnesota.org/about/why-the-arc/history-of-the-arc-in-minnesota/)). Church agencies with European immigrant roots, such as Lutheran Social Service, are part of today's service system ([MNopedia, Swedish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/swedish-immigration-minnesota)).

**Access at heritage events.** Twin Cities Irish Fest describes its grounds as paved and level for wheelchairs and scooters. It also keeps a dark, cool Quiet Space for guests with autism or sensory processing needs ([Visit Saint Paul](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/guide-to-the-irish-fest/)).

**Questions worth asking, not answers.** Do older relatives remember family members sent to Faribault or a state hospital? Does a family prefer a church-affiliated agency, a secular one, or neither? In a small town, is privacy a concern? No Minnesota study opened looked at how European-heritage families as a group understand or talk about disability, and none should be assumed.

## 13. What is changing now

- **Old institutions are reorganizing.** In May 2026, BetterLife announced a definitive agreement to take in Sons of Norway, which would keep its name, lodges, foundation and cultural programs. The deal was expected to close later in summer 2026, pending regulatory approval ([PR Newswire via WOWK](https://digital-release.wowktv.com/business/press-releases/cision/20260506CG52468/betterlife-announces-definitive-agreement-to-partner-with-sons-of-norway-extending-its-presence-to-more-than-400-communities)). The Irish Fair of Minnesota now runs as Twin Cities Irish Fest ([Visit Saint Paul](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/guide-to-the-irish-fest/)).
- **Fewer pews.** ELCA synods in Minnesota lost 13 to 20 percent of baptized members between 2017 and 2024, and in-person worship in the Minneapolis Area Synod fell by almost half ([ELCA data kits](https://cdn.elca.org/cdn/wp-content/uploads/3G_Minneapolis_Area_data_kit.pdf)). About 29 percent of Minnesota adults now claim no religion ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/religious-landscape-study/state/minnesota/)).
- **A smaller share of a changing state.** Non-Hispanic white Minnesotans fell from 83 percent of the population in 2010 to 74 percent ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/race-ethnicity)). Older parishes and neighborhoods built by Europeans are now home to newer immigrants. MNopedia notes that Swede Hollow passed from Swedes to later immigrant groups ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/swede-hollow)), and Ascension parish in north Minneapolis now depends on Latin American members ([America magazine, Jan. 9, 2026](https://www.americamagazine.org/news/2026/01/09/minneapolis-ice-shooting-catholic/)). A Mindekirken member wrote in May 2026 that the church sits on "what was once Native American land" in a neighborhood that now also celebrates Native, Somali and Central and South American residents ([The Alley](https://alleynews.org/2026/05/join-mindekirken-for-syttende-mai/)).
- **Faith leaders and immigration enforcement.** After a federal immigration agent shot and killed Renee Good in south Minneapolis on Jan. 7, 2026, clergy held an interfaith gathering the next day, and Catholic pastors described parishes where families were afraid to leave home ([America magazine/OSV News](https://www.americamagazine.org/news/2026/01/09/minneapolis-ice-shooting-catholic/)). On Feb. 27, Archbishop Hebda and three cardinals celebrated a Mass in solidarity with migrants in St. Paul ([AP via KSAT](https://www.ksat.com/news/politics/2026/02/27/in-minnesota-us-cardinals-and-popes-ambassador-decry-mass-deportations-and-call-for-reconciliation/)).
- **Heritage as choice.** Heritage is becoming something people choose to take part in. Spring Grove's inherited Norwegian dialect is fading ([Post Bulletin](https://www.postbulletin.com/newsmd/video-spring-grove-carries-on-norwegian-heritage)), while immersion schools, language villages and festivals stay busy. The American Swedish Institute's 2026 "Nordic Echoes" show argues that "all tradition is change" ([Yahoo News](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/american-swedish-institute-reveals-2026-233302470.html)). FinnFest brought its national festival to Minneapolis in 2026 and plans a larger one there in 2027 ([FinnFest program guide](https://finnfest.us/wp-content/uploads/2026/08/FinnFest_USA_2026_Attendee_Program_Guide-Update-8.9.26.pdf)).
- **Some events have gone quiet.** No Svenskarnas Dag or Scandinavian Summer Fest after 2018 and no Twin Cities Polish Festival after 2019 could be confirmed. Irish on Grand closed its storefront ([Visit Saint Paul](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/irish-bars-restaurants/)).

## 14. Strengths and contributions

- **Cooperatives and mutual aid.** Danish co-op creameries became a model that made Minnesota home to nearly half of the nation's cooperative creameries by 1928 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/clarks-grove-cooperative-creamery)). Fraternal societies such as Sons of Norway and the Daughters of Norway gave immigrants insurance and burial help ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/sons-norway-cottonwood-county); [MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/daughters-norway)).
- **Caring institutions.** Lutheran Social Service, which grew from an 1865 Swedish children's home, still serves the whole state, and the former Swedish Hospital is now part of Hennepin County Medical Center ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/swedish-immigration-minnesota)).
- **Labor rights.** Immigrant miners and truckers helped win the eight-hour day and union recognition ([MNopedia, 1916 strike](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/mesabi-iron-range-strike-1916); [MNopedia, 1934 strike](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/minneapolis-teamsters-strike-1934)).
- **Disability rights.** Minneapolis parents founded what became The Arc ([The Arc Minnesota](https://arcminnesota.org/about/why-the-arc/history-of-the-arc-in-minnesota/)).
- **Arts and learning.** Choral music, language villages, immersion schools and museums are open to everyone. The German immersion school and Concordia Language Villages both welcome children with no family tie to the language ([TCGIS](https://www.tcgis.org/); [Concordia Language Villages](https://www.concordialanguagevillages.org/)).
- **Volunteer building.** Robert Asp and his volunteers spent six years building the Hjemkomst, and after his death a crew that included four of his children sailed it to Norway in 1982. The Hopperstad stave church replica was built by Guy Paulson with family and donated help, then given to the City of Moorhead ([MNopedia, Hjemkomst](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/hjemkomst-ship); [MNopedia, Hopperstad replica](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/structure/hopperstad-stave-church-replica-moorhead)).

## What could not be established

- **Income, jobs and health by ancestry.** The Census Bureau does not publish income, poverty, occupation or disability by ancestry in the tables opened, so section 2 and section 12 use the white non-Hispanic group. That group includes people who are not of European descent, and it leaves out people of European descent who mark more than one race.
- **State Demographic Center figures.** The Minnesota State Demographic Center's ancestry pages on mn.gov blocked access, so its 2018 ancestry figures could not be checked. Newer 2024 Census figures are used instead.
- **Current status of some festivals.** No 2019 or later listing was found for Svenskarnas Dag or the Scandinavian Summer Fest, and no 2020 or later listing for the Twin Cities Polish Festival. No organizer's page was found for 2026 Kolacky Days, Bavarian Blast, Dožínky or St. Lucia events. Explore Minnesota, which lists many of these, blocked access.
- **Blocked or unavailable sites.** The American Swedish Institute, Mindekirken, Twin Cities Irish Fest (repeatedly unavailable), Sokol Minnesota, Celtic Junction, the Center for Irish Music, Gammelgården Museum, the Polish Museum in Winona, Lutheran Social Service, Finlandia Foundation National, Olsen Fish Company, The Norwegian American, Gustavus Adolphus College and University of Minnesota autism surveillance pages could not be opened. Facts that depended on them are left out or attributed to other outlets.
- **Chambers and professional networks.** No current Minnesota Norwegian, Swedish, German, Italian or Polish chamber of commerce could be confirmed.
- **Smaller heritages.** Little current Minnesota material was found for Dutch, Luxembourger, Belgian, Icelandic, Welsh, Greek, Slovene, Croatian or Lithuanian community life beyond Census counts and history. The St. Mary's Greek Orthodox festival date given on the existing page could not be confirmed.
- **Family life data.** No Minnesota source opened gave figures on household size, multigenerational living, marriage or eldercare specific to European heritage.
- **Health outcomes.** No Minnesota source reported health outcomes by European ancestry.
