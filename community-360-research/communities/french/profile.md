# French-Heritage and French-Speaking Communities in Minnesota
Checked: October 31, 2026

This page covers three groups that overlap but are not the same. The largest is Minnesotans whose families came, often many generations ago, from French Canada or France. A much smaller group is recent arrivals from France, Belgium, Switzerland, Quebec and other parts of Canada. A third group speaks French because of where they or their parents grew up, in Haiti or in French-speaking countries of West and Central Africa, where French is often a school and government language alongside another home language. Each group has its own history, and the evidence for each is very different in size and quality.

## 1. People and place

**How many claim French roots.** The American Community Survey asks people to name their ancestry. In the 2025 one-year survey, 127,624 Minnesotans (give or take 6,530) named French ancestry, other than Basque, and 34,940 (give or take 3,878) named French Canadian. Another 12,303 named Belgian, 14,922 Swiss, 6,130 Canadian and 2,167 Haitian, the last with a wide margin of 1,066 ([U.S. Census Bureau, ACS 2025, table B04006, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT1Y2025.B04006?g=040XX00US27)). People may give two ancestries, so these counts overlap and cannot simply be added. The steadier five-year survey for 2020 to 2024 gives 150,045 French and 41,490 French Canadian ([ACS 2020-2024, B04006](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT5Y2024.B04006?g=040XX00US27)).

**Fewer people name it each decade.** In the 2006 to 2010 survey, 214,073 Minnesotans named French ancestry and 54,739 French Canadian ([ACS 2006-2010, B04006](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT5Y2010.B04006?g=040XX00US27)). By 2011 to 2015 the counts were 184,336 and 53,052 ([ACS 2011-2015, B04006](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT5Y2015.B04006?g=040XX00US27)). Ancestry is self-reported, and the survey does not say why fewer people now name a French line. Over the same years, people naming Haitian ancestry rose from about 710 to about 2,783, though the margins on those small numbers are wide.

**Mostly born here, and older.** Among Minnesotans of French ancestry in 2025, only about 1,072 were born outside the United States. Their median age was 52.7, against 39.5 for all Minnesotans, and 29.6 percent were 65 or older, against 19.1 percent statewide ([ACS 2025, Selected Population Profile S0201, French ancestry](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSSPP1Y2025.S0201?g=040XX00US27&t=533)). The Census Bureau's detailed Minnesota profile was available for French ancestry but not for French Canadian or Haitian ancestry.

**Born in a French-speaking country.** The 2020 to 2024 survey counts about 1,626 Minnesotans born in France (give or take 397), 260 in Belgium, 485 in Switzerland and 11,885 in Canada. From French-speaking Africa and the Caribbean it counts about 4,077 born in Cameroon, 1,731 in Togo, 1,653 in Haiti, 1,270 in Morocco, 1,253 in Ivory Coast, 880 in the Democratic Republic of Congo, 411 in the Republic of Congo, 411 in Algeria and 203 in Senegal ([ACS 2020-2024, B05006](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT5Y2024.B05006?g=040XX00US27)). Several of these margins are large relative to the estimate. Birthplace is not language: Canada is mostly English-speaking, and Cameroon has both English- and French-speaking regions. Sahan Journal reported that Minnesota's Cameroon-born population grew from about 1,400 in 2016 to about 3,600 in 2022, and tied part of the growth to the conflict that began in Cameroon's English-speaking regions in 2016 ([Sahan Journal, June 5, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/minnesota-cameroonian-community-more-than-doubles/)). Sahan Journal and MPR News have both cited an estimate of about 4,000 Haitians in Minnesota ([Sahan Journal, July 2, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/supreme-court-ends-tps-haiti-minnesota/); [MPR News, July 16, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/07/16/minnesota-haitians-worry-about-what-comes-as-next-as-tps-protections-end)).

**Who speaks French at home.** About 16,918 Minnesotans age 5 and older speak French (including Cajun) at home, and 4,906 of them speak English less than "very well." Another 1,914 speak Haitian Creole at home ([ACS 2020-2024, B16001](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT5Y2024.B16001?g=040XX00US27)). The 2025 one-year survey, which groups the two, counts 19,643 speakers of "French, Haitian or Cajun," with a margin of 4,986 ([ACS 2025, C16001](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT1Y2025.C16001?g=040XX00US27)). Only 2.6 percent of Minnesotans of French ancestry speak a language other than English at home ([ACS 2025, S0201](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSSPP1Y2025.S0201?g=040XX00US27&t=533)). In other words, most people of French descent speak English at home, and many people who speak French at home are not of French descent.

**Where.** In the 2020 to 2024 survey, the largest numbers of people naming French ancestry live in Hennepin (34,876), Ramsey (14,350), Dakota (11,314), Anoka (10,132) and Washington (9,632) counties. As a share of the population, the French imprint is strongest in the northwest. In Red Lake County, 14.6 percent of residents name French ancestry and another 4.5 percent French Canadian. Marshall, Polk and Pennington counties follow, at about 5 to 6 percent French ([ACS 2020-2024, B04006 by county](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSDT5Y2024.B04006?g=040XX00US27)). Place names carry the history: Little Canada in Ramsey County; Gentilly, Terrebonne, Huot, Lambert and Lafontaine in the Polk and Red Lake County area ([Papen, Canadian Journal of Applied Linguistics, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/CJAL/article/download/19766/21474/26177)); and Faribault, named for Alexander Faribault, son of a Quebec-born fur trader ([MNHS Historic Sibley Site](https://www.mnhs.org/sibley/learn/jean-baptiste-faribault)).

**How they came: the fur trade.** MNopedia describes the fur trade that tied this region to Montreal from the 1600s to the 1850s. It was built on alliances with Dakota and Ojibwe nations and later on the North West Company's depot at Grand Portage. MNopedia says most of the voyageurs who paddled the trade goods were French Canadians "from impoverished backgrounds," many of whom ended up in debt to company stores ([MNopedia, Fur Trade in Minnesota](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/fur-trade-minnesota)).

**From the Red River to St. Paul.** A 1964 Ramsey County History article traces Benjamin Gervais, born in Rivière-du-Loup, Quebec, in 1786, from fur-trade work near Pembina to the Selkirk colony on the Red River, then to the Fort Snelling area in 1827. When civilians were evicted from the fort's reservation in 1840, the Gervais family bought the claim of Pierre "Pig's Eye" Parrant for $10. In 1841 Gervais and Vital Guerin gave Father Lucien Galtier land for the log chapel he named for St. Paul ([Ramsey County History, Fall 1964](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Fall1964_Wall.pdf)). In 1844 Gervais claimed land north of the settlement, built the area's first commercial grist mill and founded what became Little Canada. A school opened there in 1850 with classes taught in French ([City of Little Canada, Little Canada's History](https://www.canadiandays.org/284/Little-Canadas-History); [Gervais Grist Mill Site](https://www.canadiandays.org/262/Gervais-Grist-Mill-Site)). The same 1964 article says the school joined a public district three years later and hired an English-speaking teacher to receive public funds.

**Farms in the northwest.** Linguist Robert Papen writes that Gentilly and Red Lake Falls were founded between 1873 and 1876 by families from Manitoba and St. Paul, after an 1863 treaty opened the region to farming. Railroad agents then recruited more French Canadian families, and largely French-speaking settlements grew up in what were then Polk County and later Polk and Red Lake counties ([Papen, CJAL](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/CJAL/article/download/19766/21474/26177)).

**A Midwest French press.** The Minnesota Historical Society says that by 1895 there were 70,000 French Canadian immigrants in Minnesota and neighboring states. The Minneapolis weekly *Echo de l'Ouest*, founded in 1883, reported 5,000 subscribers in 1896 and published until January 4, 1929. MNHS counts 12 French-language newspapers published in Minnesota ([MNHS, Echo de l'Ouest](https://www.mnhs.org/newspapers/hub/echo-de-louest)).

**Generations and language loss.** As early as the 1880s, *Echo de l'Ouest* complained about families who anglicized names, such as Dubois to Wood. Around 1900 an editorial gave three reasons French was fading: jobs required English, politics ran in English, and educated French speakers stayed in Quebec ([MNHS](https://www.mnhs.org/newspapers/hub/echo-de-louest)). Papen reports a 1980 study in Red Lake Falls in which 70 percent of 39 people interviewed said French was their first language. All of them were 50 or older; everyone younger named English ([Papen, CJAL](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/CJAL/article/download/19766/21474/26177)).

**Newer French speakers.** Today's French-speaking newcomers include people who move for work or study, and a volunteer group called Minnesota Accueil that welcomes French speakers, runs family groups and organizes picnics ([Minnesota Accueil, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.minnesotaaccueil.com/actualites.html)). They also include people from Haiti, Togo, Senegal, Congo, Cameroon and other countries, many of whom use another language at home. The Senegalese Association of Minnesota, for example, teaches Wolof to children ([Senegalese Association of Minnesota](https://senegalesemn.com/)). A Togolese language workshop at the Alliance Française in 2024 covered French, Ewe, Kabye and Aja ([Alliance Française, Mois de la Francophonie 2024](https://www.afmsp.org/mois-de-la-francophonie-2024)).

## 2. All walks of life

Unless noted, figures here describe Minnesotans of French ancestry in the 2025 survey, compared with all Minnesotans ([ACS 2025, S0201, French ancestry](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSSPP1Y2025.S0201?g=040XX00US27&t=533)). No comparable profile exists for French Canadian, Haitian or French-speaking African Minnesotans.

- **Well-off households.** Median household income was $96,186 (give or take $6,952), close to the state's $92,297. Median family income was $129,489. Per-person income was $62,137, against $50,187 statewide. Households are small, 2.14 people on average.
- **Professionals and owners.** Among adults 25 and older, 41.4 percent hold a bachelor's degree or more, about the state rate of 40.8 percent, and 14.2 percent hold a graduate or professional degree. Half of employed people (49.9 percent) work in management, business, science and the arts, and 6.1 percent are self-employed in an unincorporated business, slightly above the state's 5.1 percent. Owner-occupied homes make up 75.3 percent of households, and the median home value is $373,400.
- **Working and middle-income families.** One in five workers (20.5 percent) is in sales and office jobs, 16.0 percent in service jobs, 8.2 percent in production and transportation, and 5.3 percent in construction and maintenance. The labor force share, 61.3 percent, is below the state's 67.4 percent, which fits an older group; 39.0 percent of households receive Social Security.
- **People in poverty or under strain.** Seven percent of people live below the poverty line (give or take 1.3 points), against 9.0 percent statewide. Among single people living alone or with non-relatives, 18.7 percent are poor. Families headed by a woman with no spouse have a poverty rate of 14.2 percent, with a wide margin. Among renters, 54.0 percent spend 30 percent or more of income on rent, more than the state's 48.7 percent. About 5.6 percent of households use SNAP food benefits.

**Across history.** MNopedia's account of the fur trade shows wide gaps between traders and paddlers. Jean-Baptiste Faribault, born in Quebec, built a three-story stone house at Mendota in 1839 and 1840 for $5,000; MNHS also records that he bought an enslaved woman in 1833 ([MNHS, Historic Sibley Site](https://www.mnhs.org/sibley/learn/jean-baptiste-faribault)). By contrast, MNopedia describes voyageurs who were still paddling, deep in debt, twenty years after signing on, and some indentured servants who received no pay ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/fur-trade-minnesota)). Around 1900, some French Canadians who came to build the State Capitol were skilled stone carvers from mobile family trades ([Université de Saint-Boniface, Frenette and Willis, 2018](https://migrationsfrancophones.ustboniface.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Vignette1_Capitol_18mars18.pdf)).

**Newer arrivals in every class.** Among Cameroonian Minnesotans quoted by Sahan Journal in 2024 were an accounting manager, a medical laboratory supervisor at M Health Fairview, and a University of Minnesota School of Public Health professor, alongside new arrivals helping run a family grocery ([Sahan Journal, June 5, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/minnesota-cameroonian-community-more-than-doubles/)). Haitian Minnesotans include Rose Mingo-Holtz, a U.S. citizen who founded and directs Mes Amis French School ([MPR News, July 16, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/07/16/minnesota-haitians-worry-about-what-comes-as-next-as-tps-protections-end)). They also include workers whose permission to work depended on Temporary Protected Status. In 2025, David Policard of the nonprofit Vanse told Sahan Journal that "so many people" had already been let go from jobs, and that some were considering cash jobs and rented rooms ([Sahan Journal, July 2, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/tps-ends-haiti-minnesota-deportation/)).

## 3. Work and the economy

**The first economy: furs.** For more than a century the fur trade was the region's main business. Montreal men carried goods 1,500 miles each way to Grand Portage, and "north men" wintered inland, earning the name *hivernants*, or winterers ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/fur-trade-minnesota)). Some former traders became territorial officials and land agents; MNopedia notes that some used kinship ties to negotiate the 1850s land cessions.

**Farming, milling and town building.** French Canadian settlers ran farms and the grist mill at Little Canada ([City of Little Canada](https://www.canadiandays.org/262/Gervais-Grist-Mill-Site)) and farmed the Red River Valley after the 1870s ([Papen](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/CJAL/article/download/19766/21474/26177)). *Echo de l'Ouest*'s founder, Zéphirin Demeules, worked for riverboat captain and merchant Louis Robert, ran general and grocery stores in Osseo and Minneapolis, and printed ads favoring businesses owned by fellow French Canadians ([MNHS](https://www.mnhs.org/newspapers/hub/echo-de-louest)).

**Skilled trades.** Between 1895 and 1905, a little over thirty French Canadian workers helped build the Minnesota State Capitol, by the English text of a 2018 Université de Saint-Boniface summary; its French text says about forty. About twenty were stone carvers, two-thirds were born in Quebec, and half settled permanently in the Twin Cities. The summary credits a volunteer project run with the University of Minnesota's Labor Education Service, "Who Built Our Capitol?" ([Frenette and Willis, 2018](https://migrationsfrancophones.ustboniface.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Vignette1_Capitol_18mars18.pdf)).

**Today.** French-ancestry workers are concentrated in education, health care and social assistance (31.9 percent, against 27.2 percent statewide) and in professional and scientific work (14.6 percent). Fewer work in manufacturing (9.1 percent) than Minnesotans overall (12.7 percent). Unemployment was 4.7 percent in 2025, give or take 1.5 points ([ACS 2025, S0201](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSSPP1Y2025.S0201?g=040XX00US27&t=533)).

**Ties to France and Canada.** The Alliance Française's director has served since 2014 as Honorary Consul of France for Minnesota and the Dakotas. The post handles certificates, signature checks and proxy votes; passports and visas go through the Consulate General in Chicago ([Alliance Française, Honorary Consul of France](https://www.afmsp.org/honorary-consul-of-france)). The Alliance's 2020-2021 directory listed a Minnesota chapter of the French-American Chamber of Commerce, a Belgian honorary consul in St. Paul, the Canadian Consulate General in Minneapolis, and French-speaking lawyers, realtors, financial planners and translators ([Alliance Française, French Resource Directory 2020-2021](https://www.afmsp.org/s/frdtopost.pdf)). The chamber's own site was not reachable on Oct. 31, 2026, so its current activity is not confirmed here.

**Work permits and fear of losing them.** French-speaking Haitian and Cameroonian workers have been affected by the end of Temporary Protected Status (see section 13). Sahan Journal reported in July 2025 that employers were already "nervous" about keeping Haitian TPS holders on the payroll ([Sahan Journal, July 2, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/tps-ends-haiti-minnesota-deportation/)).

## 4. Businesses and commercial life

No source found describes a French business district in Minnesota today. Historic French Canadian settlement left its mark in St. Paul, Northeast Minneapolis around St. Anthony, Little Canada and the northwest farm towns, but the businesses that now carry French culture are scattered. Among those found, most are French-style restaurants and bakeries, some owned by people born in France and many by people who are not. The full list, with status and evidence, is in businesses.json.

**Bakeries and pastry.** Patisserie 46 in South Minneapolis and Rose Street Patisserie in St. Paul were founded by chef John Kraus and advertise "authentic French" breads and pastries; their site takes Thanksgiving preorders and Galette des Rois orders for early January ([Patisserie 46](https://patisserie46.com/)). Marc Heu Patisserie Paris, at 156 Dale St. N. in St. Paul, is run by Marc and Gaosong Heu. Sahan Journal describes Marc Heu as a French-born Hmong pastry chef raised in French Guiana ([Sahan Journal, 2023](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/marc-heu-patisserie-paris-st-paul-dale-street-hmong-chef/)), and a June 2026 Ramsey County podcast says he trained at École Lenôtre in Paris ([Between 2 Urns, June 26, 2026](https://www.buzzsprout.com/2614919/episodes/19406714-between-2-urns-marc-heu)). Patrick's Bakery & Café ("We bring France to you!") has locations in Richfield, Edina and Maple Grove ([Patrick's Bakery & Café](https://patricksbakerycafe.com/)). Trung Nam French Bakery on University Avenue in St. Paul is a longtime Vietnamese French bakery ([Counselor Realty, Jan. 22, 2026](https://vsiskin.counselorrealty.com/blog/2026/01/22/wintertime-cafes-and-bakeries-locals-love-around-the-twin-cities)).

**Restaurants.** Bûcheron, from Adam Ritter and Jeanie Janas Ritter, won the James Beard Award for Best New Restaurant in 2025; Mpls.St.Paul Magazine calls its menu "just as French as it is Minnesotan" ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, 2026](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/the-best-restaurants-in-the-twin-cities-2026/)). Thérèse, a French bistro at 50th and France in Edina, is from Argentine-born chef Daniel del Prado, who calls it an homage to 1980s London French restaurants ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/restaurant-review-therese-french-arrival/)). Oh Crêpe! on France Avenue serves Breton buckwheat galettes and crêpes, with fall 2026 menus posted ([Oh Crêpe!](https://www.ohcrepemn.com/)). Barbette, which calls itself an organic French brasserie, posts current hours at 1600 W. Lake St. in Minneapolis ([Barbette](https://www.barbette.com/)). Salut Bar Américain in St. Paul is permanently closed ([Salut](https://www.salutbaramericain.com/)).

**Food made at home and to order.** Valerie's French Kitchen is a personal chef service run by a native of France ([Valerie's French Kitchen](https://valeriesfrenchkitchen.com/)); its owner catered the French-American Heritage Foundation's 10th-anniversary event ([Church of St. Louis, King of France](https://stlouiskingoffrance.org/calendar/come-celebrate-the-french-american-heritage-foundations-10-year-anniversary/)).

**Francophone African and Haitian businesses.** Sahan Journal described Cameroonian hairdressers and groceries, including Bali African Market in New Hope, in 2024 ([Sahan Journal, June 5, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/minnesota-cameroonian-community-more-than-doubles/)). No current page confirming a Haitian, Senegalese or Congolese restaurant in Minnesota was found.

**Schools as businesses.** Mes Amis French School in the Hopkins and Minnetonka area runs French immersion preschool and after-school programs ([Mes Amis](https://mesamisfrenchschool.org/)).

**Directories and media.** The Alliance Française has published a French Resource Directory; its 2020-2021 edition was the eighth, and the Alliance was taking listings for a 2026 edition ([Alliance Française](https://www.afmsp.org/french-resource-directory)). Minnesota Accueil keeps a French-language list of addresses and professionals for newcomers ([Minnesota Accueil](https://www.minnesotaaccueil.com/)).

## 5. Religion and spiritual life

**A Catholic foundation.** St. Paul's first Catholic chapel was built for French Canadian families. Father Lucien Galtier said Mass in the homes of French Catholic families before Benjamin Gervais and Vital Guerin gave land in 1841 for the log chapel he named for St. Paul ([Ramsey County History, Fall 1964](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Fall1964_Wall.pdf)). The Archdiocese of Saint Paul and Minneapolis says Joseph Crétin, born in France in 1799, was named the first bishop of St. Paul on July 19, 1850, and ordained bishop on January 26, 1851. It says he worked to bring Catholic immigrants from Europe to settle the region ([Archdiocese, Crétin biography](https://www.archspm.org/past-bishops/most-reverend-joseph-cretin/); [Archdiocese, 175 years](https://www.archspm.org/from-pigs-eye-to-saint-paul-175-years-of-the-catholic-church-in-minnesota/)).

**French parishes that still stand.**
- *Church of St. Louis, King of France*, 506 Cedar St., St. Paul, was founded in 1868 as the city's "first exclusive French church." Its school, École St. Louis, opened in 1873. The present church, finished in 1909, was designed by Emmanuel Masqueray while he was also designing the Cathedral of Saint Paul, and its windows reflect "French, French-Canadian and American history" ([Church of St. Louis, King of France, History](https://stlouiskingoffrance.org/our-church/history-of-our-church/)). The archdiocese lists its Masses in English today ([Archdiocese listing](https://www.archspm.org/location/st-louis-king-of-france-catholic-church/)). The parish calendar still hosts French heritage events, such as the French-American Heritage Foundation's 10th-anniversary gathering ([St. Louis, King of France calendar](https://stlouiskingoffrance.org/calendar/come-celebrate-the-french-american-heritage-foundations-10-year-anniversary/)).
- *Our Lady of Lourdes*, 1 Lourdes Place, Minneapolis, was bought in 1877 by the French Canadian Catholic community, which had been part of St. Anthony of Padua. The archdiocese calls it the oldest continuously used church in Minneapolis and the first U.S. parish named for Our Lady of Lourdes; its listed Masses are in English ([Archdiocese, Our Lady of Lourdes](https://www.archspm.org/location/our-lady-of-lourdes-catholic-church/)). In 1887 *Echo de l'Ouest* urged parents to send children to the parish school, where French was required for admission ([MNHS](https://www.mnhs.org/newspapers/hub/echo-de-louest)).
- *St. John the Evangelist* in Little Canada was built in 1852 and is a Ramsey County historic site ([City of Little Canada](https://www.canadiandays.org/267/St-John-the-Evangelist-Catholic-Church)). A 1964 local history tells of a settler who refused to pay pew rent there and carried his own folding chair to Mass every Sunday ([Ramsey County History](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Fall1964_Wall.pdf)).

**Newer French speakers, many faiths.** No current French-language Catholic Mass in the Twin Cities was confirmed in any source found. Francophone newcomers bring a range of faiths. The Senegalese Association of Minnesota's president opens his welcome with "Asalaam Malekum" ([Senegalese Association of Minnesota](https://senegalesemn.com/)). Catholic parishes in Maple Grove, New Brighton and Minneapolis have long-running partnerships with parishes and communities in Haiti ([Central Minnesota Catholic, 2021](https://thecentralminnesotacatholic.org/minnesota-parishes-relief-organizations-mobilize-to-support-haiti/)). The Alliance Française's 2020-2021 directory listed a French-language congregation of Jehovah's Witnesses in Minneapolis ([Alliance Française directory](https://www.afmsp.org/s/frdtopost.pdf)); its current status was not confirmed. No source was found on how many French-heritage Minnesotans today are Catholic, Protestant, of another faith or of no faith.

## 6. Celebrations and observances

These dates appear in calendar-entries.json with their sources.

- **Bastille Day (July 14).** The Alliance Française's annual celebration in 2026 ran 5 to 8 p.m. on Tuesday, July 14, with live music, French food and games ([Alliance Française, Bastille Day 2026](https://www.afmsp.org/bastille-day-2026-afmsp)).
- **Canadian Days, Little Canada.** The city's festival has been held every year since 1977 ([Canadian Days, About Us](https://www.canadiandays.org/433/About-Us)). The 2026 festival opened July 31 at Spooner Park ([City of Little Canada, Oct. 22, 2026](https://www.canadiandays.org/m/newsflash/home/detail/653)), under the theme "Where 50 Years Feels Like Home" ([Photo Gallery](https://www.canadiandays.org/449/Photo-Gallery)). The 2022 theme was "Let's Rendezvous in '22!"
- **Mois de la Francophonie (March).** Each March the Alliance Française features one French-speaking country: Togo in 2024, Laos in 2025 and Tunisia in 2026 ([Alliance Française, 2026](https://www.afmsp.org/mois-de-la-francophonie-2026); [2024](https://www.afmsp.org/mois-de-la-francophonie-2024)).
- **Voyageur and fur-trade days.** The Minnesota Historical Society's Snake River Fur Post near Pine City held its Winter Frolic on Jan. 24, 2026, and a new Voyageur Day on July 18, 2026, both with reenactors from La Compagnie des Hivernants de la Rivière Saint Pierre ([MNHS, Dec. 18, 2025](https://www.mnhs.org/media/news/winter-frolic-2026-snake-river-fur-post); [MNHS, June 29, 2026](https://www.mnhs.org/media/news/snake-river-fur-post-introduce-new-summer-event)).
- **Breton Fest Noz.** Minnesota Accueil announced the sixth Breizh Fest, celebrating Brittany with dancing, galettes, crêpes and cider, for June 5, 2026, at Minneapolis Cider Company ([Minnesota Accueil](https://www.minnesotaaccueil.com/actualites.html)).
- **Francophone picnics.** Minnesota Accueil held spring and back-to-school picnics for French speakers on May 31, 2026, at Bryant Lake Park in Eden Prairie and Aug. 30, 2026, at Kenwood Park in Minneapolis ([Minnesota Accueil](https://www.minnesotaaccueil.com/actualites.html)).
- **French film.** MSP Film's annual Lumières Françaises series ran July 31 to Aug. 6, 2026, at The Main Cinema ([MSP Film](https://mspfilm.org/series/lumieres-francaises/)).
- **Haitian Independence Day (January 1) and Ancestors' Day (January 2).** The Embassy of Haiti describes Jan. 1, 1804, as the founding of "the first black republic in the world," celebrated with soup joumou ([Embassy of Haiti](https://www.haiti.org/celebration-of-haiti-s-212th-year-of-independence/)). No Minnesota public event was found for 2026.
- **Haitian Flag Day (May 18).** The Embassy of Haiti dates the flag to May 18, 1803 ([Embassy of Haiti](https://www.haiti.org/national-haitian-flag-day-celebration-2015-activities-in-the-u-s/)). Afoutayi held a Minnesota Flag Day celebration on May 17, 2025, at New Branches Church in Minneapolis ([DanceMN](https://dancemn.org/submissions/afoutayi-ayiti-haiti-flag-day-celebration/)). The group announced it was moving its work to Toronto by summer 2026 ([Afoutayi](https://www.afoutayidmaco.com/)).
- **Haiti earthquake remembrance (January 12).** The 2010 earthquake struck on Jan. 12, 2010 ([U.S. Geological Survey](https://earthquake.usgs.gov/fdsnws/event/1/query?eventid=usp000h60h&format=geojson)). Many Haitian Minnesotans' Temporary Protected Status dated from it ([Sahan Journal, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/tps-ends-haiti-minnesota-deportation/)). No Minnesota remembrance event was found.
- **Fête nationale du Québec (Saint-Jean-Baptiste, June 24).** Quebec celebrates on June 23 and 24 ([Gouvernement du Québec](https://www.quebec.ca/culture/fete-nationale)). In 1887 *Echo de l'Ouest*'s editor addressed a Société St. Jean Baptiste in Duluth ([MNHS](https://www.mnhs.org/newspapers/hub/echo-de-louest)), but no current Minnesota celebration was found.
- **Galette des Rois (Epiphany).** Patisserie 46 and Rose Street Patisserie take orders for the king cake for pickup in early January ([Patisserie 46](https://patisserie46.com/)).
- **Other community gatherings.** The Cameroonian community's biggest U.S. gathering has been an August soccer tournament in the Twin Cities, hosted by the Minnesota Cameroon Community ([Sahan Journal, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/minnesota-cameroonian-community-more-than-doubles/)). The Senegalese Association of Minnesota describes an annual cultural festival of more than 800 people ([Senegalese Association](https://senegalesemn.com/)). Dates for 2026 were not found.
- **Christian holidays.** Easter and Christmas are kept on the calendar, but no Minnesota celebration of either specific to French-heritage or French-speaking communities was found.

## 7. Family, home and daily life

**Households.** Minnesotans of French ancestry live in smaller and older households than the state as a whole: 2.14 people on average, against 2.38. Half of households (50.5 percent) are married couples, and 18.6 percent have the householder's own children under 18, against 25.7 percent statewide. Only 1.3 percent of adults 30 and older live with grandchildren ([ACS 2025, S0201](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSSPP1Y2025.S0201?g=040XX00US27&t=533)). These figures describe ancestry, not any family's customs.

**Family names and lineage.** Ramsey County's 1964 history lists Little Canada mailboxes bearing Gervais, Nadeau, Ducharme, Donais, Bibeau, Auge, Garceau, Tereau and Melancon ([Ramsey County History](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Fall1964_Wall.pdf)). The Little Canada Historical Society specializes in French-Canadian genealogy, and a fifth great-grandson of Benjamin and Genevieve Gervais has presented his research on the founding family ([City of Little Canada, Historical Society](https://www.canadiandays.org/244/Historical-Society)). Family research now has a new practical side: a Canadian law in force since Dec. 15, 2025, lets some descendants of Canadians born abroad claim Canadian citizenship, and an Oct. 2026 CIC News article urges Minnesotans with French Canadian roots to check their records ([CIC News, Oct. 7, 2026](https://www.cicnews.com/2026/10/generations-of-minnesotans-may-now-be-canadian-citizens-through-their-ancestry-and-not-know-it-yet-1081081.html)). CIC News is published by an immigration consultancy.

**Language in the household.** Bottineau's children reportedly spoke only French when they started school in St. Anthony in the 1840s ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/respected-guide-hunter-pierre-bottineaus-legacy-in-northeast/)). By 1980 in Red Lake Falls, French remained a first language only among people 50 and older ([Papen](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/CJAL/article/download/19766/21474/26177)). Several groups support parents who want to pass on French or another home language. Minnesota Accueil runs a family group called Bons Plans Familles Minnesota ([Minnesota Accueil](https://www.minnesotaaccueil.com/actualites.html)); Les Petits Livres mails French children's books and comics to families ([Les Petits Livres](https://www.lespetitslivres.com/)); and the Senegalese Association teaches Wolof so children "stay connected to their roots" ([Senegalese Association](https://senegalesemn.com/)). Cameroonian Minnesotan Modoh Wanda told Sahan Journal that as a child, family gatherings of her Bamileke community were "how we kept the culture" ([Sahan Journal, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/minnesota-cameroonian-community-more-than-doubles/)).

**Food.** Sources document crêpes and galettes from Brittany ([Oh Crêpe!](https://www.ohcrepemn.com/)), king cake at Epiphany ([Patisserie 46](https://patisserie46.com/)), Haitian soup joumou on Independence Day ([Embassy of Haiti](https://www.haiti.org/celebration-of-haiti-s-212th-year-of-independence/)), and Togolese fonio couscous and Réunion Island cari taught at the Alliance Française ([Alliance Française, 2024](https://www.afmsp.org/mois-de-la-francophonie-2024)). No Minnesota source was found describing French Canadian home cooking today.

**Aging.** Almost three in ten French-ancestry Minnesotans are 65 or older. Among those 65 and older, 4.6 percent live in poverty, lower than the state's 8.1 percent for that age group ([ACS 2025, S0201](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSSPP1Y2025.S0201?g=040XX00US27&t=533)).

## 8. Arts, media, sports and culture

**Cultural centers.** The Alliance Française Mpls/St Paul, at 227 Colfax Ave. N. in Minneapolis, began in the 1920s under University of Minnesota professor Jacques Fermand, incorporated on Oct. 29, 1975, and reports more than 1,200 members and over 900 students a year. It calls itself the sixth-largest Alliance in the United States and serves the Twin Cities and Rochester ([Alliance Française, Mission & History](https://www.afmsp.org/mission); [AFMSP50](https://www.afmsp.org/afmsp50)). Its program includes film clubs, book groups, a gallery, cooking classes and monthly conversation gatherings. Recent guests have included Togolese author Edem Awumey and Togolese musicians ([Alliance Française, 2024](https://www.afmsp.org/mois-de-la-francophonie-2024)). It also houses the Minneapolis-Tours Sister Cities committee, which marked the cities' 35th anniversary ([Minneapolis Tours Sister Cities](https://www.afmsp.org/mplstours-sistercities)).

**Heritage and reenactment.** The French-American Heritage Foundation, led in 2018 by president Mark Labine, compiled the books *They Spoke French* and *In the Beginning, There Was a Chapel* and gives talks on French settlement ([Dakota County Historical Society flyer, 2018](https://www.mendotadakota.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/French-in-Mendota-Flyer.pdf)). Its 10th-anniversary event offered French-Canadian music and watercolors of 25 French heritage sites in Minnesota ([St. Louis, King of France](https://stlouiskingoffrance.org/calendar/come-celebrate-the-french-american-heritage-foundations-10-year-anniversary/)). The foundation's own website could not be reached, so its current programs are not described here. La Compagnie des Hivernants de la Rivière Saint Pierre portrays voyageur life at MNHS events ([MNHS, 2026](https://www.mnhs.org/media/news/snake-river-fur-post-introduce-new-summer-event)). An earlier group, La Société Canadienne-Française du Minnesota, founded in 1979 under "Minnesota's Official Voyageur" John Rivard, published more than 125 issues of the newsletter *Chez Nous* and is now inactive ([Chez Nous](https://chez-nous.net/aboutla.html)).

**Haitian arts.** Afoutayi Haitian Dance, Music and Arts Company moved from California to Minnesota in 2014 and taught dance, drumming and Haitian Creole singing in schools, libraries and parks ([Afoutayi](https://www.afoutayidmaco.com/); [Sahan Journal, July 3, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/culture-community/afoutayi-haitian-dance-djenane-saint-juste/)). Founder Djenane Saint Juste published a children's book, *The Mermaid and the Whale*, in English, Haitian Creole, French and Spanish ([Sahan Journal](https://sahanjournal.com/culture-community/afoutayi-haitian-dance-djenane-saint-juste/)). The company announced its move to Toronto by summer 2026 (section 13).

**Media.** Minnesota once had 12 French-language newspapers; *Le Canadien* in St. Paul (1877-1904) and *Echo de l'Ouest* in Minneapolis (1883-1929) ran longest ([MNHS](https://www.mnhs.org/newspapers/hub/echo-de-louest)). Today, *Bonjour Minnesota* on community radio KFAI, on the air since the 1980s, covers music and news of the French-speaking world, according to Minnesota Accueil; KFAI's own page did not load ([Minnesota Accueil, March 16, 2026](https://www.minnesotaaccueil.com/actualites.html)). Minnesota Accueil itself posts French-language news, events and consular notices.

**Film.** MSP Film's 2026 international festival, April 8 to 19, included 12 French-language films, and its annual Lumières Françaises series featured Éric Rohmer in summer 2026 ([Minnesota Accueil](https://www.minnesotaaccueil.com/actualites.html); [MSP Film](https://mspfilm.org/series/lumieres-francaises/)).

**Sports.** The Cameroonian community's annual August soccer tournament draws Cameroonians "from all around the country" ([Sahan Journal, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/minnesota-cameroonian-community-more-than-doubles/)). The Alliance Française planned a 3-on-3 international soccer tournament, now postponed ([Alliance Française](https://www.afmsp.org/events/soccertournament)). The 2020-2021 French Resource Directory listed a Minneapolis pétanque club that had played since 1998 ([directory](https://www.afmsp.org/s/frdtopost.pdf)); it was not reconfirmed.

**Writers and history-keepers.** Linguist Robert Papen and historians Yves Frenette and John Willis have documented French Minnesota from Quebec and Manitoba ([Papen](https://journals.lib.unb.ca/index.php/CJAL/article/download/19766/21474/26177); [Frenette and Willis](https://migrationsfrancophones.ustboniface.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Vignette1_Capitol_18mars18.pdf)). Dick Bernard edited *Chez Nous* ([Chez Nous](https://chez-nous.net/aboutla.html)).

## 9. Civic and political life

**Early public life.** A 1964 county history credits French Canadian pioneers with settling St. Paul, St. Anthony and Little Canada ([Ramsey County History](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Fall1964_Wall.pdf)). *Echo de l'Ouest* was considered Republican, Catholic and "a strong supporter of French-Canadian interests," and published a directory of French Canadians in Minneapolis public affairs ([MNHS](https://www.mnhs.org/newspapers/hub/echo-de-louest)). It also pushed back, in 1893, against Irish Catholic leaders who insisted on English.

**Organizations now.**
- *Alliance Française Mpls/St Paul* (culture and language) and the *Honorary Consul of France* for Minnesota and the Dakotas, Christina Selander Bouzouina, appointed in 2014 ([Alliance Française](https://www.afmsp.org/honorary-consul-of-france)).
- *Consulate General of Canada in Minneapolis*, 701 Fourth Ave. S., serving Canadians in Minnesota, Iowa, Nebraska and the Dakotas, including proof-of-citizenship applications ([Government of Canada](https://www.international.gc.ca/country-pays/us-eu/minneapolis.aspx?lang=eng)).
- *French-American Heritage Foundation* (heritage) and the *Little Canada Historical Society* (genealogy) ([Mendota flyer](https://www.mendotadakota.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/French-in-Mendota-Flyer.pdf); [Little Canada](https://www.canadiandays.org/244/Historical-Society)).
- *Minnesota Accueil*, a volunteer welcome group for French speakers, which in May 2026 reminded French citizens that online voting for their consular representatives was open ([Minnesota Accueil](https://www.minnesotaaccueil.com/actualites.html)).
- *Senegalese Association of Minnesota*, founded in 2009, which offers immigration-paperwork help, ESL and Wolof classes, and monthly gatherings ([Senegalese Association](https://senegalesemn.com/)).
- *Diaspora Forces*, a Togolese and African community group in Minneapolis ([Diaspora Forces](https://diasporaforces.org/)).
- *Minnesota Cameroon Community (MINCAM)*, founded in 2008. Sahan Journal reported in 2024 that the Attorney General had petitioned in 2022 over the group's management of its St. Paul community center and that the group was in "a transitional period" ([Sahan Journal, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/minnesota-cameroonian-community-more-than-doubles/)).
- *Vanse*, a nonprofit that helps Haitian immigrants, run by David Policard in 2025 ([Sahan Journal, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/tps-ends-haiti-minnesota-deportation/)).

**Debates inside the communities.** Sources show several. *Echo de l'Ouest* argued for decades over keeping French versus assimilating ([MNHS](https://www.mnhs.org/newspapers/hub/echo-de-louest)). Cameroonian Minnesotans told Sahan Journal that as the community grew, more groups formed around tribes, and a former MINCAM president said there were "a lot of differences" over what to do with the group's community center, whose closing "created a void" ([Sahan Journal, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/minnesota-cameroonian-community-more-than-doubles/)).

**Facing hostility.** In fall 2024, after false national claims about Haitian immigrants in Springfield, Ohio, Rose Mingo-Holtz told Sahan Journal she worried about threats to her school, and scholar Beaudelaine Pierre called TPS "a constant state of dispossession" ([Sahan Journal, Oct. 7, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/haitian-minnesotans-springfield-ohio-rumors-backlash/)).

**Elected officials.** No current Minnesota elected official was found who publicly identifies with French Canadian, French, Haitian or French-speaking African heritage in any source found. This is a gap in the evidence, not a finding that none exist.

## 10. Education

**History.** Little Canada's first school, opened in 1850, taught in French until it joined a public district three years later and hired an English-speaking teacher ([Ramsey County History](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Fall1964_Wall.pdf); [City of Little Canada](https://www.canadiandays.org/284/Little-Canadas-History)). St. Paul's École St. Louis opened in 1873 ([St. Louis, King of France](https://stlouiskingoffrance.org/our-church/history-of-our-church/)), and Notre Dame de Lourdes school in Minneapolis required French for admission in 1887 ([MNHS](https://www.mnhs.org/newspapers/hub/echo-de-louest)).

**French immersion today.**
- *L'Étoile du Nord French Immersion*, a St. Paul Public Schools elementary taught by "fluent French-speaking educators" ([SPPS](https://frenchimmersion.spps.org/)).
- *Normandale Elementary* in Edina, with a K-5 French immersion program ([Edina Public Schools](https://normandale.edinaschools.org/)).
- *Notre École*, a K-5 French immersion STEAM school in Golden Valley whose staff come "from France, Canada, and different parts of the Francophone world" ([Notre École](https://www.notreecole.org/)).
- *The French Académie* in Minnetonka, which says it is the only private French immersion school in the Twin Cities accredited by France's Ministry of Education network (AEFE) ([The French Académie](https://www.thefrenchacademie.org/)).
- *Mes Amis French School*, a French immersion preschool and after-school program founded in 2005 by Rose Mingo-Holtz, who is originally from Haiti ([Mes Amis](https://mesamisfrenchschool.org/); [Sahan Journal, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/haitian-minnesotans-springfield-ohio-rumors-backlash/)).
- *Lac du Bois*, Concordia Language Villages' French village near Bemidji, open since 1962, with a voyageur-themed canoe program called Les Voyageurs ([Concordia Language Villages](https://www.concordialanguagevillages.org/languages/french/)).
- The *Alliance Française* teaches more than 900 students a year and runs programs in schools ([Alliance Française](https://www.afmsp.org/mission)).

**Colleges and scholars.** Macalester College's French and Francophone Studies chair, Joëlle Vitiello, moderated a 2024 discussion with Togolese author Edem Awumey ([Alliance Française](https://www.afmsp.org/mois-de-la-francophonie-2024)). Manka Nkimbeng of the University of Minnesota School of Public Health, who spoke to Sahan Journal as part of the Cameroonian community, studies dementia among African immigrant families (section 11).

**Learning across languages.** Djenane Saint Juste told Sahan Journal that Haitian children used to learning through "song, drumming and movement" can face a "big cultural shock" in quiet American classrooms, and that Creole was for many years prohibited in Haiti's schools, where children were required to learn French ([Sahan Journal, July 3, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/culture-community/afoutayi-haitian-dance-djenane-saint-juste/)).

## 11. Health and wellbeing, broadly

**Age shapes health.** French-heritage Minnesotans are older on average, so later-life health is a large part of the picture. In 2025, 28.8 percent of French-ancestry Minnesotans 65 and older reported a disability, close to the state's 27.2 percent for that age group ([ACS 2025, S0201](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSSPP1Y2025.S0201?g=040XX00US27&t=533)).

**Language access.** The Minnesota Department of Human Services includes French among the 15 languages in the "language block" that must appear on its public documents, telling readers how to get free interpreting. Haitian Creole is not one of the 15 ([DHS policy](https://mn.gov/dhs/assets/language-block-required-documents-available-general-public_tcm1053-646949.pdf)). A January 2026 Department of Public Safety study counted 16,313 French speakers, 4,819 of whom speak English less than "very well," and 1,578 Haitian Creole speakers. It reported that driver's license offices receive requests for French materials and that the department was translating more into French ([DPS, January 2026](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/mandated/260600/Report.pdf)).

**Dementia and African immigrant families.** University of Minnesota researchers led by Manka Nkimbeng, working with African Career, Education and Resources in Brooklyn Park, surveyed African immigrant families in Minnesota about dementia. Their 2022 paper lists language and accent barriers, lack of diversity among health care providers, and fear tied to immigration status among concerns raised ([Nkimbeng et al., 2022](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/ijerph/ijerph-19-04075/article_deploy/ijerph-19-04075.pdf)). A 2026 university webinar described stigma and misconceptions that can delay care ([UMN Office for Public Engagement](https://engagement.umn.edu/content/partnership-practice-co-developing-dementia-outreach-african-immigrant-community)). The work covers African immigrants broadly and does not report French speakers separately.

**Stress tied to status.** Haitian artist Florencia Pierre told Sahan Journal in 2024 that constant pressure to be accepted takes a physical toll ([Sahan Journal, Oct. 7, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/haitian-minnesotans-springfield-ohio-rumors-backlash/)), and an immigration law professor described the loss of TPS as "highly traumatizing" ([Sahan Journal, July 2, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/supreme-court-ends-tps-haiti-minnesota/)).

**Providers.** The Alliance Française's 2020-2021 directory listed French-speaking pediatricians, a pediatric dentist, a clinical psychologist and a yoga therapist in the Twin Cities ([directory](https://www.afmsp.org/s/frdtopost.pdf)). Their current practice was not reconfirmed. No clinic serving French-speaking or Haitian patients as its main mission was found in Minnesota.

## 12. Disability

**What Minnesota data show.** In the 2025 survey, 15.1 percent of Minnesotans of French ancestry reported a disability, against 12.0 percent of all Minnesotans. Within age groups the gap nearly disappears: 4.5 percent of children (state 4.0), 10.4 percent of adults 18 to 64 (state 10.1) and 28.8 percent of people 65 and older (state 27.2), all with margins of 1.6 to 3.3 points ([ACS 2025, S0201](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSSPP1Y2025.S0201?g=040XX00US27&t=533)). The 2024 survey gave 14.1 percent overall and 23.2 percent at 65 and older ([ACS 2024, S0201](https://data.census.gov/table/ACSSPP1Y2024.S0201?g=040XX00US27&t=533)). Because the gap nearly disappears within age groups, the group's older age may account for much of it; the survey itself does not say. No disability data were found for French Canadian, Haitian or French-speaking African Minnesotans as separate groups.

**Questions the evidence raises.**
- *Who defines disability?* The ACS counts people who report difficulty hearing, seeing, thinking, walking, self-care or living independently. A Haitian teaching artist told Sahan Journal that Haitian children used to learning through movement can be seen in U.S. classrooms as "troublemakers" or as behind, and that they "suddenly shine" when given room to move ([Sahan Journal, July 3, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/culture-community/afoutayi-haitian-dance-djenane-saint-juste/)). Who names a difference, and in which language, may shape what a family hears.
- *What does a label open or close?* DHS puts French, but not Haitian Creole, in its required notice of free interpreting ([DHS](https://mn.gov/dhs/assets/language-block-required-documents-available-general-public_tcm1053-646949.pdf)). How often French-speaking or Creole-speaking families reach disability waivers or special education, and in what language, was not found in any Minnesota source found.
- *Aging and memory.* With three in ten French-ancestry Minnesotans past 65, age-related disability and dementia may matter a great deal; no Minnesota source measured this. For African immigrant families, the University of Minnesota dementia research above found stigma, language barriers and fear related to immigration status ([Nkimbeng et al., 2022](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/ijerph/ijerph-19-04075/article_deploy/ijerph-19-04075.pdf)).
- *What produced an impairment?* No Minnesota source tied disability in these communities to work, war, disaster or migration. That remains an open question.

**People and organizations.** The Alliance Française says its 2018 building in North Minneapolis gave it ADA accessibility ([directory](https://www.afmsp.org/s/frdtopost.pdf)). The 2020-2021 directory also listed an interpreter certified by the Registry of Interpreters for the Deaf who offered French Sign Language interpretation; this was not reconfirmed. No Minnesota disability organization run by or for French-speaking or French-heritage Minnesotans was found.

## 13. What is changing now

- **Haitian TPS ends (2025-2026).** The Department of Homeland Security announced on June 27, 2025, that it would end TPS for Haitians ([Sahan Journal, July 2, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/tps-ends-haiti-minnesota-deportation/)). After court delays, the Supreme Court ruled 6-3 on June 25, 2026, allowing the end of TPS for Haitians and Syrians; Sahan Journal reported that the ruling would end it by the end of July, "but no final date has been set" ([Sahan Journal, July 2, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/supreme-court-ends-tps-haiti-minnesota/)). Rose Mingo-Holtz, who sits on Vanse's board, said the group was fielding questions from Haitians across the state, and Sahan Journal reported that some Haitian Minnesotans had already decided to leave the country ([Sahan Journal](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/supreme-court-ends-tps-haiti-minnesota/); [MPR News, July 16, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/07/16/minnesota-haitians-worry-about-what-comes-as-next-as-tps-protections-end)). No source found gives the number of Haitian Minnesotans with TPS.
- **A Haitian arts group leaves.** Afoutayi announced that all its activities would move to Toronto by summer 2026, closing with a free virtual workshop in February 2026 for Minnesota families, "especially as some members of our company and community have been directly impacted" by immigration changes ([Afoutayi](https://www.afoutayidmaco.com/)).
- **Canadian citizenship by descent (Dec. 2025).** Canada's Bill C-3 came into force on Dec. 15, 2025, removing the first-generation limit on citizenship by descent. CIC News estimated about 47,000 Canadian and French Canadian ancestry responses in Minnesota and urged families to check eligibility ([CIC News, Oct. 7, 2026](https://www.cicnews.com/2026/10/generations-of-minnesotans-may-now-be-canadian-citizens-through-their-ancestry-and-not-know-it-yet-1081081.html)). The Consulate General in Minneapolis handles proof-of-citizenship requests ([Government of Canada](https://www.international.gc.ca/country-pays/us-eu/minneapolis.aspx?lang=eng)).
- **Anniversaries.** The Alliance Française turned 50 as a Minnesota nonprofit on Oct. 29, 2025 ([AFMSP50](https://www.afmsp.org/afmsp50)). The Archdiocese marked 175 years in 2025 ([Archdiocese](https://www.archspm.org/from-pigs-eye-to-saint-paul-175-years-of-the-catholic-church-in-minnesota/)). Little Canada's Canadian Days used the 2026 theme "Where 50 Years Feels Like Home" ([Canadian Days](https://www.canadiandays.org/449/Photo-Gallery)).
- **Fewer naming French roots; more newcomers.** French and French Canadian ancestry counts have fallen steadily since 2006-2010, while Haitian ancestry and Cameroon-born counts have grown (section 1).
- **New French restaurants draw attention.** Bûcheron won a national James Beard award in 2025 and Thérèse opened in Edina ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/the-best-restaurants-in-the-twin-cities-2026/); [review](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/restaurant-review-therese-french-arrival/)). Salut Bar Américain has closed ([Salut](https://www.salutbaramericain.com/)).
- **State translation.** The Department of Public Safety reported in January 2026 that it had begun translating driver's license materials into French ([DPS](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/mandated/260600/Report.pdf)).

## 14. Strengths and contributions

- **Founding institutions.** French Canadian families helped found St. Paul, St. Anthony and Little Canada, gave the land for the chapel that gave St. Paul its name, and ran the area's first commercial grist mill ([Ramsey County History](https://rchs.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/11/RCHS_Fall1964_Wall.pdf); [Little Canada](https://www.canadiandays.org/262/Gervais-Grist-Mill-Site)).
- **Skilled hands on the Capitol.** French Canadian stone carvers worked on the State Capitol from 1895 to 1905 ([Frenette and Willis](https://migrationsfrancophones.ustboniface.ca/wp-content/uploads/2024/01/Vignette1_Capitol_18mars18.pdf)).
- **A long civic memory.** Volunteer historical societies, a heritage foundation, reenactors and a 50-year town festival keep the history public ([Little Canada](https://www.canadiandays.org/244/Historical-Society); [MNHS](https://www.mnhs.org/media/news/snake-river-fur-post-introduce-new-summer-event)).
- **Language as a shared resource.** Public and private immersion schools, Concordia's Lac du Bois and a century-old Alliance Française give thousands of Minnesotans, of every background, access to French ([SPPS](https://frenchimmersion.spps.org/); [Concordia](https://www.concordialanguagevillages.org/languages/french/); [Alliance Française](https://www.afmsp.org/mission)).
- **Bridges between peoples.** The Alliance Française's Mois de la Francophonie has featured Togo, Laos and Tunisia; a French-born Hmong chef and an Argentine-born chef are among those bringing French cooking to new audiences ([Alliance Française](https://www.afmsp.org/mois-de-la-francophonie-2026); [Sahan Journal](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/marc-heu-patisserie-paris-st-paul-dale-street-hmong-chef/)).
- **Newcomers building community.** Senegalese, Togolese, Cameroonian and Haitian Minnesotans run language classes, mutual aid, festivals, soccer tournaments, arts programs and schools ([Senegalese Association](https://senegalesemn.com/); [Sahan Journal, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/minnesota-cameroonian-community-more-than-doubles/); [Mes Amis](https://mesamisfrenchschool.org/)).

## What could not be established

- **A single count of French speakers by origin.** Census tables count French speakers and birthplaces separately; no source found shows how many Minnesotans who speak French at home are Haitian, African, European or Canadian in origin. Minnesota Compass has no profile for any of these groups ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities)).
- **Profiles for French Canadian, Haitian or French-speaking African Minnesotans.** The Census Bureau's detailed profile exists only for French ancestry; income, poverty and disability for the other groups could not be shown.
- **How many Haitian Minnesotans held TPS,** and the exact date TPS ended. The U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services page could not be reached. Cameroon's TPS end date was also not confirmed.
- **Current French-language worship.** No current French-language Catholic Mass and no Haitian or African francophone congregation was confirmed in any source found. A directory listing for a St. Paul Haitian congregation could not be reached.
- **Francophone African and Haitian businesses.** No current Haitian, Senegalese, Congolese, Togolese or Ivorian restaurant or grocery in Minnesota was confirmed; the one Cameroonian market named in 2024 coverage was not reconfirmed.
- **Elected officials.** No current Minnesota elected official with a publicly stated French Canadian, French, Haitian or francophone African identity was found.
- **Organizations whose sites were unreachable or blocked:** the French-American Heritage Foundation, the French-American Chamber of Commerce (Minnesota), the Congolese Community of Minnesota, KFAI's *Bonjour Minnesota* page, Explore Minnesota's event pages, Our Lady of Lourdes' parish site and Every.org's listing for Vanse.
- **French Canadian life today.** Beyond festivals, genealogy and parishes, no recent Minnesota source describes French Canadian foodways, language use or family life in the present day.
- **Disability evidence.** No Minnesota study, DHS report or community organization addressing disability among French-speaking or French-heritage Minnesotans was found.
- **Star Tribune reporting** on languages spoken at home (July 31, 2025) could not be reached; comparable Census figures are given instead.
