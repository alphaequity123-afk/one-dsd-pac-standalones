# Koreans in Minnesota

## 1. People and place

**Two counts, two definitions.** Minnesota Compass, using the American Community Survey for 2020 to 2024, counts 28,180 Korean Minnesotans, give or take 2,630. It places 76.2 percent in the Twin Cities and 23.8 percent in Greater Minnesota ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/korean)). Compass builds its cultural community profiles from a combination of self-reported race, ancestry, birthplace and parents' characteristics ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/cultural-communities)). The State Demographic Center's January 2026 chartbook, using the 2019 to 2023 survey, reports a much smaller Korean group: 15,700 people, give or take 2,200, in about 6,700 households ([State Demographic Center, January 2026](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). The difference is mostly definition. The state report puts anyone who belongs to two or more of its groups, for example Korean and White, into a separate "Multiple Cultural Groups" category, so many people of mixed heritage drop out of its Korean count. Both are estimates with wide margins. The Minnesota Historical Society Press, describing Sooh-Rhee Ryu's book *Koreans in Minnesota*, gives a third figure: about 16,000 Koreans, many of them first-generation immigrants, plus a large adoptee community ([MNHS Press](https://shop.mnhs.org/products/koreans-minnesota)).

**Three ways people came.** The record shows at least three streams, and they overlap.

- *Students and professionals.* The University of Minnesota helped rebuild Seoul National University after the Korean War, and, as a former president of the SNU alumni association at the university told the Minnesota Daily in 2015, "hundreds of Korean students" came to study in Minnesota through that project, many returning to teach ([Minnesota Daily, Nov. 19, 2015](https://mndaily.com/uncategorized/kaler-looks-strengthen-u-ties-east-asia/11/19/2015/snoadmin/)). An oral history catalog record for Philip C. Ahn, who came to Macalester College in 1953 after serving as an interpreter in the war, describes a close-knit group of Korean students at Macalester in the 1950s as the first significant group of Koreans in the state. The same record lists Korean wives of American servicemen who settled in Minnesota and the history of Korean churches among the topics he discussed ([Minnesota Digital Library item record](https://digital.centerforknitandcrochet.org/items/show/8308)). The MNHS Press description of *Koreans in Minnesota* includes a personal account by Soon Ja Lee, who arrived in 1953 ([MNHS Press](https://shop.mnhs.org/products/koreans-minnesota)).
- *Adoptees.* Minnesota's part in Korean adoption began in 1955 through a Children's Home Society of Minnesota program and grew when that agency and Lutheran Social Services began Korean programs in 1967 and 1969. MNopedia, in an entry by scholar Kim Park Nelson, says Minnesota has the highest concentration of Korean adoptees of any state, and, in a passage discussing 2017, that adoptees make up almost half of Minnesota's Korean American population ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/korean-transracial-adoption-minnesota)). Estimates of how many differ. Sahan Journal in 2024 used a figure of about 10,000 to 15,000 Korean adoptees in Minnesota ([Sahan Journal, July 29, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/health/minnesota-adoption-genetics-family-health-history/)). AK Connection, the adult adoptee organization, says on its site that Minnesota is home to about 10 percent of all Korean adoptees worldwide ([AK Connection](https://akconnection.org/)). MPR News calls Minnesota home to the largest population of Korean adoptees in the U.S. ([MPR News, Nov. 26, 2025](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2025/11/26/mistaken-minnesotas-korean-adoptees-grapple-with-confessed-systemic-corruption)). No count from a government source separates adoptees from immigrants.
- *Immigrant families.* Later immigrants built congregations and community groups. The Korean American Association of Minnesota, which MNHS Press calls "the cornerstone of Korean immigrant society," lists a long line of former presidents and a 2026 slate of officers ([MNHS Press](https://shop.mnhs.org/products/koreans-minnesota); [KAAM](https://www.mnkorea.org/about)).

**Age and birthplace.** In the Compass profile, 36.4 percent are 17 or younger and only 2.6 percent are 65 or older. Foreign-born residents are 44.7 percent, give or take 4.1 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/korean)). In the narrower state count, about three in four (11,800 of 15,700) were born abroad and the median age is 36 ([State Demographic Center](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). Neither source says how many of the foreign-born are adoptees. Among foreign-born Korean adults 16 to 64 in the state count, about 84 percent have lived in the U.S. more than 10 years ([State Demographic Center](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). The next generation is visible too: AK Connection now holds meetups for "DoKADs," descendants of Korean adoptees ([AK Connection, Sept. 27, 2026](https://akconnection.org/event/dokad-meetup/)).

**Where.** Compass gives only the Twin Cities and Greater Minnesota split. Named places in the sources run from Snelling Avenue in St. Paul's Midway, which Visit Saint Paul calls the city's "Mini Koreatown," to Brooklyn Center, Shoreview, Vadnais Heights and Maplewood, and to Mankato and North Mankato in southern Minnesota ([Visit Saint Paul](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/directory/sole-caf%C3%A9/); [KAAM church list](https://www.mnkorea.org/blank-2); [Sahan Journal/MPR News, April 29, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/news-partners/asian-grocers-navigate-ever-changing-tariff-landscape/)). In 2021, Twin Cities PBS profiled a Korean-born woman, Minam Morris, who ran a small Asian grocery and made egg rolls by hand in Solway, a town of 98 in northern Minnesota ([TPT, Dec. 7, 2021](https://www.tptoriginals.org/some-seoul-in-solway/)). Brook Mallak, an attorney, writes of growing up as a Korean adoptee "in greater Minnesota"; no source counts how many adoptees live outside the Twin Cities ([Korean Quarterly, March 29, 2026](https://koreanquarterly.org/front_page_below_fold/the-quiet-strength-of-community/)).

**Language.** Among Korean Minnesotans 5 and older, 81.5 percent speak only English at home, 18.5 percent speak another language, and 8.7 percent, give or take 2.6 points, speak English less than "very well" ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/korean)). The state count, which uses a different question cut, finds about 4 percent who speak English "not well" or "not at all" ([State Demographic Center](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). The Korean Service Center lists translation and interpretation among its services for seniors, which signals continuing need among older immigrants ([Korean Service Center](https://www.kscmn.org/special-access)).

## 2. All walks of life

The figures below are from the Compass 2020 to 2024 profile in 2024 dollars unless noted ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/korean)). Margins are wide because the group is small.

- **Comfortable and well-off households.** Median household income is $102,938, give or take $20,667. Just over half of households, 51.2 percent, take in $100,000 or more. The state's narrower count shows a median of $77,700, give or take $26,800, and median earnings for workers of $64,900 ([State Demographic Center](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). The two sources use different years and definitions, so the gap is not a trend.
- **Professionals and owners.** Among adults 25 and older, 63.6 percent hold a bachelor's degree or more, including 24.9 percent with a graduate or professional degree. Visible careers include the Ramsey County attorney, who took office in 2011 as the first Korean American chief prosecutor in the country by the county's account, and whose family came from South Korea in 1973 when he was 3 ([Ramsey County](https://www.ramseycountymn.gov/your-government/leadership/county-attorneys-office/meet-john-j-choi); [MPR News, Jan. 4, 2011](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2011/01/04/choi-ramsey-county-attorney)); chef Ann Kim, a Korean immigrant and James Beard Award winner who built Pizzeria Lola and Young Joni ([Pizzeria Lola](https://www.pizzerialola.com/); [Twin Cities Business](https://tcbmag.com/tcb-100-people/ann-kim/)); Heewon Lee, a Korean-born adoptee who is assistant director of the University of Minnesota's genetic counseling program ([Sahan Journal, July 29, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/health/minnesota-adoption-genetics-family-health-history/)); and scholar Kim Park Nelson, whose research on adoptees continues ([AK Connection, July 10, 2026](https://akconnection.org/2026/07/10/summer-2026-newsletter-from-ak-connection/)).
- **Middle-income and working families.** Owner-occupied homes are 64.1 percent of households. Employment among adults 18 to 64 is 84.6 percent, 82.9 percent for men and 86.0 percent for women. Married couples with children under 18 are 23.8 percent of households. Another 6.3 percent of people live between the poverty line and one and a half times that line.
- **People living alone, renters and those in poverty.** Poverty is 3.7 percent, give or take 1.4 points, but it is 12.6 percent among people 18 to 24, with a very wide margin of 8.2 points. Among renters, 30.8 percent spend too much of their income on housing, and 6.5 percent of households have no vehicle. Strikingly, 37.4 percent of households are a person living alone, give or take 7.2 points. The state count finds the same pattern: 40 percent of Korean households are one person ([State Demographic Center](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). The Korean Service Center helps seniors apply for and renew Minneapolis public housing and apply for Supplemental Security Income, which shows that some Korean elders live on low incomes ([Korean Service Center](https://www.kscmn.org/special-access)).

**Over time.** The state demographer's earlier analysis of 2016 to 2020 data put Korean household income at about $69,000, below Chinese, Vietnamese and Hmong households in that report ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 25, 2023](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/hmong-indian-asian-minnesota-us-census-data-income-poverty/)). Compass now shows a higher figure for 2020 to 2024, but the periods overlap and the margins are large, so the numbers suggest a direction, not a precise change.

No Minnesota figure for Korean household wealth was found. Income and home ownership are the closest measures available.

## 3. Work and the economy

**Who works.** In the state count, about 85 percent of Korean Minnesotans aged 16 to 64 are in the labor force, and most workers (8,700 of 10,400) work 35 hours a week or more ([State Demographic Center](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). No occupation or industry table for Korean Minnesotans was found.

**Science, medicine and the university.** The Minnesota Project linked the University of Minnesota with Seoul National University in medicine, agriculture and engineering after the war ([Minnesota Daily, Nov. 19, 2015](https://mndaily.com/uncategorized/kaler-looks-strengthen-u-ties-east-asia/11/19/2015/snoadmin/)). Philip Ahn's record traces a career typical of that era: Macalester graduate in 1957, junior scientist at the Hormel Institute in Austin, then a lipid chemist at the University of Minnesota ([Minnesota Digital Library item record](https://digital.centerforknitandcrochet.org/items/show/8308)).

**Small business and food.** Restaurants and groceries are the most visible Korean businesses in the sources, along with newer ventures that ride the popularity of Korean food. In North Mankato, Korean American Sook Nam and her husband opened Seoul Foods about four months before an April 2025 report, because Asian families were driving more than an hour for staples. By April 2025 they were worried about tariffs on goods from China, which made up about half their inventory ([Sahan Journal/MPR News, April 29, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/news-partners/asian-grocers-navigate-ever-changing-tariff-landscape/)).

**Labor.** Workers at Ann Kim's Uptown restaurant, Kim's, voted to unionize in 2024; less than two months later the restaurant announced it would close, citing "ongoing financial losses" ([Twin Cities Business, Aug. 22, 2024](https://tcbmag.com/ann-kim-to-shutter-kims-in-uptown/)). No other Minnesota source on Korean union membership was found.

**Chambers and networks.** No Korean American chamber of commerce or business association in Minnesota was found. The Korean American Association of Minnesota is a community association, not a business group ([GiveMN](https://www.givemn.org/organization/Kaam)).

## 4. Businesses and commercial life

A fuller list, with the evidence for each place's status, accompanies this profile. Here is the shape of it. Korean Minnesota does not have a large Koreatown; its businesses are spread out, and some long-known names have recently closed.

**Snelling Avenue, St. Paul.** Visit Saint Paul places Sole Cafe in "the heart of St. Paul's Mini Koreatown." Sole Cafe (684 Snelling Ave. N.) and Mirror of Korea (761 Snelling Ave. N.), a second-generation family restaurant whose founding chef, Yong Lee, has about 40 years in Korean restaurants, are both listed with hours ([Visit Saint Paul](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/directory/sole-caf%C3%A9/); [Visit Saint Paul](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/directory/mirror-of-korea/)). A Hamline University student paper described a small Korean grocery nearby as the "main go-to place for Korean grocery" ([The Oracle, March 24, 2021](https://hamlineoracle.com/8779/life/variety/aapi-owned-businesses-of-midway/)). Visit Saint Paul also lists Juche, an East Side lounge, and bb.q Chicken ([Visit Saint Paul](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/top-korean-restaurants-in-saint-paul/)). None of these posted a dated 2025 or 2026 notice on a page that could be opened, so they are recorded as not confirmed.

**Chef-driven restaurants, and closures.** Ann Kim's Young Joni in Northeast Minneapolis, which won her the James Beard award for Best Chef: Midwest, closed on Sept. 14, 2025, after a lease dispute; Mpls.St.Paul Magazine reported that Pizzeria Lola and Hello Pizza remained open ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, June 16, 2025](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/foodie/young-joni-restaurant-closure-northeast-minneapolis-ann-kim/)). Her Korean American restaurant Kim's in Uptown closed in August 2024 ([Twin Cities Business](https://tcbmag.com/ann-kim-to-shutter-kims-in-uptown/)). Minari, chef Jeff Watson's restaurant in Northeast Minneapolis, which Minnesota Monthly says celebrates his Korean heritage, opened a bar lounge in September 2025 ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, Sept. 19, 2025](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/pikok-lounge-minari-new-bar-minneapolis-restaurants/); [Minnesota Monthly](https://www.minnesotamonthly.com/food-drink/coming-soon-to-northeast-minneapolis-minari/)).

**Long-running names.** Hoban in Eagan and its Uptown barbecue sister, Dong Yang's grocery and back-of-store deli near Columbia Heights, and K-Town appear in magazine guides, but no current, dated sign of operation could be confirmed for them ([Minnesota Monthly](https://www.minnesotamonthly.com/directory/dining/hoban-korean-bbq/); [Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/dong-yang/); [Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/locations/k-town/)).

**Chains and malls.** KPOT, a Korean barbecue and hot pot chain, opened its first Minnesota location in Maple Grove in March 2025 ([CCX Media, Jan. 22, 2025](https://ccxmedia.org/news/new-korean-bbq-and-hot-pot-restaurant-opening-soon-in-maple-grove/); [Yahoo News](https://www.yahoo.com/news/minnesota-restaurant-openings-closings-march-131542727.html)). Asia Village in Blaine's Northtown Mall opened in June 2026 with a Korean street food restaurant and a Korean beauty store among its tenants ([Sahan Journal, June 15, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/asia-village-opens-blaine-northtown-mall/)). In June 2026, Mpls.St.Paul Magazine reported that an entity that appears to be H Mart's real estate arm bought part of Midway Marketplace, 1450 University Ave. W., for $6 million; the magazine said it could not be sure H Mart would open there, and its writer worried about the effect on family-owned Asian grocers in Midway and Frogtown ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, June 16, 2026](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/foodie/h-mart-korean-asian-grocery-supermarket-st-paul-mn/)).

**Media.** Korean Quarterly, founded in 1997, publishes in English on Korean Americans and adopted Koreans, with frequent Minnesota news ([Korean Quarterly](https://koreanquarterly.org/); [Korean Quarterly, April 7, 2025](https://koreanquarterly.org/features/to-know-we-are-not-alone/)). No Minnesota Korean-language newspaper or broadcast was found.

**What is thin.** No Korean-owned law, accounting, insurance, tax, travel or real estate firm in Minnesota could be confirmed, and no Korean salon or professional directory turned up. The business list is therefore shorter than the community's size and income would suggest.

## 5. Religion and spiritual life

**Churches as hubs.** Philip Ahn's oral history includes the history of Korean churches in the Twin Cities among its topics, which places them early in the community's life ([Minnesota Digital Library item record](https://digital.centerforknitandcrochet.org/items/show/8308)). The Korean American Association of Minnesota lists eight congregations on its church page, among them Baptist, United Methodist and Presbyterian churches, including one in Mankato ([KAAM](https://www.mnkorea.org/blank-2)). Several of those listed websites could not be reached, so current status varies.

- The Korean Presbyterian Church of Minnesota in Brooklyn Center has served the area for more than 40 years. CCX Media reported in 2022 that it holds a Korean-language service mostly for first-generation members and an English ministry, which a member said was the only English ministry in a Korean church in the Twin Cities, along with Korean language and culture classes ([CCX Media, May 29, 2022](https://ccxmedia.org/news/brooklyn-center-korean-church-celebrates-faith-culture/); [PC(USA)](https://pcusa.org/congregation/korean-pc-minnesota-church-brooklyn-ctr-mn)). The same member said two Korean Presbyterian churches had once merged into one.
- The True Light of the Lord Church in Shoreview lists Sunday worship at 9:30 and 11 a.m. and livestreams services and dawn prayer ([True Light of the Lord Church](https://www.tllcmn.org/)).
- St. Andrew Kim Catholic Church at 509 54th St. W., Minneapolis, named for the first Korean priest, began with 14 families celebrating Mass at the Saint Paul Seminary in 1973, became a parish in 1990, and had priests sent from the Archdiocese of Seoul until 2015. The archdiocese calls it the only Korean Catholic community in Minnesota for the past 50 years; Mass is offered in English and Korean ([Archdiocese of Saint Paul and Minneapolis](https://www.archspm.org/location/st-andrew-kim-catholic-church/)).

**Faith in adoptee life.** The Korean Adoptees Ministry Center describes itself as a Christian ministry for Korean adoptees, Korean Americans and their families, offering social services, trips to Korea and cultural events ([KAM Center](https://www.kamcenter.org/)). Many adoptees were raised in their adoptive families' churches, but no source measured adoptees' religious life.

**Buddhism and no faith.** No Korean Buddhist temple in Minnesota was found. No Minnesota survey of Korean Minnesotans' religious affiliation, including people of no faith, was found either; it should not be assumed that most Korean Minnesotans are churchgoers.

## 6. Celebrations and observances

**Seollal (Lunar New Year).** In 2026 the holiday fell on Feb. 17 ([Sahan Journal, Feb. 18, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/lunar-new-year-horse-celebrate-minnesota/)). A recurring public celebration is held by community groups with the dance and drum school JangmiArts. It was at White Bear Lake High School on Feb. 10, 2024, and Feb. 1, 2025, with Korean food, shopping and performances, according to CBS Minnesota, which named the Korean American Association of Minnesota as co-host ([CBS Minnesota, Feb. 9, 2024](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/lunar-new-year-celebrations-twin-cities/); [CBS Minnesota, Jan. 29, 2025](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/lunar-new-year-minnesota-celebrations/)). Sahan Journal named the host in 2025 and 2026 as the Korean Cultural Association, with a tteokguk (rice cake soup) lunch, a folk painting workshop and taekwondo in 2025, and a marketplace, dance and dessert reception on Feb. 21, 2026, at Mariner Middle School in White Bear Lake ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 30, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/10-ways-to-celebrate-lunar-new-year-minnesota/); [Sahan Journal, Feb. 18, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/lunar-new-year-horse-celebrate-minnesota/)). Sejong Academy students mark the Korean New Year with drumming, saebae (bowing to elders) and a hanbok walk ([Sahan Journal, June 10, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/education/sejong-academy-maplewood-korean-immersion-karen-students/)). AK Connection's newsletter mentions its own annual Seollal celebration ([AK Connection, July 10, 2026](https://akconnection.org/2026/07/10/summer-2026-newsletter-from-ak-connection/)).

**Chuseok (harvest festival).** In 2026, South Korea's Chuseok holiday ran Sept. 24 to 26 ([PublicHolidays.co.kr](https://publicholidays.co.kr/)). AK Connection's Chuseok Festival was held Saturday, Sept. 26, 2026, from 4 to 10 p.m. at East Side Neighborhood Services in Minneapolis, free and open to all, with a sensory-friendly hour for young families, a Korean market, noraebang (karaoke) and performances ([AK Connection](https://akconnection.org/event/ak-connections-chuseok-festival/)). In 2024, Adoptee Hub timed its Kimchi Festival in St. Louis Park to Chuseok and hoped for more than 3,000 people; the first, in 2022, ran out of kimchi in 45 minutes ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 26, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/kimchi-festival-korean-adoptee-hub-st-louis-park/)). No 2025 or 2026 Kimchi Festival was found.

**Korean Culture Festival.** The Korean American Association of Minnesota held its K-Culture Festival at the Mall of America on Sept. 21, 2024, and Sept. 20, 2025, and its 2026 Korean Culture Festival there on Sept. 12, 2026, from 1 to 5 p.m. ([KAAM](https://www.mnkorea.org/post/2026-korean-culture-festival); [KAAM](https://www.mnkorea.org/post/k-culture-festival-saturday-9-20-2025-1-5pm)).

**Children's Day (May 5).** AK Connection held a Children's Day gathering for adoptees and families on May 3, 2026, and the Korean Adoptees Ministry Center held its own ([AK Connection](https://akconnection.org/event/childrens-day/); [AK Connection, April 10, 2026](https://akconnection.org/2026/04/10/april-2026-updates-from-ak-connection/)). The Korean Service Center lists Parents' Day among the holidays it marks with seniors ([Korean Service Center](https://www.kscmn.org/special-access)).

**Culture camps.** Korean Culture Camp of Minnesota, founded in 1977, has set its 2027 camp for July 19 to 23 with a 50th-year celebration on July 24 ([KCC](https://kccmn.org/)). Kamp Kimchee in Crosslake has set July 12 to 16, 2027 ([Kamp Kimchee](https://kampkimchee.org/)). Camp Choson and Camp Moon Hwa are also named in MNopedia ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/korean-transracial-adoption-minnesota)).

**National days.** March 1 (Independence Movement Day) and Aug. 15 (Liberation Day) are public holidays in South Korea ([PublicHolidays.co.kr](https://publicholidays.co.kr/)). No Minnesota commemoration of either was found. Jan. 13 is observed as Korean American Day, marking the anniversary of Korean immigration to the U.S. ([Office of the Governor of Wisconsin, Jan. 13, 2026](https://evers.wi.gov/Documents/011326_Acessible%20Proclamation_Korean%20American%20Day.pdf)); no Minnesota proclamation or event was found. No Korean-specific Minnesota observance of Christmas or Easter was documented, though the congregations listed in section 5 are Christian.

## 7. Family, home and daily life

**Households.** Korean Minnesota households are small: 2.29 people on average, and about 37 to 40 percent are one person living alone ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/korean); [State Demographic Center](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). About a third of households in the state count have children under 18. Neither source explains why so many live alone. Who these households are, by age and by whether the person is an immigrant or an adoptee, is an open question.

**Two family stories, often overlapping.** Many Korean Minnesotans were raised by adoptive families, most of them white, as MNopedia's account of transracial adoption describes ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/korean-transracial-adoption-minnesota)). Adoptee Hub's founder, Ami Nafzger, told Sahan Journal that transracial adoptees can be told by their families that "you don't need to be Korean" ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 26, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/kimchi-festival-korean-adoptee-hub-st-louis-park/)). A volunteer at the same festival, raised by a Korean adoptee mother who was not much "into Korean culture," described learning about it as an adult alongside her. AK Connection has begun planning for the next generation, opening its markets to Korean Americans and holding events for descendants of adoptees ([AK Connection, July 10, 2026](https://akconnection.org/2026/07/10/summer-2026-newsletter-from-ak-connection/)). For many adoptees, family history itself is missing: a University of Minnesota genetic counselor who is a Korean adoptee told Sahan Journal she is reminded "multiple times a year" that she has no family health history ([Sahan Journal, July 29, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/health/minnesota-adoption-genetics-family-health-history/)).

**Food.** Kimchi, tteokguk at the New Year, and shared tables at camps recur in the sources. Kamp Kimchee serves a chef-prepared Korean lunch to children and families every day of camp ([Kamp Kimchee](https://kampkimchee.org/)). The Korean Service Center's Grace Place serves Korean meals three times a day, and its dining program serves Korean meals 365 days a year ([Korean Service Center](https://www.kscmn.org/grace-place); [Korean Service Center](https://www.kscmn.org/all-programs)).

**Aging.** The Korean Service Center runs Grace Place, 24-hour customized living established in 1999 for people over 65 or with disabilities, plus transportation, medical companions, respite care, a caregiver support program and help with Social Security, SSI, housing and voting ([Korean Service Center](https://www.kscmn.org/grace-place); [Korean Service Center](https://www.kscmn.org/caregiver-support); [Korean Service Center](https://www.kscmn.org/special-access)). It marked its 35th anniversary on Sept. 25, 2026 ([Korean Service Center](https://www.kscmn.org/35th-anniversary)).

**Dress, marriage and childhood.** Students wear hanbok for a fashion walk at Sejong Academy's New Year celebration ([Sahan Journal, June 10, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/education/sejong-academy-maplewood-korean-immersion-karen-students/)). No Minnesota source on marriage patterns or child-rearing in Korean immigrant families was found.

## 8. Arts, media, sports and culture

**Writers.** MNopedia names poet Sun Yung Shin and memoirist Jane Jeong Trenka among adult adoptees whose work has drawn national and international notice ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/korean-transracial-adoption-minnesota)). Shin, a Minnesota-based writer, launched her memoir *Heart Eater* at Moon Palace Books in September 2026; she told Sahan Journal that adoption records "aren't necessarily neutral" and can leave adoptees with "ambiguous loss" ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 11, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/twin-cities-things-to-do-sun-yung-shin-st-kates-selby-ave-jazz-cedarfest/)).

**Theater.** MNopedia records that Theater Mu's first season included *Mask Dance* (1993), based on conversations with local Korean adoptees, followed by adoptee-themed plays in 1998, 2011 and 2014 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/korean-transracial-adoption-minnesota)). AK Connection's summer 2026 newsletter noted a Guthrie Theater production featuring Korean adoptee actress Katie Bradley ([AK Connection](https://akconnection.org/2026/07/10/summer-2026-newsletter-from-ak-connection/)).

**Dance, drumming and visual art.** JangmiArts began in the 1980s, per MNopedia, and teaches Korean dance and drumming; it performs at Seollal and teaches at Camp Choson ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/korean-transracial-adoption-minnesota); [GiveMN](https://www.givemn.org/organization/Campchoson)). Sejong Academy teaches samulnori, traditional percussion, in its regular curriculum ([MN Association of Charter Schools](https://mncharterschools.org/directory/location-detail.php?ID=2585)). Korean-born artist Bo Young An, who moved to Minneapolis in 2020 for graduate school at MCAD, showed her first solo exhibit at St. Paul's Second Shift Studio in spring 2026 ([Sahan Journal, April 3, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/twin-cities-things-to-do-korean-art-dj-party-arboretum-show/)). The Walker Art Center hosted a review of Korean American Deaf artist Christine Sun Kim's work in 2026 ([Korean Quarterly, April 28, 2026](https://koreanquarterly.org/front_page_below_fold/deafness-heard-seen-and-understood/)). The Korean alternative pop band Leenalchi is set to make its Minnesota premiere at the Cedar Cultural Center on Nov. 7 ([Korean Quarterly, Oct. 15, 2026](https://koreanquarterly.org/front_page_below_fold/old-music-made-new/)).

**Film.** AK Connection ran a free adoption film series in June and July 2026, paired with anonymous research by Kim Park Nelson for a book on adoption in film ([AK Connection](https://akconnection.org/2026/07/10/summer-2026-newsletter-from-ak-connection/)).

**Media.** Korean Quarterly is the community's own outlet. Twin Cities PBS reporter Kaomi Lee, a Korean adoptee, hosted MPR's November 2025 forum on adoption fraud ([MPR News](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2025/11/26/mistaken-minnesotas-korean-adoptees-grapple-with-confessed-systemic-corruption)).

**Sports.** When the Minnesota Twins brought Korean slugger Byung Ho Park to Target Field as a rookie in 2016, about 800 fans filled four sections dubbed "Byung Ho's Balcony" on a mid-April Monday when rain loomed, wearing hats with "Minnesota Twins" written in Korean, Mpls.St.Paul Magazine reported ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, 2016](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/five%28ish%29-questions-with/)). Park returned to Korea after the 2017 season ([MLB.com, Nov. 27, 2017](https://www.mlb.com/news/byungho-park-to-leave-twins-play-in-korea-c262438322)). AK Connection now holds an annual Adoptee Day at the Twins with the Mixed Roots Foundation ([AK Connection](https://akconnection.org/2026/07/10/summer-2026-newsletter-from-ak-connection/)). Taekwondo is taught at Sejong Academy ([MN Association of Charter Schools](https://mncharterschools.org/directory/location-detail.php?ID=2585)) and performed at Seollal by Lee's Champion Taekwondo ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 30, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/10-ways-to-celebrate-lunar-new-year-minnesota/)). No other Korean Minnesotan professional athletes were documented.

## 9. Civic and political life

**Elected officials.** The Korean American Institute's January 2025 almanac counts three Korean American elected officials in Minnesota's history, without naming them in the summary ([Korean American Institute, Jan. 13, 2025](https://ka.institute/wp-content/uploads/2025/05/KAI-press-advisory-on-Korean-American-Political-Almanac.pdf)). Those documented here are:

- The Ramsey County attorney, a Korean American in office since 2011 and a former St. Paul city attorney ([Ramsey County](https://www.ramseycountymn.gov/your-government/leadership/county-attorneys-office/meet-john-j-choi); [KAGC](https://kagc.us/korean-american-elected-officials-candidates-local/)).
- A Korean adoptee who grew up in Independence and represents Ward 5 on the St. Paul City Council ([Korean Quarterly, March 21, 2024](https://koreanquarterly.org/front_page_below_fold/korean-power-lead-city-council/); [City of Saint Paul](https://www.stpaul.gov/department/city-council)).
- Mitra Jalali, whose mother is a Korean adoptee, was St. Paul City Council president in 2024, when Korean Quarterly noted that both council leaders were Korean American, a first for the city and state. She stepped down in early 2025, and the Ward 4 seat was filled in an August 2025 special election ([Korean Quarterly](https://koreanquarterly.org/front_page_below_fold/korean-power-lead-city-council/); [FOX 9, Aug. 13, 2025](https://www.fox9.com/news/st-paul-special-election-molly-coleman-replaces-mitra-jalali-2025)).

**Organizations.** The Korean American Association of Minnesota, based in Vadnais Heights, aims to build a Korean cultural center for all Korean Minnesotans, "immigrants, second generation, adoptees and our families" ([GiveMN](https://www.givemn.org/organization/Kaam); [KAAM](https://www.mnkorea.org/home-2)). AK Connection, founded in 2000, is run "by adoptees, for adoptees" ([AK Connection](https://akconnection.org/about/)). Adoptee Hub offers birth family search help, reunification support and mental health care ([Adoptee Hub](https://www.adopteehub.org/aboutus)). The Korean American Women's Association of Minnesota held member meetings and an Aug. 8, 2026 picnic in Edina ([KAWAMN](https://kawamn.org/)). The Minnesota Association for Korean Americans has given Korean American high school seniors about $385,000 in scholarships since its first gala in 1995 ([Korean Quarterly, April 10, 2025](https://koreanquarterly.org/mn-news/scholarship-support-for-three-decades/)).

**Adoptee rights.** In March 2025, South Korea's Truth and Reconciliation Commission reported fraud in adoption records it had examined and called it a human rights violation under Korean law; a third round of investigation opened Feb. 26, 2026 ([Korean Quarterly, March 23, 2026](https://koreanquarterly.org/mn-news/small-organization-big-job/)). The U.S. Korean Rights Group, with a Twin Cities resident, Rob Anderson, as vice president, represents U.S. adoptees in talks with the Korean government and collected video messages for a September 2026 conference in Seoul ([Korean Quarterly](https://koreanquarterly.org/mn-news/small-organization-big-job/); [Korean Quarterly, Aug. 1, 2026](https://koreanquarterly.org/front_page_below_fold/a-call-for-faces-and-voices/)). Attorney Greg Luce, who works with intercountry adoptees who lack U.S. citizenship, spoke at a 2026 Minneapolis event co-sponsored by AK Connection ([Korean Quarterly, May 12, 2026](https://koreanquarterly.org/front_page_below_fold/a-fighter-in-their-corner/)).

**Debates inside the community.** Views differ on adoption itself. Sun Yung Shin argues that adoption "is often promoted as purely humanitarian" but can involve "widescale theft of people's lives" ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 11, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/twin-cities-things-to-do-sun-yung-shin-st-kates-selby-ave-jazz-cedarfest/)), while parent-founded camps describe their mission as building self-esteem and "celebrating Korea and adoption in Minnesota" ([Kamp Kimchee](https://kampkimchee.org/)). There are also generational and language lines: the Korean Presbyterian Church built an English ministry partly because, as one teenager put it, a fast Korean sermon is hard to follow ([CCX Media](https://ccxmedia.org/news/brooklyn-center-korean-church-celebrates-faith-culture/)).

## 10. Education

**Attainment.** Among Korean Minnesotans 25 and older, 95.9 percent finished high school and 63.6 percent hold a bachelor's degree or more ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/korean)). The state count, for ages 25 to 64, finds 68 percent with a bachelor's degree ([State Demographic Center](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)).

**Sejong Academy.** This tuition-free Korean immersion charter school, now at 2410 Stillwater Road E. in St. Paul, opened in September 2014 with kindergarten through grade 6 and now serves pre-K through grade 12, with International Baccalaureate programs authorized in 2022 and 2025 ([Sejong Academy](https://www.sejongacademy.org/); [MN Association of Charter Schools](https://mncharterschools.org/directory/location-detail.php?ID=2585)). Its founders imagined it serving Korean adoptees, but from the start most students were Karen; by 2025 about 90 percent were, and the school has more Karen-speaking students than nearly any district or charter in the state, Sahan Journal reported ([Sahan Journal, June 10, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/education/sejong-academy-maplewood-korean-immersion-karen-students/)). A kindergarten teacher there was part of Minnesota's first cohort of heritage language license candidates, which included Korean ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 6, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/education/minnesota-teachers-heritage-language-licenses-hmong-karen-somali/)).

**Weekend and summer schools.** The Korean Institute of Minnesota teaches Korean language and culture; its fall 2026 semester runs Sept. 19 to Dec. 5 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/data-insights/articles/importance-data-disaggregation-minnesotas-asian-communities); [AK Connection](https://akconnection.org/event/korean-institute-of-minnesota-fall-semester/)). The Korean Presbyterian Church runs language and culture classes ([CCX Media](https://ccxmedia.org/news/brooklyn-center-korean-church-celebrates-faith-culture/)). Concordia Language Villages runs Sup sogŭi Hosu, a Korean Language Village ([Concordia Language Villages](https://www.concordialanguagevillages.org/languages/korean/)).

**University.** The University of Minnesota calls its Korean language program the largest non-heritage Korean as a Foreign Language program in the U.S., driven largely by interest in Korean pop culture and in North Korea ([University of Minnesota](https://cla.umn.edu/asian-middle-eastern-studies/languages/korean)). MNopedia counts at least 14 doctoral dissertations and three master's theses on Korean adoption at the university ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/korean-transracial-adoption-minnesota)).

## 11. Health and wellbeing, broadly

**Coverage.** Four percent of Korean Minnesotans lack health insurance, give or take 1.7 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/korean)).

**Mental health.** The Korean Service Center, funded through the Minnesota Department of Health's Eliminating Health Disparities Initiative, works to reduce stigma around mental health among Korean immigrants, offers counseling, promotes the 988 crisis line, and teaches suicide-prevention programs such as QPR and safeTALK with NAMI Minnesota. It works on this with the Karen Organization of Minnesota, the Korean Adoptees Ministry Center and SEWA-AIFW ([Korean Service Center](https://www.kscmn.org/eliminating-health-dispariies-initiatives)). AK Connection runs a support circle for Korean adoptees with a licensed counselor, and Adoptee Hub lists holistic mental health care ([AK Connection, April 10, 2026](https://akconnection.org/2026/04/10/april-2026-updates-from-ak-connection/); [Sahan Journal, Sept. 26, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/kimchi-festival-korean-adoptee-hub-st-louis-park/)).

**Adoptee health.** Without family medical history, adoptees can miss earlier screening that a known family history would trigger, Heewon Lee explained at a 2024 adoption research conference at the University of Minnesota. She recommended clinical genetic testing over consumer tests, and a University of Minnesota pediatrician who is an adoptee advises patients on building a personal health history ([Sahan Journal, July 29, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/health/minnesota-adoption-genetics-family-health-history/)). The University of Minnesota's hospital system is home to the first adoption medical clinic in the U.S., established in 1986 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/korean-transracial-adoption-minnesota)).

**Traditional practice.** No Minnesota source on Korean traditional medicine clinics or on how Korean Minnesotans use them was found.

## 12. Disability

**Minnesota numbers.** Compass reports that 5.5 percent of Korean Minnesotans have a disability, give or take 1.2 points, and 5.0 percent of those under 65 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/korean)). The state's 2026 chartbook suppresses its Korean disability estimate because the sample is too small ([State Demographic Center](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). The survey counts people who report serious difficulty in six areas: vision, hearing, walking or climbing stairs, concentrating or remembering, self-care, and independent living ([State Demographic Center](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). Because only 2.6 percent of Korean Minnesotans are 65 or older, this rate should not be compared with older groups without accounting for age.

**A Minnesota study of adoptees.** A University of Minnesota study assessed adolescents adopted in infancy, drawn from three large Minnesota agencies, from 1998 to 2004. Most of the 514 international adoptees were born in South Korea, according to an NPR report on the findings ([NPR, May 6, 2008](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/90184184)). The authors found that most adopted teens were psychologically healthy. Being adopted roughly doubled the odds of contact with a mental health professional and of a disruptive behavior disorder diagnosis, while domestic adoptees had higher odds of externalizing disorders than international adoptees ([University of Minnesota, Keyes et al., 2008](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/the-mental-health-of-us-adolescents-adopted-in-infancy/)). The study cannot say why. Open questions include how much reflects early care before placement, how much reflects adoptive parents being more likely to seek help, and how much reflects experiences after placement. These are questions, not findings.

**Who defines disability here?** Questions worth asking include who gives a Korean-speaking family a label, in what language, and whether the family agrees with it. For adoptees, missing family history can change which screenings a doctor orders ([Sahan Journal, July 29, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/health/minnesota-adoption-genetics-family-health-history/)). What a label opens or closes, and whether a Korean-speaking family or an adoptee sees it the same way, was not studied in any Minnesota source found.

**Services.** The Korean Service Center's Grace Place serves people over 65 or with disabilities in independent apartments, and its caregiver program supports caregivers of people 60 and older or of any age with Alzheimer's disease or a related disorder ([Korean Service Center](https://www.kscmn.org/grace-place); [Korean Service Center](https://www.kscmn.org/caregiver-support)). AK Connection's Chuseok Festival began with a sensory-friendly hour ([AK Connection](https://akconnection.org/event/ak-connections-chuseok-festival/)).

**People.** Christine Sun Kim, a Korean American artist who is Deaf, uses American Sign Language in her art and talks about Deaf culture; her 2026 Walker Art Center exhibit offered a digital braille guide and audio descriptions ([Korean Quarterly, April 28, 2026](https://koreanquarterly.org/front_page_below_fold/deafness-heard-seen-and-understood/)). She is not a Minnesotan, but the exhibit was a Minnesota event covered by the community's paper.

**What was not found.** No Minnesota organization specifically for Korean families with disabilities, no Korean-language disability parent group, no Minnesota Department of Health or Human Services data on Korean Minnesotans with disabilities, and no Minnesota study of how Korean immigrant families understand disability were found.

## 13. What is changing now

**Adoption fraud findings (2025 to 2026).** South Korea's government admitted in 2025 that widespread corruption tainted hundreds of thousands of adoptions, and MPR News reported that Minnesota adoptees were "left reeling" ([MPR News, Nov. 26, 2025](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2025/11/26/mistaken-minnesotas-korean-adoptees-grapple-with-confessed-systemic-corruption)). Korean Quarterly reported that fraud was found mostly in files of adoptees placed in the 1970s and 1980s, now middle-aged ([Korean Quarterly, March 23, 2026](https://koreanquarterly.org/mn-news/small-organization-big-job/)).

**Immigration enforcement (early 2026).** During the federal "Operation Metro Surge" in the Twin Cities, Korean Quarterly reported the unlawful detention and later release of three Korean adoptees who are U.S. citizens, held on no grounds except looking Asian, according to the paper's account. Adoptee Hub held sessions on rights, safety and identification in January and February 2026; some adoptees asked whether relocating to Korea was an option ([Korean Quarterly, Jan. 29, 2026](https://koreanquarterly.org/front_page_below_fold/on-ice-action-and-walking-around-asian/)). A Korean Quarterly columnist, Chen Zhou, wrote that "my wife and I" decided to carry U.S. passports everywhere ([Korean Quarterly, Feb. 11, 2026](https://koreanquarterly.org/essays-and-columns/when-fear-replaces-freedom-in-minnesota/)), and Brook Mallak, a Korean-born adoptee and attorney, described retreating to a basement on Jan. 24, 2026, after seeing an idling SUV nearby ([Korean Quarterly, March 29, 2026](https://koreanquarterly.org/front_page_below_fold/the-quiet-strength-of-community/)). Sahan Journal reported in February 2026 that the administration had announced the end of the operation ([Sahan Journal, Feb. 18, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/lunar-new-year-horse-celebrate-minnesota/)). Some intercountry adoptees grew up without U.S. citizenship, and lawyers who serve them describe this as a live concern ([Korean Quarterly, May 12, 2026](https://koreanquarterly.org/front_page_below_fold/a-fighter-in-their-corner/)).

**Generations.** Adoptees' children and grandchildren are now old enough for their own programs; AK Connection held its first meetup "by and for" descendants of Korean adoptees in September 2026 ([AK Connection](https://akconnection.org/event/dokad-meetup/)). The Korean Service Center, meanwhile, is focused on aging immigrants ([Korean Service Center](https://www.kscmn.org/)).

**Commerce.** A possible H Mart in St. Paul (June 2026), the closure of Young Joni (2025), and Korean tenants at the new Asia Village in Blaine (2026) show Korean food and goods reaching beyond the community itself ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/foodie/h-mart-korean-asian-grocery-supermarket-st-paul-mn/); [Sahan Journal](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/asia-village-opens-blaine-northtown-mall/)). The University of Minnesota credits Korean pop culture with drawing students who have no Korean background to its language classes ([University of Minnesota](https://cla.umn.edu/asian-middle-eastern-studies/languages/korean)).

**Schools.** Sejong Academy, the state's Korean immersion school, now serves mostly Karen students and added a Karen New Year celebration in 2025 ([Sahan Journal, June 10, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/education/sejong-academy-maplewood-korean-immersion-karen-students/)).

## 14. Strengths and contributions

- **Institutions built by the community.** The Korean Service Center marked 35 years in 2026; AK Connection has run since 2000; Korean Culture Camp since 1977; the St. Andrew Kim Catholic community since 1973 ([Korean Service Center](https://www.kscmn.org/35th-anniversary); [AK Connection](https://akconnection.org/about/); [KCC](https://kccmn.org/); [Archdiocese](https://www.archspm.org/location/st-andrew-kim-catholic-church/)).
- **Public service and law.** Ramsey County credits its county attorney's office with changing how government responds to domestic violence, sex trafficking and sexual assault ([Ramsey County](https://www.ramseycountymn.gov/your-government/leadership/county-attorneys-office/meet-john-j-choi)).
- **Knowledge that changed a field.** MNopedia calls Minnesota's Korean adoptees "visible and important participants in cultural production and academic research" ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/korean-transracial-adoption-minnesota)).
- **Food and culture for everyone.** From Ann Kim's restaurants to the Mall of America Korean Culture Festival, Korean Minnesotans have shared their culture widely ([Twin Cities Business](https://tcbmag.com/tcb-100-people/ann-kim/); [KAAM](https://www.mnkorea.org/post/2026-korean-culture-festival)).
- **Solidarity across communities.** The Korean Service Center partners with Karen and South Asian organizations on mental health, and a Korean immersion school became a home for Karen refugee families ([Korean Service Center](https://www.kscmn.org/eliminating-health-dispariies-initiatives); [Sahan Journal](https://sahanjournal.com/education/sejong-academy-maplewood-korean-immersion-karen-students/)).
- **Scholarships.** MAKA's $385,000 in scholarships over three decades ([Korean Quarterly](https://koreanquarterly.org/mn-news/scholarship-support-for-three-decades/)).

## What could not be established

- A government count that separates Korean adoptees from Korean immigrants. Community estimates range from about 10,000 to 15,000 adoptees; a figure of 15,000 to 20,000 that is sometimes cited could not be traced to a source.
- Occupations, industries, union membership and household wealth for Korean Minnesotans.
- Any Korean chamber of commerce or business association, and any Korean-owned law, accounting, insurance, tax, travel or real estate firm in Minnesota.
- Current, dated signs of operation for several long-known restaurants and groceries (Sole Cafe, Mirror of Korea, Hoban, Dong Yang, K-Town, Juche, a Snelling Avenue grocery). They may well be open; the pages that could be read did not prove it.
- A Korean Buddhist temple in Minnesota, and any survey of Korean Minnesotans' religious affiliation.
- A Minnesota observance of March 1 or Aug. 15, or a Minnesota Korean American Day proclamation.
- A Minnesota disability organization or study focused on Korean families, and state agency disability data for Korean Minnesotans.
- Korean traditional medicine clinics in Minnesota.
- Pages that could not be opened: the JangmiArts and Korean Cultural Association websites (blocked), the Adoption Literacy site (blocked), several Korean church websites listed by the Korean American Association (addresses no longer resolve), the Minnesota Reformer, Axios Twin Cities, a University of Minnesota Conservancy report on the Minnesota Project (blocked), and a Korean Quarterly article on an accessible Korean adoptee tour (blocked).
