# Latino communities in Minnesota

This page covers what is shared across Latino Minnesota and the places where Latino life is most visible statewide. Separate pages go deeper on [Mexican](../mexican/profile.md), [Puerto Rican](../puerto-rican/profile.md), [Salvadoran](../salvadoran/profile.md), [Guatemalan](../guatemalan/profile.md), [Ecuadorian](../ecuadorian/profile.md), [Colombian](../colombian/profile.md) and [Cuban](../cuban/profile.md) Minnesotans. The Guatemalan and Salvadoran pages carry much of the Worthington story in detail.

## 1. People and place

**How many.** The American Community Survey for 2020 to 2024 counts 365,831 Hispanic or Latino Minnesotans, 6.4 percent of a state population of 5,739,445 ([U.S. Census Bureau, table B03003, published through Census Reporter](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B03003&geo_ids=04000US27)). The single-year 2024 survey puts it at 388,435, or 6.7 percent, give or take 803 people ([Census Bureau, table B03001, 2024 one-year](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B03001&geo_ids=04000US27)). The 2020 Census counted 345,640, up from 250,258 in 2010 and 143,382 in 2000, according to Comunidades Latinas Unidas En Servicio (CLUES) ([CLUES](https://clues.org/about/latinos-in-minnesota/)). In 1980 the share was 0.9 percent ([WCCO, Sept. 28, 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/good-question-amid-hispanic-heritage-month-whats-minnesotas-latino-history/)). These counts rest on how people answer a census question about ethnicity. People of any race can answer yes.

**A word with limits.** "Latino" and "Hispanic" are shared labels, not one people. The state's historical essay calls Latino a contested term, worked out among the groups who claim it ([MNopedia, last modified Jan. 14, 2026](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-minnesota)). Rodolfo Gutierrez of HACER, a Latino research group, said in 2025 that shared labels can hide differences of origin, education and income ([Ampers, March 12, 2025](https://ampers.org/latino-organizations-look-to-the-future-of-leadership/)). Ruby Azurdia-Lee, who led CLUES in 2023, has described a community that includes Afro-Latino and Indigenous people as well as people with fair skin ([WCCO, 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/good-question-amid-hispanic-heritage-month-whats-minnesotas-latino-history/)). Words differ too. The state council and CLUES now write "Latine" in some places, and a University of Minnesota department writes "Latinx" ([Minnesota Council on Latino Affairs 2025 report](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/mandated/261122.pdf); [CLUES calendar](https://clues.org/event-calendar/); [UMN Chicano & Latino Studies](https://cla.umn.edu/chicano-latino)).

Numbers can move fast and read differently by source. The 2024 single-year survey shows 10,304 Venezuelans, give or take 3,207, about twice the five-year average ([2024 one-year table](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B03001&geo_ids=04000US27)). Minnesota Compass profiles Hondurans separately at 9,409, give or take 2,059, with 52.1 percent in the Twin Cities and 47.9 percent in Greater Minnesota, both give or take about 11 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/honduran)). Compass has no profile for all Latinos together, and none for Venezuelan, Dominican or Peruvian Minnesotans ([Compass cultural communities list](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities)).

**Where.** About 64 percent live in the seven core Twin Cities counties: Hennepin 99,318, Ramsey 45,588, Dakota 38,684, Anoka 21,612, Washington 14,552, Scott 9,930 and Carver 5,624. Outside the metro, Olmsted has 9,814, Stearns 7,700, Nobles 7,594, Rice 7,105, Kandiyohi 6,484 and Mower 5,494. Nobles is 34.5 percent Latino and Watonwan 30.4 percent, the highest shares of any county ([Census Bureau, table B03003 by county, 2020 to 2024](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B03003&geo_ids=050|04000US27)).

Among cities, Minneapolis has about 43,051 and St. Paul about 29,082. Worthington (6,230, or 45.2 percent), Willmar (5,480, or 25.6 percent), Austin (4,924, or 18.7 percent) and West St. Paul (4,430, or 20.7 percent) are the focus below ([Census Bureau, focus cities, 2020 to 2024](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B03001,B19013I,B25003I,B17001I,B05003I,C15002I&geo_ids=16000US2771734,16000US2770420,16000US2702908,16000US2769700,16000US2758000,16000US2743000,16000US2754214,16000US2757040)). St. James is 47.0 percent Latino, Long Prairie 40.5 percent and Madelia 33.1 percent, with margins of error that matter in towns this size ([Census Bureau, selected towns, B03003](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B03003&geo_ids=16000US2757040,16000US2738060,16000US2739230)).

**Beyond the best-known towns.** The same survey, read for every Minnesota city, shows Latino life spread well past the places usually named. The suburbs hold large numbers: Bloomington about 9,904 (11.2 percent), Burnsville 7,934 (12.3 percent), Richfield 6,540 (17.8 percent), Brooklyn Center 5,364 (16.5 percent), South St. Paul 3,660 (17.8 percent) and Columbia Heights 3,392 (15.3 percent). In Greater Minnesota, Rochester has about 8,020 (6.6 percent), Faribault 3,345 (13.6 percent), Northfield 2,747 (13.2 percent), Albert Lea 2,694 (14.7 percent, give or take 129), Mankato 2,370 (5.2 percent), Owatonna 2,279 (8.6 percent) and Glencoe 1,125 (19.8 percent). Suburban margins of error run from several hundred to about 1,100 people ([Census Bureau, table B03003, all Minnesota places, 2020 to 2024](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B03003&geo_ids=160|04000US27)).

**St. James and Watonwan County.** In St. James and nearby Butterfield, most public school students are Latino, MPR News reported in March 2026 ([MPR News, March 5, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/03/05/greater-minnesota-schools-felt-the-fear-as-ice-presence-surged)). Convivencia Hispana, a volunteer network started by co-workers at the Smithfield Foods plant, says its members trace roots to Mexico, Honduras, Guatemala and El Salvador. In a profile written during the pandemic, it credited itself with more Latino seats on town committees and more school interpreters, and described a Golden Age group for Latino elders ([LatinoLEAD, undated](https://www.latinoleadmn.org/post/greater-mn-story-convivencia-hispana)). The University of Minnesota Morris holds Spanish-language oral histories recorded in St. James in 2022 ([Uniting Cultures oral history collection](https://digitalcommons.morris.umn.edu/unitingcultures/27)).

**Albert Lea.** About 14.7 percent of Albert Lea is Latino in the 2020 to 2024 survey ([Census Bureau, all places](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B03003&geo_ids=160|04000US27)). Recent public record is mostly about enforcement, described in section 13.

**West Side of St. Paul.** The West Side Flats held a Mexican settlement from the early 1900s, with Luis Garzón's grocery as a gathering place from the early 1920s and the Anáhuac mutual aid society from 1922. Our Lady of Guadalupe began as a mission in 1931. At least 15 percent of the settlement was forcibly sent to Mexico that same year, by MNopedia's chronology. The Flats were cleared in the 1960s ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-minnesota)). The neighborhood group says 16 percent of today's residents are foreign-born and 53 percent are people of color, with no Latino share given ([West Side Community Organization](https://www.wsco.org/)). The commercial district is known as District del Sol, covering Cesar Chavez, Wabasha and Robert streets ([St. Paul Voice, Aug. 2026](https://www.stpaulpublishing.com/new-west-side-brand-unveiled-vote-soon-to-rename-cesar-chavez-street/)). No census count for the West Side alone could be read. The city of West St. Paul, next door to the south, is a separate place.

**Lake Street, Minneapolis.** Latino families in south Minneapolis go back generations. John Pacheco, president of the Latino Chamber of Commerce in 2023, grew up there in the 1960s after grandparents came from Mexico in the 1920s ([WCCO, 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/good-question-amid-hispanic-heritage-month-whats-minnesotas-latino-history/)). Sagrado Corazón de Jesús opened in south Minneapolis in 1994 and Mercado Central on Lake Street in 1999, by MNopedia's dates. In the city overall, 23,272 people name Mexican origin and 6,645 name Ecuadorian, give or take 1,441, the largest Ecuadorian count of any city checked ([Census Bureau, focus cities](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B03001,B19013I,B25003I,B17001I,B05003I,C15002I&geo_ids=16000US2771734,16000US2770420,16000US2702908,16000US2769700,16000US2758000,16000US2743000,16000US2754214,16000US2757040)).

**Worthington.** Most of the 6,230 Latino residents name Mexican (2,842, give or take 509) or Central American origin (2,615, give or take 507), including 1,714 Guatemalans and 527 Salvadorans; 40 percent are foreign-born ([Census Bureau, focus cities](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B03001,B19013I,B25003I,B17001I,B05003I,C15002I&geo_ids=16000US2771734,16000US2770420,16000US2702908,16000US2769700,16000US2758000,16000US2743000,16000US2754214,16000US2757040)). A University of Minnesota capstone study dates Latino immigrant arrival in its four rural study cities to between 1990 and 2000 ([Humphrey School and Center for Rural Policy, May 2021](https://www.ruralmn.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Building-Community-Embracing-Difference-_-2021-_Gehlen-et-al.pdf)). A 2001 state rural study found Latinos were 19.3 percent of Worthington in the 2000 Census ([Center for Rural Policy and HACER, Dec. 2001](https://hacer-mn.org/staging/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/vitalitylatinocommunitiesruralminnesota.pdf)). In a personal essay, Andrea Duarte-Alonso, who grew up there, describes neighbors from El Salvador and Guatemala and parents working at the JBS plant, formerly Swift ([Barn Raiser, July 4, 2024](https://barnraisingmedia.com/immigrants-find-a-home-rural-minnesota-worthington/)). Nearly two-thirds of Worthington High School students were Latino in January 2024, by Sahan Journal's count ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/education/worthington-school-board-votes-remove-lgbtq-puerto-rican-flags/)). Jaime Salinas, who leads the city's chamber, Forward Worthington, described in February 2026 "a real mixed pot of Latino nationalities," naming Mexicans, Guatemalans and Salvadorans, with newer Lao and Haitian arrivals ([MPR News, Feb. 23, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2026/02/23/worthington-chamber-ceo-says-ice-surge-led-to-fear-economic-disruption)).

**Willmar.** The Minneapolis Fed reported Latinos in Willmar since at least the 1920s as seasonal farm workers, with many settling after 1970s layoffs and more arriving after Hormel bought Jennie-O in 1986 ([Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis, 2011](https://www.minneapolisfed.org/article/2011/little-mogadishu-in-the-allamerica-city-somali-entrepreneurship-in-willmar-minnesota)). Mexican origin leads at 2,882, then 917 Hondurans, 657 Guatemalans and 381 Nicaraguans, the last three with wide margins ([Census Bureau, focus cities](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B03001,B19013I,B25003I,B17001I,B05003I,C15002I&geo_ids=16000US2771734,16000US2770420,16000US2702908,16000US2769700,16000US2758000,16000US2743000,16000US2754214,16000US2757040)). The 2021 capstone cites labor demand at the Jennie-O plant, the city's largest employer, as a main draw since 1989 ([Humphrey School, 2021](https://www.ruralmn.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Building-Community-Embracing-Difference-_-2021-_Gehlen-et-al.pdf)).

**Austin.** MPR reported in 2010 that immigrant workers came for meatpacking jobs after the 1985 to 1986 Hormel strike and were staying permanently ([MPR News, Aug. 2010](https://minnesota.publicradio.org/projects/2010/08/austin-at-a-crossroads/)). Mexican origin leads (3,264, give or take 567), followed by Guatemalan (636) and Salvadoran (242), with wide margins; 45 percent are foreign-born ([Census Bureau, focus cities](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B03001,B19013I,B25003I,B17001I,B05003I,C15002I&geo_ids=16000US2771734,16000US2770420,16000US2702908,16000US2769700,16000US2758000,16000US2743000,16000US2754214,16000US2757040)).

**How people came.** MNopedia traces the first lasting Latino home in Minnesota to Luis Garzón, a musician who fell ill while touring in Minneapolis in 1886. Sugar beet recruiting then drew Mexican and Tejano workers north, more than 7,000 a year by 1928. Some moved into South St. Paul meatpacking or railroad jobs ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-minnesota)). University of Minnesota professor Jimmy Patino ties 1980s Central American turmoil, and Cuban settlement in the upper Midwest, to the point when a mostly Mexican community grew more varied ([WCCO, 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/good-question-amid-hispanic-heritage-month-whats-minnesotas-latino-history/)). Not everyone's story is one of immigration. MNopedia notes that many Latinos' U.S. citizenship traces back to 1848, when much of today's United States was still part of Mexico ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-minnesota)).

**Generations and languages.** The median age is 26.0, and 35.8 percent are under 18 ([table B01002I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B01002I&geo_ids=04000US27); [table B05003I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B05003I&geo_ids=04000US27)). Foreign-born residents number 113,985, or 31.2 percent; 92 percent of Latino children here were born in the United States ([table B05003I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B05003I&geo_ids=04000US27)). Among people 5 and older, 58.7 percent speak Spanish at home, 40.8 percent speak only English and 25.0 percent speak English less than "very well." Another 0.5 percent, about 1,772 people, speak some other language at home ([table B16006](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B16006&geo_ids=04000US27)). That last figure may hide Indigenous languages, since census categories do not separate them here. Schools give a second view. In 2024-25, 64,526 Minnesota students had Spanish as their primary home language, and 41,454 of them were English learners, up from 29,361 Spanish-speaking English learners in 2020. Districts reported 348 home languages in all, but the state's published report lists only the top 12, so any count of K'iche', Mam, Mixtec or other Indigenous languages sits in data tables the report does not reproduce ([Minnesota Department of Education, English Learners in Minnesota 2024-25, via Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/other/261381.pdf)). Hints of Indigenous identity appear in reporting: Justin Ek, a founder of Mankato's Day of the Dead festival, was described by the Associated Press in 2025 as an Indigenous Latino artist ([AP via KPRC, Nov. 1, 2025](https://www.click2houston.com/news/national/2025/11/01/joyful-day-of-the-dead-commemorations-rally-us-latino-communities-despite-immigration-raid-fears/)), and a Worthington restaurant owner from San Marcos, Guatemala, told The Globe he had served customers "who spoke Spanish and dialect" ([The Globe, May 2026](https://www.dglobe.com/news/local/la-eterna-primavera-to-open-friday-in-worthington)). No Minnesota count of Mixtec, K'iche', Mam or Garifuna speakers could be found.

## 2. All walks of life

All figures here are for Latino Minnesotans from the 2020 to 2024 survey, in 2024 dollars, unless noted. Margins of error appear where they change the reading.

- **Well-off households.** About 9,866 households, or 9.6 percent, earn $200,000 or more, give or take 804 households. Just over a third, 34.7 percent, earn $100,000 or more ([table B19001I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B19001I&geo_ids=04000US27)). Wealth, as distinct from income, is not measured for Latino Minnesotans. The Minnesota Budget Project, reviewing Prosperity Now's scorecard in 2021, found Minnesota household wealth data only for white households, whose 2016 median net worth was $201,600 against $185,000 for all households ([Minnesota Budget Project, June 7, 2021](https://mnbudgetproject.org/?p=930)). Home ownership, the main store of family wealth, is reported below. Public examples of long business success include Boca Chica, open on the West Side since 1964, and El Burrito Mercado, serving since 1979 ([Boca Chica](https://www.bocachicarestaurant.com/); [El Burrito Mercado](https://www.elburritomercado.com/)). Painter Jimmy Longoria's work hangs in the homes and offices of national figures, and he has lived by his art since 2010 ([MPR News, Oct. 21, 2024](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2024/10/21/at-70-artist-jimmy-longoria-still-paints-with-ambition)).
- **Professionals and owners.** A quarter of adults 25 and older, 24.9 percent, hold a bachelor's degree or more, against 41.0 percent of white non-Latino adults ([table C15002I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=C15002I&geo_ids=04000US27); [table C15002H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=C15002H&geo_ids=04000US27)). Management, business, science and arts jobs are the largest single group, at 30.4 percent of workers ([table C24010I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=C24010I&geo_ids=04000US27)). Twin Cities Business named 19 Hispanic leaders in 2024 from nominations, among them a Realtor who moved from Mexico in 2001 after an engineering career, a third-generation lawyer of Honduran and Salvadoran family who founded a St. Paul firm in 2016, a Bremer Bank community banker serving East Lake Street, the founder of the Rayito de Sol Spanish immersion early learning centers, a Chilean-born finance director, a Best Buy territory executive and an Allina Health vice president who grew up the daughter of migrant farm workers ([Twin Cities Business, 2024 Notable Hispanic Leaders](https://tcbmag.com/notable_category/2024-notable-hispanic-leaders/)). Henry Jiménez leads Propel Nonprofits, a community lender ([Twin Cities Business, 2024](https://tcbmag.com/notable_category/2024-notable-hispanic-leaders/); [MPR News, Sept. 9, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2026/09/09/hispanic-heritage-awards-come-to-minneapolis-recognize-the-people-of-minnesota)). Other visible careers include CLUES chief executive Liliana Letran-Garcia, Latino Economic Development Center director Alma Flores, Minnesota Council on Latino Affairs chair Dr. Ronald Salazar and executive director Sonia Reyes ([CLUES](https://clues.org/); [Hola America News, Feb. 18, 2026](https://holaamericanews.com/minnesota-latino-leaders-press-lawmakers-to-protect-immigrants-after-ice-occupation/); [Council report](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/mandated/261122.pdf)).
- **Middle-income and working families.** Median household income is $74,040, give or take $1,541, against $92,471 for white non-Latino households and $89,062 statewide ([B19013I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B19013I&geo_ids=04000US27); [B19013H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B19013H&geo_ids=04000US27); [B19013](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B19013&geo_ids=04000US27)). Owners hold 54.2 percent of homes, against 77.1 percent for white non-Latino households ([B25003I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B25003I&geo_ids=04000US27); [B25003H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B25003H&geo_ids=04000US27)). Among people 16 to 64, 78.9 percent of men and 71.3 percent of women are employed ([C23002I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=C23002I&geo_ids=04000US27)).
- **People in poverty.** Poverty stands at 14.8 percent, 53,122 people, give or take 3,548, about twice the white non-Latino rate of 7.2 percent. For children it is 17.0 percent, and for people 65 and older, 19.3 percent ([B17001I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B17001I&geo_ids=04000US27); [B17001H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B17001H&geo_ids=04000US27)). About 13.2 percent of households earn under $25,000 ([B19001I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B19001I&geo_ids=04000US27)). Nearly a quarter of adults, 24.6 percent, did not finish high school ([C15002I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=C15002I&geo_ids=04000US27)).

**Place changes the picture.** In the focus cities, median household income runs from about $58,935 (give or take $7,971) in Worthington and $59,637 in Willmar to $71,674 in Minneapolis. Poverty runs from 11.1 percent in Willmar to 22.3 percent in Worthington and 26.8 percent in Austin, where margins are wide. Home ownership is 64.6 percent in Austin and 35.6 percent in Minneapolis. Bachelor's degrees reach 32.3 percent of Latino adults in Minneapolis and 3.3 percent in Austin ([Census Bureau, focus cities](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B03001,B19013I,B25003I,B17001I,B05003I,C15002I&geo_ids=16000US2771734,16000US2770420,16000US2702908,16000US2769700,16000US2758000,16000US2743000,16000US2754214,16000US2757040)).

**Hardship and strength sit side by side.** HACER found 18,682 Latino adults 65 and older statewide in its 2026 work on older adults and caregivers, a small but growing share of Minnesota's older people ([HACER, 2026](https://hacer-mn.org/addressing-the-economic-realities-of-latino-older-adults-and-caregivers-in-minnesota/)). That count is higher than the 15,801 older people in the five-year table used in section 11, which covers an earlier span, so read both as estimates.

## 3. Work and the economy

**Today's jobs.** Of 180,465 employed Latino Minnesotans, 30.4 percent work in management, business, science and arts, 22.7 percent in service, 20.7 percent in production and moving goods, 15.1 percent in sales and office work, and 11.1 percent in construction, natural resources and maintenance. Men and women differ sharply. Among men, 18,681 work in construction and maintenance jobs, against 1,315 women ([table C24010I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=C24010I&geo_ids=04000US27)).

**Fields and packing plants.** Minnesota employers used the Bracero Program from 1943 to 1964. Centro Campesino organized in southeastern Minnesota in 1997 to support rural Latino workers. By the 2010s large clusters of Latino families lived near meat and poultry plants ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-minnesota)). Worthington's JBS plant is the town's largest employer, by Duarte-Alonso's account ([Barn Raiser, 2024](https://barnraisingmedia.com/immigrants-find-a-home-rural-minnesota-worthington/)). In Austin, the Quality Pork Processors workforce was reported in 2011 as mostly Hispanic ([Mother Jones, June 2011](https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2011/06/hormel-spam-pig-brains-disease/)). A 2023 story profiled one Austin family, with a mother and relatives at the Hormel plant, showing how long these ties can run ([WCCO, 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/good-question-amid-hispanic-heritage-month-whats-minnesotas-latino-history/)).

**Labor.** MNopedia names the Service Employees International Union, UNITE HERE, the food and commercial workers union and CTUL in Latino labor organizing. It also records a large April 9, 2006 Capitol rally of about 35,000 people for immigration reform ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-minnesota)). Centro de Trabajadores Unidos en la Lucha organizes low-wage workers, including in construction, from Chicago Avenue in Minneapolis ([CTUL](https://ctul.net/)). COPAL runs a workers' center, Centro de Trabajadores Primero de Mayo, on East Lake Street, with offices in Mankato and Rochester ([COPAL](https://copalmn.org/)). The Labor Council for Latin American Advancement and UNITE HERE Local 17 spoke for workers during the winter 2025 to 2026 enforcement surge ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 29, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/minnesota-workers-employers-navigate-immigration-enforcement/)). Two unions show where Latino workers are concentrated, though neither publishes an ethnic count. In 2007, officials of SEIU Local 26, the Twin Cities janitors' union, told MPR that most of its members were ethnic minorities, "mostly Latino immigrants," with 10 percent Somali ([MPR News, Feb. 1, 2007](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2007/02/01/janitors)); the local now reports more than 8,000 members in cleaning, security and airport work ([Minnesota SEIU](https://www.seiumn.org/mn-seiu-locals)). No count of Latino union members in Minnesota could be found.

**Owners.** The Latino Chamber of Commerce says the state has more than 12,000 Latino-owned businesses; Sahan Journal reported 14,000 in February 2025 ([Latino Chamber](https://latinochambermn.com/); [Sahan Journal, Feb. 21, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/minnesota-latino-businesses-statewide-support/)). Neither gives a date for the count, so read them as rough. Dated federal figures, compiled by the state's Emerging Entrepreneur Board from the Census Bureau's Annual Business Survey, are lower for firms with employees and close to 14,000 for one-person firms: 1,419 Hispanic-owned employer businesses in 2021, or 1.3 percent of the state's employer businesses, and about 14,000 Hispanic-owned businesses without employees in 2020, or 3.4 percent. The report set these against a Hispanic share of the population of 5.8 to 6.1 percent ([Emerging Entrepreneur Board annual report, 2024](https://mn.gov/deed/assets/emerging-entrepreneurs-board-annual-report-2024_tcm1045-651926.pdf)). The Latino Economic Development Center calls itself the only Latino-focused community lender in Minnesota and the Upper Midwest. It also backs regenerative farming and is converting a Payne Avenue building into a food hall, Plaza del Sol ([LEDC](https://www.ledcmn.org/)). HACER has studied Latina entrepreneurs through focus groups and interviews ([HACER, 2024](https://hacer-mn.org/empowering-latina-entrepreneurs-in-minnesota/)). A 2009 state council report gives old but rare figures, including Mercado Central's 45 businesses and more than $12 million in 2009 sales ([Chicano Latino Affairs Council, 2009](https://mn.gov/mcla/assets/A%20Closer%20Look%20at%20Minnesota%20Latino%20Workforce%20and%20Business%20Development_tcm1099-295204.pdf)).

## 5. Religion and spiritual life

**Catholic roots, and a wider field.** Spanish Mass is widespread but uneven across the state. In an August 2024 survey of Catholic dioceses, the Archdiocese of St. Paul and Minneapolis reported 24 of its 187 parishes offering Mass in Spanish; the Diocese of Winona-Rochester reported 11 of 96, St. Cloud 9 of 132, New Ulm 6 of 62, Crookston 2 of 67 and Duluth none of 80. That is 52 of 624 parishes statewide. St. Cloud also reported 123 parishes with a Latino presence but no Spanish Mass ([U.S. Conference of Catholic Bishops, Diocesan Survey on Parishes and Hispanic/Latino Ministry, Aug. 2024](https://www.usccb.org/resources/Diocesan%20Survey%20on%20Parishes%20and%20HispanicLatino%20Ministry%202024.pdf)). Our Lady of Guadalupe on Concord Street in St. Paul began in 1931 as a mission in a rented storefront, moved to its site in 1961 after the Flats were cleared, and lists four Spanish Masses a week ([Archdiocese of St. Paul and Minneapolis](https://www.archspm.org/location/our-lady-of-guadalupe-catholic-church)). It was named a diocesan shrine in December 2019, the only one in the archdiocese, and its feast Mass that year drew about 700 people ([The Catholic Spirit, Dec. 13, 2019](https://thecatholicspirit.com/news/local-news/st-paul-parish-named-diocesan-shrine-to-our-lady-of-guadalupe/)). Incarnation-Sagrado Corazón de Jesús on Pleasant Avenue in south Minneapolis lists a 1 p.m. Sunday Spanish Mass ([Incarnation](https://www.incarnationmpls.org/)). Queen of Angels in Austin marked the Guadalupe feast in 2019 with Mass, mariachi and chinelos dancers ([MPR News, Dec. 16, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/12/16/photos-austin-community-marks-the-feast-of-our-lady-of-guadalupe)).

**Who gets a Mass.** Access varies. The Diocese of St. Cloud ended the weekly Spanish Mass at St. Joseph's in Waite Park in June 2024, saying no Spanish-speaking priest could replace the one reassigned. More than 100 people marched in protest that July ([Minnesota Reformer via St. Cloud Live, Aug. 1, 2024](https://www.stcloudlive.com/news/local/the-only-spanish-language-mass-in-waite-park-got-canceled-the-congregation-is-fighting-back)). Whether the Mass later returned could not be established. In Worthington, by contrast, the Associated Press found in October 2024 an overflow crowd at the second Spanish-language Mass of the weekend at St. Mary's Catholic Church, with music from one of 10 Latino choirs. Dagoberto Mendez, who directs the Guatemalan choir Nueva Inspiración, said belonging to something familiar was a relief; a longtime English-speaking parishioner said some would like bilingual Masses, and the pastor, the Rev. Tim Biren, spoke of bridging different pastoral needs and worship styles. A copy of the Cristo Negro de Esquipulas, venerated in Guatemala, hangs in the entry. At Indian Lake Baptist Church outside town, the pastor is the Rev. Lucio Berumen, who is Mexican and serves both its English and Karen congregations ([AP via WDIV, Oct. 31, 2024](https://www.clickondetroit.com/news/national/2024/10/31/in-amharic-karen-and-spanish-worship-is-like-home-for-migrants-in-heartland-town/)). A 2001 rural study found that some churches led cross-cultural contact while others kept separate congregations ([Center for Rural Policy and HACER, 2001](https://hacer-mn.org/staging/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/vitalitylatinocommunitiesruralminnesota.pdf)).

**Protestant and evangelical congregations.** Dios Habla Hoy in south Minneapolis, started in 2015, is a bilingual church where its pastor, Sergio Amezcua, says about 90 percent of members are Latino and about half U.S.-born. He describes himself as a conservative who has felt let down by federal actions in Minneapolis, a reminder that politics and faith here do not line up in one way ([WBUR On Point, Jan. 28, 2026](https://www.wbur.org/onpoint/2026/01/28/minneapolis-is-standing-up-to-ice)). St. Paul's-San Pablo Lutheran in Minneapolis began as a Swedish congregation and now worships in Spanish and English, with a statue of Our Lady of Guadalupe at the front ([Associated Press via Rapid City Post, Jan. 12, 2026](https://rapidcitypost.com/a-minneapolis-church-holds-a-service-of-lament-and-hope-amid-fears-over-immigration-crackdown/)). In 2003, Misión El Santo Niño Jesús in St. Paul was described as the Episcopal diocese's only Hispanic church ([MPR, Dec. 12, 2003](https://archive.mpr.org/stories/2003/12/12/mexican-minnesotans-celebrate-our-lady-of-guadalupe)). Its current status was not confirmed.

**Faith and service.** Fe y Justicia, a faith-based nonprofit, worked on health coverage changes ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 2, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/health/undocumented-immigrants-healthcare-minnesotacare/)). Someone working with Latino families told the state in 2026 that many families seek out trusted faith leaders before formal mental health systems; that is one person's experience, not a measured finding ([Olmstead Plan community input, May 2026](https://mn.gov/olmstead/assets/SPAN_HACER%20Public%20Comment%20Report_6-3%20plain%20language%20edit_tcm1143-753621.pdf)). No Minnesota survey of Latino religious affiliation, including people of no faith or Latino Muslims, could be found. The only related figure read was national: about 43 percent of U.S. Latinos identify as Catholic, as cited in the 2024 Reformer story. The parish counts above measure where Spanish Mass is offered, not how many Latino Minnesotans attend, and nothing found counts Latino Protestant or evangelical congregations in the state.

## 7. Family, home and daily life

Latino Minnesotans live in about 102,755 households, averaging 2.9 people: 3.14 among owners and 2.61 among renters ([B25010I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B25010I&geo_ids=04000US27)). About 68.6 percent are family households, 43.2 percent married couples, 15.9 percent led by a woman with no spouse present, and 22.4 percent one person living alone ([B11001I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B11001I&geo_ids=04000US27)). That last number complicates any picture of Latino homes as always crowded with kin.

Food, music and childhood memory appear most vividly in first-person accounts. Duarte-Alonso remembers neighborhood picnics with frijoles charros and Latin music on Galena Street in Worthington, and describes the Worthington High School boys soccer team as mostly from immigrant families ([Barn Raiser, 2024](https://barnraisingmedia.com/immigrants-find-a-home-rural-minnesota-worthington/)). Novedades Krystal 2 at Midtown Global Market sells quinceañera dresses ([Midtown Global Market](https://www.midtownglobalmarket.org/merchants)). Centro Tyrone Guzmán's Wise Elders, Wise Elders Families and Respite programs show aging and caregiving as live concerns in Minneapolis ([Centro](https://www.centromn.org/)). HACER runs Familias Fuertes, a bilingual family communication program ([HACER](https://hacer-mn.org/)). Minnesota sources on marriage or household customs by origin group were not found, and a shared Latino pattern should not be assumed.

## 9. Civic and political life

**The state council.** The Minnesota Council on Latino Affairs was created in 1978 as the Chicano Latino Affairs Council and renamed in 2015 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-minnesota)). In 2025 it supported nine bills and tracked more than 50, and it strongly opposed the end of MinnesotaCare for undocumented adults ([Council report](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/mandated/261122.pdf)).

**Coalitions and organizing.** CLUES, founded in 1981 on the West Side, expanded to Willmar and Austin in 2017 and reports more than 140 employees, 700 volunteers and 30,000 people served a year ([CLUES, about](https://www.clues.org/about)). The Minnesota Latino Leadership Alliance (MiLLA) brings together Latino-led groups. In February 2026 it asked lawmakers for the STAR Act, relief for immigrant-owned businesses and protection of nonprofit funding ([Hola America News](https://holaamericanews.com/minnesota-latino-leaders-press-lawmakers-to-protect-immigrants-after-ice-occupation/)). Unidos MN, born from the DREAMer movement, works on immigration, education and climate ([Unidos MN](https://unidos-mn.org/)). COPAL reports engaging about 70,000 community stakeholders and is raising $20 million for a Latino Center for Community Engagement ([COPAL](https://copalmn.org/)). The West Side Community Organization led 2026 renaming and enforcement-response work ([WSCO](https://www.wsco.org/)).

**In office.** MNopedia lists Conrad Vega as the first Latino legislator in 1977. Sources differ on the first Latina: MNopedia gives Edwina Garcia in both 1991 and 1998 in different parts of the essay, while Sahan Journal describes Patricia Torres Ray as the first Latina elected, in 2006 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-minnesota); [Sahan Journal, 2022](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/minnesota-legislature-senate-house-latina-lawmakers/)). María Isa Pérez-Vega, of Puerto Rican descent, has served District 65B since 2023 and filed for Ramsey County Commissioner in 2026 ([Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/legdb/fulldetail?id=15574)). A full list of current Latino officeholders could not be built.

**Debates inside the community.** The biggest open debate is the name of Cesar Chavez Street (section 13). Others are about labels, about who leads, and about partisanship. HACER's Gutierrez has spoken of the difficulty of unity across differences ([Ampers, 2025](https://ampers.org/latino-organizations-look-to-the-future-of-leadership/)). Pastor Amezcua's self-description as a conservative is a useful reminder that Latino politics in Minnesota is not uniform. No Minnesota poll of Latino voters could be found.

## 10. Education

**K-12.** St. Paul's Adams Spanish Immersion grew by more than a quarter in two years and was the district's second-largest elementary as of January 2024; Wellstone also runs dual-language Spanish ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 23, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/education/st-paul-schools-halt-enrollment-slide-hmong-spanish-east-african-programs/)). Academia In Lak'ech, a charter school on LaCrosse Avenue on St. Paul's East Side, posts a 2026-27 calendar. Its site still shows an email domain for the former Academia César Chávez name, and Sahan Journal reports a St. Paul charter school renamed itself after the 2026 allegations ([Academia In Lak'ech](https://www.academiainlakech.org); [Sahan Journal, July 17, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/saint-paul-cesar-chavez-street-renaming/)). The University's partner list still shows the older name, as well as El Colegio in Minneapolis, a high school serving students in English and Spanish ([UMN community partners](https://cla.umn.edu/chicano-latino/engagement-collaboration/community-partners)). El Colegio's own site could not be opened, but AP reported its students' Day of the Dead altars in November 2025 ([AP via KPRC, Nov. 1, 2025](https://www.click2houston.com/news/national/2025/11/01/joyful-day-of-the-dead-commemorations-rally-us-latino-communities-despite-immigration-raid-fears/)).

**Statewide.** About one in 10 Minnesota public school students identified as Latino or Hispanic in 2022, the Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis found from state enrollment files ([Minneapolis Fed, Latino/Hispanic student snapshot, 2022](https://www.minneapolisfed.org/~/media/assets/articles/2022/educational-outcomes-and-minnesotas-economy-latino-hispanic-student-snapshot/educational-outcomes-latino-hispanic.pdf?la=en)). Hispanic or Latino students in the class of 2025 graduated in four years at 72.4 percent, up from 71.7 percent, against 89.9 percent for white students and 84.9 percent statewide, according to the state education department as reported by FOX 9 ([FOX 9, April 3, 2026](https://fox9.com/news/minnesota-high-school-graduation-rates-2025)).

**In Worthington,** students of color were more than 80 percent of about 3,300 K-12 students in 2024, and Spanish was the first language of most children, per the Associated Press. About 800 unaccompanied children were released to sponsors in Nobles County over ten years, according to federal data compiled by the Migration Policy Institute ([AP via KESQ, Oct. 31, 2024](https://kesq.com/news/ap-national-news/2024/10/31/takeaways-from-aps-report-on-how-immigration-transformed-a-minnesota-farm-town/)). Duarte-Alonso recalls a school referendum that needed six tries before passing in 2019 ([Barn Raiser, 2024](https://barnraisingmedia.com/immigrants-find-a-home-rural-minnesota-worthington/)). In January 2024, the all-white school board voted to remove a Pride flag and a Puerto Rican flag from teacher José Morales Collazo's classroom at a school where nearly two-thirds of students are Latino; the one dissenting board member, immigration lawyer Erin Schutte Wadzinski, said the town had not fully integrated its changing population ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/education/worthington-school-board-votes-remove-lgbtq-puerto-rican-flags/)).

**College.** The University of Minnesota's Department of Chicano & Latino Studies dates to 1971, by MNopedia, and in 2026 named Cindy García a Waldfogel Scholar ([UMN](https://cla.umn.edu/chicano-latino)). St. Cloud State students won a Chicano/a studies minor after a 1995 hunger strike ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-minnesota)). The state's North Star Promise tuition program survived attempts in 2025 to restrict eligibility, which the council opposed ([Council report](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/mandated/261122.pdf)). LEDC runs a Latino Scholarship Fund ([LEDC](https://www.ledcmn.org/)).

**Parents and school systems.** La Oportunidad has run youth leadership and family programs since 1987 ([La Oportunidad](https://oportunidad.org/)). A 2025 state law requires districts to engage parents of students with disabilities who do not speak English, with translated materials and interpretation ([Council report](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/mandated/261122.pdf)). ## 11. Health and wellbeing, broadly

**Coverage.** About 16.3 percent of Latino Minnesotans lacked health insurance in the 2020 to 2024 survey: 21.8 percent of adults 19 to 64 and 9.0 percent of those under 19 ([C27001I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=C27001I&geo_ids=04000US27)). Those numbers predate a major change. MinnesotaCare ended for undocumented adults on Jan. 1, 2026, affecting about 15,000 people, by Sahan Journal's count, though children under 18 remain eligible ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 2, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/health/undocumented-immigrants-healthcare-minnesotacare/)). The state council describes the change as falling hard on Latino communities ([Council report](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/mandated/261122.pdf)). What has happened to care since has not been measured in anything that could be found.

**Clinics and services.** La Clinica opened on the West Side in 1969 through grassroots organizing ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-minnesota)); its current parent site could not be opened. CLUES runs Abriendo Caminos behavioral health clinic at 2600 East 25th Street in Minneapolis and a Family Wellness hub on Maria Avenue in St. Paul ([CLUES](https://clues.org/)). Centro Tyrone Guzmán offers maternal and infant programs, and a monthly clinic for Latino residents has met at St. Joseph's in Waite Park ([Centro](https://www.centromn.org/); [Sahan Journal, Jan. 2, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/health/undocumented-immigrants-healthcare-minnesotacare/)). COPAL has a health and wellness program, and HACER has a Long COVID program ([COPAL](https://copalmn.org/); [HACER](https://hacer-mn.org/)).

**Local evidence.** HACER surveyed 120 Latino residents in Watonwan County, including St. James and Madelia, and interviewed 13 ([HACER, 2025](https://hacer-mn.org/access-to-the-watonwan-county-health-system-for-latino-communities/)). HACER also worked on breast cancer education among Latinas in southern Minnesota ([HACER publications](https://hacer-mn.org/publications-reports/)). The state set up a health care interpreter workgroup in 2025 ([Council report](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/mandated/261122.pdf)).

**Traditional practice.** No Minnesota source documenting curanderos, sobadores or herbal practice could be read. The planned Latino Museum says it will include medicinal plant exhibits ([Minnesota Latino Museum](https://www.mnlatinomuseum.org/)).

## 12. Disability

**What the survey counts.** The American Community Survey finds a disability among 8.6 percent of Latino Minnesotans, about 31,226 people: 5.4 percent under 18, 9.1 percent ages 18 to 64 and 28.1 percent 65 and older. White non-Latino rates are 4.1, 9.5 and 27.8 percent, with an overall rate of 12.2 percent ([B18101I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B18101I&geo_ids=04000US27); [B18101H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B18101H&geo_ids=04000US27)). The lower overall Latino rate reflects, in part, a much younger population; the rates by age sit close together. The survey asks six questions in a set way. Whether they catch what families themselves name as disability is an open question.

**Autism and how children get counted.** In parts of Anoka, Hennepin and Ramsey counties in 2022, autism was identified in 40.9 per 1,000 Hispanic 8-year-olds, against 29.6 per 1,000 white children ([CDC, MMWR, 2025](https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/74/ss/ss7402a1.htm)). The University of Minnesota reports this as 1.4 times the white rate ([Institute on Community Integration, April 2025](https://ici.umn.edu/news/new-autism-prevalence-findings-in-minnesota)). What the difference means remains a question. It may reflect who gets evaluated, who is reached, or other things. The monitored area is a small metro slice, not the state, and no such figures exist for Worthington, Willmar or Austin.

**Barriers families have named.** A Minnesota Department of Health fact sheet reports that a state qualitative study of culturally diverse autism families named long waitlists, hard navigation, language barriers and transportation. It says fear about parents' immigration status made access harder, and that families sought respite from people in their own community ([MDH, June 2020](https://www.health.mn.gov/docs/communities/titlev/autism.pdf)). In May 2026, seven people connected to HACER commented on the state's draft Olmstead Plan. Five work in a disability-related field and one is a person with a disability. They described bilingual evaluations taking months or more than a year, families not seeing mental health conditions as disability, cost and fear of bills, and unfamiliarity with special education ([Olmstead Plan community input, May 2026](https://mn.gov/olmstead/assets/SPAN_HACER%20Public%20Comment%20Report_6-3%20plain%20language%20edit_tcm1143-753621.pdf)). These are a few people's experiences, offered in a public process.

**What a label opens or closes.** One question is who defines disability and when: a school, a doctor, a county or a family. Another is what a diagnosis costs a family who fears contact with government. A third is what a family loses when no evaluator speaks its language, whether Spanish or an Indigenous language. Minnesota evidence on these exists mostly as testimony, not numbers. No current figures could be found on special education rates by ethnicity in state reports, or on disability among Indigenous-language speakers from Latin America.

**Waivers and home care.** The fullest Minnesota evidence on disability services by ethnicity is a study for the Department of Human Services by researchers at Purdue University and the University of Minnesota, using Medicaid records from 2017 to 2019 and published in March 2021. Hispanic people, counted alone or with another category, were 3 percent of the older and disabled Medicaid population studied, and 63 percent of them lived in the Twin Cities. Among people under 65 with a recorded disability, 32 percent of Hispanic people were on the Developmental Disabilities waiver or another disability waiver, against 46 percent of white people, and 52 percent of Hispanic people used neither a waiver nor personal care assistance, the highest share of any group. Among Hispanic people 65 and older, 60 percent used no home and community-based service. Of Hispanic people screened for the Developmental Disabilities waiver who were not already on it, half ended up with neither a waiver nor personal care help afterward ([Arling and others, Comparison of Racial and Ethnic Groups in Level of Care Screenings and Use of HCBS, for Minnesota DHS, March 26, 2021](https://developtoolmn.org/dhs/assets/racial-and-ethnic-comparison-report-quantatitive_tcm1053-663962.pdf)). The authors wrote that they could not say why, since claims data say little about barriers to access, screening bias or service quality. Those gaps sit beside the family accounts above of long waits, language barriers and fear of contact with government, but the study does not link them, and the question stays open.

**What produced an impairment.** Some impairment has a documented workplace cause, and it deserves asking about without pinning it on any culture or people. At Quality Pork Processors in Austin, the Minnesota Department of Health in 2007 investigated a neurological illness among workers exposed to aerosolized pig brain material. Mother Jones reported 15 cases in the state's survey and 21 in a Mayo Clinic count, in a workforce it described as mostly Hispanic. It reported that six of 13 workers who filed claims were then let go over identity documents ([Mother Jones, June 2011](https://www.motherjones.com/politics/2011/06/hormel-spam-pig-brains-disease/)). No current count of meatpacking or farm injury among Latino Minnesotans could be found. Whether labor conditions, housing, or care delays shape disability here is a question the available evidence raises but does not settle.

**People and organizations.** PACER Center announced a 16-month project with Family Voices of Minnesota, funded by DHS in 2024, for Spanish- and Somali-language parent groups and materials ([PACER, July 17, 2024](https://www.pacer.org/?p=8448)). At the University's Institute on Community Integration, Andrea Castillo and Fanny Fernandez have worked as Latino community connectors in the Act Early project ([ICI, March 2024](https://ici.umn.edu/news/connecting-communities)). Centro Tyrone Guzmán offers a Respite program ([Centro](https://www.centromn.org/)). The Spanish-language family center discapacitados abriéndose caminos (d.a.c.) in South St. Paul still has a site, but its latest visible date is 2015, so its current work could not be confirmed ([d.a.c.](https://dacfamilycenter.org)). HACER's publication list opened here shows no report labeled as disability work ([HACER](https://hacer-mn.org/publications-reports/)).

## 13. What is changing now

**Enforcement and its aftermath.** A federal surge called Operation Metro Surge reached Minnesota in late 2025 and the winter of 2026. The federal government said in January that it had arrested 10,000 people, a figure Sahan Journal notes has been disputed ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 29, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/minnesota-workers-employers-navigate-immigration-enforcement/)). About 2,000 agents were sent, per the Associated Press in January ([AP, Jan. 12, 2026](https://rapidcitypost.com/a-minneapolis-church-holds-a-service-of-lament-and-hope-amid-fears-over-immigration-crackdown/)). Dios Habla Hoy's Sunday attendance fell from about 500 to 600 to about 80 in January, and its food delivery registrations passed 28,000 families, by its pastor's account ([WBUR, Jan. 28, 2026](https://www.wbur.org/onpoint/2026/01/28/minneapolis-is-standing-up-to-ice)). City officials cited about $129 million in St. Paul small business losses in January and February, and at least $203 million in one month's impact in Minneapolis ([Sahan Journal, April 29, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/cinco-de-mayo-st-paul-west-side-latino-business-recovery/)). In October 2026, a Mercado Central owner said recovery was underway but slow, with some businesses closed for good ([MPR News, Oct. 6, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/10/06/minneapolis-shops-invite-customers-for-latino-business-week-amid-ice-surge)). This is group-level reporting, and it says nothing about any one person.

**The surge in Greater Minnesota.** Reporting from outside the metro shows the same fear in smaller places, with local differences. In Willmar, more than 1,000 students stayed home during January and February 2026, and at the peak more than one in four were absent, said Superintendent Bill Adams, who said a teenager with legal residency was detained downtown for several days and a U.S. citizen student was stopped outside a district learning center. The district offered online learning, and February attendance later rose above average. In St. James, Superintendent Liam Dawson said nine or 10 enforcement vehicles were sometimes in town but attendance held near 95 percent; a St. James parent wrote to lawmakers that the family, despite holding legal documents, had not shopped for food in person since Dec. 20 ([MPR News, March 5, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/03/05/greater-minnesota-schools-felt-the-fear-as-ice-presence-surged)). Willmar's director of community growth, Pablo Obregon, said in March that agents had been present for weeks and that the city was preparing mental health providers for lasting effects; a Chilean-born outreach worker with legal residence said he carried his papers everywhere and stayed indoors for a time ([MPR News, March 26, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/03/26/ice-surge-in-willmar-created-unexpected-bonds-between-neighbors)). Hundreds marched against enforcement in Worthington on Feb. 1, 2026, after a local man was detained ([The Globe, Feb. 2026](https://www.dglobe.com/news/local/anti-ice-protest-march-in-worthington-shows-support-for-immigrants)), and Forward Worthington's Salinas said later that month that fear lingered though he knew of few businesses planning to close ([MPR News, Feb. 23, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2026/02/23/worthington-chamber-ceo-says-ice-surge-led-to-fear-economic-disruption)). In Albert Lea, police found agents doing a "knock and talk" at a home in January, and at a Feb. 9 city council meeting residents described children afraid to attend school ([KEYC, Jan. 17, 2026](https://www.keyc.com/2026/01/17/albert-lea-residents-report-suspicious-activity-police-find-ice-conducting-operations); [Albert Lea City Council minutes, Feb. 9, 2026](https://www.cityofalbertlea.org/wp-content/uploads/City-Council-Minutes-February-9-2026.pdf)). Even before the surge, in June 2025, a Convivencia Hispana volunteer in Watonwan County said some members had stepped back from public outreach out of caution ([MinnPost via Southern Minnesota News, June 2025](https://www.southernminn.com/faribault_daily_news/news/state/arbitrary-and-stupid-immigrant-advocates-in-southwestern-minnesota-press-on-despite-confusion-over-sanctuary-designations/article_59a29db4-a9f9-4c75-90c9-d74c8a52b70d.html)). No reporting from 2026 on Austin's Latino community could be found.

**A name and a legacy.** After a New York Times investigation in March 2026 alleged that César Chávez sexually assaulted young girls and women, Minnesota repealed Cesar Chavez Day ([MPR News, July 16, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/07/16/st-paul-neighborhood-begins-process-to-rename-csar-chavez-street); [Laws of Minnesota 2026, Chapter 42](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/laws/2026/0/Session+Law/Chapter/42)). Dolores Huerta confirmed the allegations and was described as one of those abused ([West St. Paul Reader, July 6, 2026](https://weststpaulreader.com/2026/07/06/renaming-cesar-chavez-street/)). She died on Oct. 28, 2026, in Bakersfield, California, according to her family; COPAL mourned her publicly ([NBC Los Angeles, Oct. 28, 2026](https://www.nbclosangeles.com/news/local/california-civil-rights-leader-dolores-huerta-dies-at-96/3962301/); [COPAL](https://copalmn.org/)).

On the West Side, the City Council moved to rename Concord Street in late 2003, and the first Cesar Chavez sign went up at the 2004 Cinco de Mayo, by the Reader's account. A five-member committee ruled out naming it for any person, and shortlisted Del Sol, West Side, El Rio Vista, La Raza and El Barrio. Some residents objected that Concord was left off. The vote was postponed, and in October the committee reaffirmed its list. The vote is set for Nov. 3, 2026, for West Side residents and business and property owners, at three West Side locations ([WSCO](https://www.wsco.org/streetrenaming_about); [St. Paul Voice, Aug. 2026](https://www.stpaulpublishing.com/new-west-side-brand-unveiled-vote-soon-to-rename-cesar-chavez-street/)). The outcome is not yet known. Elsewhere, Ramona Arreguin de Rosales of the St. Paul charter school told FOX 9 that the cause is much bigger than one person ([FOX 9, March 31, 2026](https://fox9.com/news/minnesota-recognizes-farmworkers-day-following-cesar-chavez-fallout-march-2026)).

**Status and belonging.** Among Latino Minnesotans, 77,561 are not U.S. citizens, or 21.2 percent, and 36,424 foreign-born people have naturalized ([B05003I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B05003I&geo_ids=04000US27)). The survey does not ask about legal status beyond that. COPAL published on Salvadoran Temporary Protected Status holders in September 2026 ([COPAL](https://copalmn.org/)). Details are on the [Salvadoran page](../salvadoran/profile.md).

**Generations and leadership.** MiLLA aims to train people to run for school boards and city councils ([Ampers, 2025](https://ampers.org/latino-organizations-look-to-the-future-of-leadership/)). CLUES changed chief executives in February 2026, and the state council changed directors after Rosa Tock retired in November 2025 ([CLUES](https://clues.org/); [Council report](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/mandated/261122.pdf)). The West Side unveiled new neighborhood branding, with a sunset for District del Sol and a hummingbird elsewhere, paid for with a $115,000 city grant ([St. Paul Voice, Aug. 2026](https://www.stpaulpublishing.com/new-west-side-brand-unveiled-vote-soon-to-rename-cesar-chavez-street/)).

**Growing into small towns.** Worthington's story has drawn national attention. A city councilor told the Associated Press, "There are many 'us' in Worthington" ([AP via KESQ, Oct. 31, 2024](https://kesq.com/news/ap-national-news/2024/10/31/takeaways-from-aps-report-on-how-immigration-transformed-a-minnesota-farm-town/)). Worthington residents formed a Cross Cultural Advisory Committee in 2020, leading to the city's first community relations position ([Barn Raiser, 2024](https://barnraisingmedia.com/immigrants-find-a-home-rural-minnesota-worthington/)).

## 14. Strengths and contributions

- **Institution building across a century.** Anáhuac in 1922, the Guadalupanas in 1931, CLUES in 1981 and a vendor-owned Mercado Central all grew from people pooling what they had. Founding Mercado families each bought at least ten $100 shares ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-minnesota); [Sahan Journal, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/mercado-central-lake-street-minneapolis-loan-forgiven-immigration/)).
- **Small towns renewed.** MNopedia credits Latino growth with reinvigorating towns and schools in Worthington, Willmar, Pelican Rapids, St. James, Le Center and Madelia. Worthington grew from under 11,000 in 2006 to over 13,300 at the latest census, by Duarte-Alonso's account ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-minnesota); [Barn Raiser, 2024](https://barnraisingmedia.com/immigrants-find-a-home-rural-minnesota-worthington/)).
- **Commercial corridors.** Lake Street and District del Sol stand as visible signs of Latino investment. The Lake Street Council's recovery fund and the West Side's Built By Us campaign show neighbors acting to keep them alive ([Sahan Journal, April 29, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/cinco-de-mayo-st-paul-west-side-latino-business-recovery/); [WSCO](https://www.wsco.org/)).
- **Work the state depends on.** More than 180,000 employed Latino Minnesotans work across management, construction, food production, service and care ([C24010I](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=C24010I&geo_ids=04000US27)).
