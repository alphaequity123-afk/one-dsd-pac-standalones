# Ethiopians in Minnesota

"Ethiopian" here is a wide doorway, not one people. Oromo, Amhara, Tigrayan, Somali, Gurage and other Ethiopian peoples live in Minnesota ([MinnPost, June 23, 2026](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)), as do Anuak people from the Gambella region ([MPR News, May 30, 2008](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2008/05/30/anuak)). It is better to ask a person than to assume. Oromo and Amharic-speaking Minnesotans have their own pages. This page looks at the whole, at what people here have built together, and at the places where they differ.

## 1. People and place

**How many.** Minnesota Compass puts the 2024 total at 53,754, give or take 12,826, and calls that the Census Bureau's best estimate of the community's true size. For almost everything else it uses the pooled 2020 to 2024 American Community Survey, where the base is 45,159, give or take 5,404, because the larger sample is steadier ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). Two rules shape those numbers. The Census Bureau recodes Oromo answers to Ethiopian, leaving no trace of the original answer, so Oromo Minnesotans who answered that way sit inside this count. And people born in Ethiopia who give Somali ancestry are counted as Somali, not Ethiopian ([Compass methodology](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/methodology)).

The Minnesota State Demographic Center builds its own Ethiopian group from the same survey: people who give Ethiopian ancestry or were born in Ethiopia, plus their U.S.-born children. For 2019 to 2023 it counts about 37,400 people, give or take 5,200, with a median age of 25 ([The Economic Status of Minnesotans 2026, Minnesota State Demographic Center, January 2026](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). The two figures use different years and definitions, so the gap between them should not be read as a trend.

Other estimates are all over the map, which is itself worth knowing:

- The Ethio American Foundation, a St. Paul and Minneapolis nonprofit, says Minnesota has more than 37,000 Ethiopian Americans and, on another page, over 22,000 residents born in Ethiopia. Neither page is dated or names a source ([Ethio American Foundation, Community Heroes](https://ethioamericanfoundation.org/community-heroes/); [Ethio American Foundation, News and Stories](https://ethioamericanfoundation.org/news-stories/)).
- The Ethiopian Community in Minnesota told legislators that 45,000 to 50,000 people in and around the Twin Cities identify as Ethiopian or Ethiopian American. The document is undated and gives no method ([HF 3908 project summary, Minnesota House](https://www.house.mn.gov/comm/docs/herYhSCSj0izw9flFc5c4g.pdf)).
- Endalkachew Chala, a scholar and community member, wrote in June 2026 that between 35,000 and 50,000 Minnesotans identify as Ethiopian or Ethiopian American ([MinnPost, June 23, 2026](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)).
- Dara Moskowitz Grumdahl wrote of nearly 40,000 in the metro in 2024 ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, July 14, 2024](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/the-new-ethiopian-scene/)).
- An Associated Press story carried by MPR News in 2019 said about 100,000, with no source given. It is far above every other figure found ([MPR News, April 27, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/04/27/ethiopia-consulate-stpaul)).

**Amhara, Tigrayan, Gurage and other peoples.** No count separates Amhara, Oromo, Tigrayan, Gurage or Anuak Minnesotans by a shared method, and the public data are built in a way that cannot produce one:

- The 2020 Census detailed tables list "Ethiopian" and "Eritrean" groups but no Amhara, Tigrayan or Gurage group ([U.S. Census Bureau, 2020 Detailed DHC-A group list](https://api.census.gov/data/2020/dec/ddhca/variables/POPGROUP.json)).
- The yearly survey's language table puts "Amharic, Somali, or other Afro-Asiatic languages" in one line, so Amharic, Tigrinya and Somali speakers cannot be told apart ([U.S. Census Bureau, table B16001 variables, 2019-2023](https://api.census.gov/data/2023/acs/acs5/groups/B16001.json)).
- School records come closest. The Minnesota Department of Education names Amharic and Oromo among the 12 most common home languages of English learners, but Tigrinya is not in that top 12 and is not reported separately ([Minnesota Department of Education](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED676212.pdf)). A home language also does not show a person's people or origin; the Worthington congregation, for example, used Amharic for services while members spoke many languages (see Languages, below).

What exists instead are community estimates and signs of presence. Sahan Journal in 2021 cited about 40,000 Oromo Minnesotans ([Sahan Journal, Nov. 24, 2021](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/ethiopia-tigray-civil-war/)). Akway Cham, then head of the Minneapolis-based Anywaa Community Association in North America, estimated 2,500 to 3,000 Anuak in Minnesota in 2008, thought to be the largest group outside Africa ([MPR News, May 30, 2008](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2008/05/30/anuak)). For Tigrayan Minnesotans, there is a registered Ethiopian Tigray Community in Minnesota (section 9), and Rep. Ilhan Omar, urging an end to the 2021 communications blackout in Tigray, wrote that the people cut off from their families included "many of my own constituents" ([Rep. Ilhan Omar, Feb. 19, 2021](https://omar.house.gov/media/press-releases/rep-ilhan-omar-statement-situation-ethiopia)). In 2018 a Minneapolis Orthodox church's free tax program asked for Tigrigna and Oromo interpreters, which says the need existed, not how many people ([Debre Selam Medhanealem, Jan. 17, 2018](https://www.debreselam.net/?p=2247)). No count or estimate of Tigrayan, Amhara or Gurage Minnesotans was found, and no Amhara or Gurage community organization in Minnesota turned up.

**Where.** Compass places 89.7 percent in the Twin Cities and 10.3 percent in Greater Minnesota, each give or take 4.0 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). The International Institute of Minnesota's guide, last updated in January 2017, named Seward and Cedar-Riverside in Minneapolis as the neighborhoods with the largest Ethiopian populations ([Finding Common Ground, International Institute of Minnesota](https://iimn.org/publication/finding-common-ground/)). Chala writes that 26th Street and 30th Avenue South in Seward had no Ethiopian institutions 20 years ago and now holds what community members estimate is the state's largest purpose-built Ethiopian Orthodox church ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)).

Life has also moved outward. In 2023-24 schools in Dakota County reported 276 English learners whose first home language is Amharic, more than Ramsey County (247) or Hennepin County (138), with 72 in Washington County and 65 in Anoka County ([Minnesota Department of Education, English Learners in Minnesota 2023-24](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED676212.pdf)). That counts only English learners, not all Amharic speakers, and the report does not explain the pattern. An Orthodox congregation moved from St. Paul to Rosemount in April 2026 after outgrowing its space ([FOX 9, July 17, 2026](https://www.fox9.com/news/community-rallies-around-rosemount-ethiopian-orthodox-church-after-fire)), and Ethiopian restaurants operate in Maple Grove and New Brighton (section 4).

Beyond the metro:

- **Rochester.** Sahan Journal reported about 800 Ethiopian residents ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 3, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/rochester-minnesota-mcknight-foundation-zenebech-aly-immigrants/)). The Post Bulletin said about 500 in 2022 ([Post Bulletin, Sept. 26, 2022](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/local/smoke-leads-the-way-rochester-ethiopians-mark-the-finding-of-the-cross)).
- **Worthington.** An Orthodox congregation there reported 50 to 100 people on a usual Sunday and about 400 at special events in 2020, drawn from southwest Minnesota, northwest Iowa and southeast South Dakota ([The Globe, Oct. 28, 2020](https://www.dglobe.com/lifestyle/ethiopian-orthodox-church-almost-ready-to-build)). By October 2024 the Associated Press described a community of about 500 members worshiping in their own sanctuary, in what used to be the local newspaper's offices ([AP via WSLS, Oct. 31, 2024](https://www.wsls.com/news/national/2024/10/31/in-amharic-karen-and-spanish-worship-is-like-home-for-migrants-in-heartland-town/)). The Globe also reported nearly 300 Anuak refugees in Worthington in 2016, with hundreds more in Austin, the Twin Cities and Sioux Falls ([The Globe via Grand Forks Herald, Feb. 5, 2016](https://www.grandforksherald.com/newsmd/anuak-community-in-worthington-pleads-for-peace-in-ethiopian-homeland)).
- **Austin.** Oballa Oballa, who sits on the city council, has been a public figure here (section 9). A current count of Ethiopian-born or Anuak residents in Austin could not be confirmed.

**How people came.** The first Oromo settlers in Minnesota, Tasissa Moti and Teferi Fufa, arrived as students in 1972. More came after the 1974 coup, refugees from Somalia, Djibouti and Sudan arrived from 1980 and 1981, and more than 200 Oromo lived in the Twin Cities by the mid-1980s ([MNopedia, last modified Aug. 12, 2026](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)). The International Institute's 2017 guide says many professionals and students already in the United States gained asylum in the early 1980s and later arrivals came as refugees, asylees, relatives and through the Diversity Visa Lottery, with growing movement from other states for jobs and family ([International Institute of Minnesota](https://iimn.org/publication/finding-common-ground/)). The Ethiopian Community in Minnesota says Ethiopians have lived here since the 1970s and that it registered with the state in 1995 ([GiveMN](https://www.givemn.org/organization/Ethiopian-Community-In-Minnesota)).

Anuak Minnesotans came by a different road. A 2004 report said more than 2,000 Anuak refugees had come to Minnesota since the early 1990s ([Post Bulletin, May 5, 2004](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/ethiopia-carries-out-genocide-on-tribe)). Human rights groups say more than 400 Anuak were killed in attacks that began in Gambella town on Dec. 13, 2003, and that memory has shaped public life here ([MPR News, May 30, 2008](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2008/05/30/anuak)). Nationally, one 2018 report for the International Organization for Migration describes four waves: elites before 1974, people fleeing the Red Terror from 1974 to 1982, family reunification to 1991, and people fleeing ethnic violence and repression after that ([IOM mapping report, background, not Minnesota-specific](https://ethiopia.iom.int/sites/g/files/tmzbdl996/files/documents/Final%2520Mapping%2520of%2520Ethiopian.pdf)).

Arrivals continue. The Minnesota Department of Health counted 272 primary arrivals from Ethiopia with humanitarian immigration statuses in 2024, out of 4,602 statewide, the fourth-largest group ([Minnesota Refugee Health Report 2024](https://www.web.health.state.mn.us/communities/rih/stats/24rhrpt.pdf)). Compass reports that 22 percent of Ethiopian-born residents have been in the United States five years or less and 26 percent more than 20 years ([Minnesota Compass, 10 things to know about immigration](https://www.mncompass.org/data-insights/featured-trend/10-things-know-about-immigration-minnesota)).

**Generations.** This is a young community. Compass counts 43.8 percent age 17 or younger, 12.5 percent under 5, and only 3.5 percent 65 or older. About 59.8 percent were born abroad, give or take 3.9 points, so roughly four in ten were not ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)).

**Languages.** Among people 5 and older, 76.2 percent speak a language other than English at home and 30.2 percent speak English less than "very well" ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). Chala names Amharic, Oromo and Tigrigna among the languages heard at the 2026 soccer tournament ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)). Amharic and Oromo are each among the 12 most common home languages for Minnesota English learners ([Minnesota Department of Education](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED676212.pdf)). In Worthington in 2020, members spoke many languages but nearly all spoke Amharic, which the church used for services ([The Globe, 2020](https://www.dglobe.com/lifestyle/ethiopian-orthodox-church-almost-ready-to-build)). Anuak is its own language; a Gambella official who addressed Anuak Minnesotans in 2008 answered in Amharic and repeated himself in Anuak ([MPR News via AP, May 31, 2008](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2008/05/31/ethopia)).

## 2. All walks of life

All figures here come from Compass for 2020 to 2024, in 2024 dollars, unless noted ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). They cover the whole umbrella, including Oromo people recoded as Ethiopian. Margins are wide.

- **Comfortable and well-off households.** Median household income is $63,667, give or take $6,426. About 23.2 percent of households take in $100,000 or more, give or take 6.0 points. No wealth or net worth data for this community turned up. Visible signs of success include Bolé's restaurants in St. Paul and Maple Grove, owned by Solomon Haile and Rekik Abaineh ([Bolé](https://boleethiopiancuisine.com/)), and Rahel Nardos, born and raised in Addis Ababa, a University of Minnesota professor and Director of Global Women's Health ([University of Minnesota Medical School](https://med.umn.edu/bio/rahel-nardos)).
- **Professionals and owners.** Among adults 25 and older, 24.1 percent hold a bachelor's degree or more, including 8.1 percent with a graduate or professional degree. Abe and Aster Dalu, a toxicologist and a registered nurse, built A&A Reliable Home Health Care in St. Paul to about 70 employees as of 2021 ([American Immigration Council, Sept. 15, 2021](https://www.americanimmigrationcouncil.org/story/ethiopian-immigrant-couple-start-home-care-business-in-minnesota/)). Amy Tsegaye and Dereje Wudmatas own Erta Ale in Lowertown ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, 2024](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/the-new-ethiopian-scene/)). Tseganesh Selameab grew up in Ethiopia, has lived in Minnesota since middle school, earned her medical degree at the University of Minnesota, and practices at Hennepin County Medical Center ([University of Minnesota](https://med.umn.edu/dm_profile/2494)).
- **Middle-income and working families.** Another 40.5 percent of households earn between $50,000 and $99,999. About 35.6 percent of households own their homes, give or take 6.7 points. Employment among adults 18 to 64 is 78.9 percent: 83.0 percent for men and 74.4 percent for women. Median rent is $1,286. The largest single household type, 33.1 percent, is married couples with children.
- **Families with less.** About 18.2 percent of households take in under $35,000 and another 18.0 percent between $35,000 and $49,999. A further 34.5 percent of people live between the poverty line and twice that line, above it but not by much.
- **People in poverty.** About 10.9 percent of people live below the poverty line, give or take 3.5 points, including 10.4 percent of children (give or take 5.3). Among people 65 and older the figure is 34.2 percent, but the margin of 15.9 points means the true number could be far lower or higher. Renters are 64.4 percent of households, and 41.9 percent of them spend too much of their income on housing. About 20.5 percent of adults 25 and older did not finish high school, and 7.3 percent of people have no health insurance. Zenebech Aly's crisis response program in Rochester exists for families facing sudden costs and loss ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 3, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/rochester-minnesota-mcknight-foundation-zenebech-aly-immigrants/)).

The State Demographic Center's January 2026 chartbook, using 2019 to 2023 and its own definition, gives a similar picture from another angle. Median household income was $58,000, give or take $6,400. Full-time, year-round workers ages 16 to 64 had median earnings of $45,000, give or take $4,400, against $61,900 for all Minnesotans; the report names Ethiopian workers among the groups that "earn the least," with Somali, Ojibwe and "Other American Indian" workers. It suggests education, occupation mix and a young age structure may help explain earnings gaps, and adds that it cannot examine pay discrimination with these data ([Minnesota State Demographic Center, January 2026](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)).

These figures describe the umbrella and cannot show whether incomes or schooling differ among Amhara, Oromo, Tigrayan and Anuak Minnesotans.

## 3. Work and the economy

**What the numbers show, and what they do not.** No published table gives occupations or industries for Ethiopian Minnesotans. Compass leaves them off its Ethiopian profile, and the State Demographic Center's chartbook reports work but not occupation. What that chartbook does show, for 2019 to 2023 ([Minnesota State Demographic Center, January 2026](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)):

- About 78 percent of Ethiopian Minnesotans ages 16 to 64 were in the labor force, close to the statewide 82 percent: about 17,000 of 21,800 people, each with margins of 1,100 to 2,900.
- Of those in the labor force, about 16,300 were working and about 600 were unemployed (give or take 400).
- About 18 percent of adults 25 to 64 had no high school diploma or GED, against 6 percent statewide. The report notes that adults without a diploma have "narrow employment prospects."
- Median earnings for full-time, year-round workers were $45,000 (section 2).

The nearest thing to an industry picture is older and broader. A 2017 Twin Cities Business and MinnPost analysis of 2014 Census figures found 43 percent of all Africa-born Minnesotans working in educational services, health care and social assistance, a third in service jobs and 28 percent in management, business, science and arts occupations. It warned that single countries cannot be measured accurately this way. Economist Bruce Corrie told the writers that some immigrants, including Oromo people from Ethiopia, arrive with medical training that does not always transfer ([Twin Cities Business, June 6, 2017](https://tcbmag.com/?p=14831)). These figures cover all Africa-born residents and should not be read as Ethiopian figures.

What follows comes from individual accounts and should not be read as a portrait of the whole.

**Food and hospitality** is the most visible trade (section 4). **Home care and nursing** recur. A&A's owners took a 10-week course at the Neighborhood Development Center and got technology and bookkeeping help from African Economic Development Solutions ([American Immigration Council](https://www.americanimmigrationcouncil.org/story/ethiopian-immigrant-couple-start-home-care-business-in-minnesota/)). The International Institute of Minnesota tells the story of a nurse from Ethiopia who came through its Medical Career Advancement program and now works in mental health and addiction services at M Health Fairview ([International Institute of Minnesota](https://iimn.org/news/kasahuns-story-a-voice-for-the-patient/)).

**Plants and rural work.** A congregation organizer told The Globe in 2014 that her path ran from John Morrell in Sioux Falls to Campbell's Soup and Swift (now JBS) in Worthington, then dishwashing at Perkins and a travel center, and later running the Queen of Sheba restaurant ([The Globe, March 24, 2014](https://www.dglobe.com/news/christians-helping-christians-first-lutheran-church-lends-hand-to-worthington-based-ethiopian-orthodox-members)). That is one person's story, but it shows how plant jobs and small businesses can sit in the same life. How many Ethiopian or Anuak Minnesotans work in meatpacking today could not be established.

**Getting to work.** Of about 18,700 workers, 77.7 percent travel by car and 10.7 percent by public transit ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)).

**Economic support.** African Economic Development Solutions, in St. Paul, has made business loans since 2008 and offers business development, financial coaching and homeownership education for African immigrants and others ([AEDS](https://www.aeds-mn.org/)). At the groundbreaking for its $4.5 million Little Africa Plaza, economist Bruce Corrie of Concordia University cited about $2.8 billion in buying power among African immigrants in Minnesota, based on a 2015 survey he later updated. That figure covers all African immigrants ([KSTP, undated, opening then expected April 2024](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/cultural-economic-hub-little-africa-plaza-breaks-ground-in-st-paul/)).

**Unions.** Nothing turned up on union membership or labor organizing specific to Ethiopian Minnesotans.

**A big week for the local economy.** Organizers expected a boost to hotels and nearby businesses from the 2026 soccer tournament, and vendor tent rentals and sponsorships helped pay for it ([Power 104.7, June 26](https://www.power1047.fm/post/national-ethiopian-soccer-tournament-comes-to-minnesota); [MPR News, June 30, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/06/30/ethiopian-soccer-tournament-draws-thousands-despite-immigration-fears)).

## 4. Businesses and commercial life

The full list is in `businesses.json`. These are the places a reader is most likely to meet.

**Restaurants.** The Red Sea on Cedar Avenue has served Ethiopian and Eritrean food since 1990, with a bar, dance floor and weekend live music, and claims to be the city's first Ethiopian restaurant ([Mpls With Love, updated October 2026](https://mplswithlove.com/guides/best-ethiopian-east-african-food); [Discover the Cities, June 2021](https://discoverthecities.com/best-ethiopian-food-restaurants-minneapolis-st-paul/)). Longfellow's stretch of Minnehaha Avenue holds Selam, Meseret and Katar River ([Mpls With Love](https://mplswithlove.com/guides/best-ethiopian-east-african-food)). Selam began as a coffee shop in 2007 and became a full restaurant in 2021; Belai Mergia and Rahel Tassew run it with their three daughters ([Minnesota Spokesman-Recorder, Nov. 29, 2024](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2024/11/29/selam-restaurant-ethiopian-gem/)).

Bolé is named for the Addis Ababa neighborhood where its owners grew up. Its original Midway site was destroyed in the 2020 unrest, and crowdfunding helped it reopen near Como Park, where Racket visited for brunch in 2025 ([Discover the Cities](https://discoverthecities.com/best-ethiopian-food-restaurants-minneapolis-st-paul/); [Racket, June 24, 2025](https://racketmn.com/brunch-buds-bole-ethiopian-cuisine-bowles-us-over)). Chef Tigist "TG" Feyisa saw a market among fasting Orthodox Christians and Minnesota vegans when she opened Lulu Ethiovegan, named for her daughter ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, 2024](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/the-new-ethiopian-scene/)). Erta Ale in Lowertown also sells hard-to-find ingredients and stays open late on weekends ([Visit Saint Paul](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/directory/erta-ale-ethiopian-restaurant/)).

**Newer places and closings.** Gebeta Ethiopian Restaurant opened in May 2026 at 1049 Dodd Road in West St. Paul. Owner Zene Jebessa, who also owns Abyssinia Mart in Apple Valley, said, "We are short of Ethiopian restaurants here in the Twin Cities." It is the third Ethiopian restaurant to try that spot, after Betty's Kitchen in 2023 and Yadot Kitffo in 2024 ([West St. Paul Reader, May 15, 2026](https://weststpaulreader.com/2026/05/15/gebeta-ethiopian-restaurant-now-open/)). Fasika, on Snelling Avenue in Hamline-Midway, closed in June 2023 after 22 years ([KSTP](https://kstp.com/?p=2780513); [Cadry's Kitchen](https://cadryskitchen.com/ethiopian-vegan-donuts-twin-cities/)). The list in `businesses.json` marks each place's status separately.

**Groceries and bakeries.** Shabelle on East Franklin sells spices, teff flour, coffee beans and injera beside its counter ([Mpls With Love](https://mplswithlove.com/guides/best-ethiopian-east-african-food)). Katar River is both restaurant and bakery. Shega Foods sells takeout and spices ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, updated Feb. 25, 2025](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/black-owned-businesses-in-the-twin-cities/)). East African Bakery in St. Paul supplies injera to other stores and restaurants, and Mamas Market & Deli, run by nine Ethiopian mothers, is listed inside Little Africa Plaza; a current date could not be confirmed for either ([Visit Saint Paul, guide to East African food](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/east-african-food-in-saint-paul/); [Visit Saint Paul, food halls and markets](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/food-halls-and-markets/)).

**Coffee.** Limu Coffee in New Brighton is Ethiopian-owned ([Spokesman-Recorder, June 11, 2020](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2020/06/11/local-black-owned-coffee-house-alternatives-to-starbucks/)) and had a customer review as recently as January 2026 ([Wanderlog](https://www.wanderlog.com/place/details/6159408/limu-coffee)).

**Districts and support.** Little Africa, in St. Paul, is a branding and marketing effort for African immigrant business along transit corridors, centered near Snelling and LaFond avenues ([Visit Saint Paul, AEDS listing](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/directory/aeds-african-economic-development-solutions/); [Visit Saint Paul, food halls](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/food-halls-and-markets/)). Visit Saint Paul says the district was established in 2013 ([Visit Saint Paul, Little Africa](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/things-to-do/neighborhoods/little-africa/)). Little Africa Plaza, a 1926 car showroom at 678 Snelling Ave. N., now holds an ethnic grocery, independent retail shops, an art sales and community room, and AEDS offices ([AEDS](https://www.aeds-mn.org/little-africa-plaza)). Visit Saint Paul adds an African Museum, Arts, and Cultural Center inside, with rotating exhibits and artist showcases ([Visit Saint Paul, Little Africa](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/things-to-do/neighborhoods/little-africa/)). The Economic Development Association of Minnesota named it a 2026 Project of the Year and toured it on Aug. 13, 2026 ([EDAM](https://www.edam.org/EDAM/Events/2026/Emerging-Professionals/On-the-Road/Home)). It serves African immigrants broadly, not only Ethiopians. A Visit Saint Paul guide to the district, undated and still listing Fasika, also names Addis Market for Ethiopian spices and foods and Snelling Cafe, open since 2003 with Eritrean and Ethiopian dishes; neither could be confirmed open on a current page ([Visit Saint Paul, Saint Paul's Vibrant Little Africa](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/saint-pauls-little-taste-of-africa/)). No Ethiopian chamber of commerce in Minnesota turned up.

**Professional firms.** No Ethiopian-owned law, accounting, tax, insurance, travel, salon or clinic business in Minnesota could be confirmed by name on a current, readable page. Directory listings for braiding salons and home care agencies do not say who owns them, and they were not counted. Two community services fill part of this space. Debre Selam Medhanealem church ran a free income tax preparation program in partnership with Amare Berhie, staffed by volunteers, at least as of 2018 ([Debre Selam Medhanealem, Jan. 17, 2018](https://www.debreselam.net/?p=2247)). The Ethio American Foundation lists business consulting, job training, financial literacy and referrals to legal resources among its free services for Ethiopian Americans in Hennepin and Ramsey counties ([211 United Way listing, last assured Sept. 16, 2025](https://search.211unitedway.org/search/a933a5c6-0db7-5bc4-b8c0-81514f816d87)). A missing firm on this page means it was not found, not that it does not exist.

## 5. Religion and spiritual life

**Differences matter here.** Chala describes Orthodox, Catholic, Evangelical and Muslim communities in the Twin Cities ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)). Stratis Health's guide also lists Jewish believers and those who practice indigenous African spirituality ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/ethiopians/)). No count of Minnesotans by faith turned up. In Rochester, community leaders in 2022 estimated about 80 percent of the local Ethiopian community was Orthodox, which describes one city ([Post Bulletin, 2022](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/local/smoke-leads-the-way-rochester-ethiopians-mark-the-finding-of-the-cross)). Nothing turned up on Ethiopian Minnesotans who hold no faith.

**Orthodox Tewahedo.** Chala estimates about eight active Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox congregations in the metro ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)). Those that could be confirmed:

- **Debre Selam Medhanealem**, Seward, 2601 30th Ave. S. About 300 people came to an open house on April 18, 2026. The building cost nearly $11 million with the land; construction began in November 2024. The congregation had worshiped at 4401 Minnehaha Ave. since 1999. Sunday services run 7 to 11 a.m. in Ge'ez and Amharic with some English. Board Chair Debalke Gebeyehu called it "a living bridge" for immigrants ([Longfellow Nokomis Messenger, April 27, 2026](https://www.longfellownokomismessenger.com/stories/debre-selam-medhanealem-ethiopian-orthodox-tewahedo-churchfinds-new-home-in-seward,161305)). Chala says members raised money across two decades, $50 at a time, at coffee ceremonies ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)). The church's own website gives the address as 2629 30th Ave. S., not 2601, and lists the groups in parish life: an *eder*, *tsiwa* associations named for the Savior, St. Mary, St. Michael, St. Gabriel and Abune Petros, and a Sunday school. Its 2025-26 calendar, published in Amharic with English dates, sets out feasts and fasts, children's programs and volunteer shifts at U.S. Bank Stadium that raised money for the new building ([Debre Selam Medhanealem, home page](https://www.debreselam.net/); [Debre Selam Medhanealem, feasts and fasts calendar, Oct. 29, 2025](https://www.debreselam.net/?p=17205)).
- **Re'ese Adbarat Tserha Aryam Kidist Selassie Cathedral**, 2601 Minnehaha Ave. The cathedral's own history says more than 500 parishioners chose on April 13, 2014 to join the structure of the Holy Synod in Ethiopia and held their first full liturgy outside "the old church building" that Easter ([Selassie Cathedral](https://minnesotaselassie.org/index.php/clergy/brief-history)). Its site lists Saturday language education, Friday faith education, faith community nursing, and a 2026 scholarship ([Selassie Cathedral](https://minnesotaselassie.org/)).
- **Debre Bisrat St. Gabriel and St. Arsema**, about 200 members. It moved from St. Paul to 3285 144th St. W., Rosemount, on April 19, 2026. A fire on July 13, 2026 did heavy damage to the building; police believed it was empty, no injuries were reported, and the cause had not been determined in these reports ([KSTP, July 13, 2026](https://kstp.com/?p=3974644); [FOX 9, July 13, 2026](https://www.fox9.com/news/authorities-respond-structure-fire-rosemount-church); [FOX 9, July 17, 2026](https://www.fox9.com/news/community-rallies-around-rosemount-ethiopian-orthodox-church-after-fire)). Deacon Bisrat Gebre said, "It's like a really bad dream." The congregation's fundraiser showed $91,227 toward a $1 million goal when checked ([GoFundMe](https://www.gofundme.com/f/support-debre-bisrat-minnesota-to-rebuild)).
- **Menbere Tsehay Sent Mary**, Worthington. Members once drove to Sioux Falls or Sioux City, then rented at First Lutheran from 2013. In 2020 they were raising money to build on Minnesota 60 ([The Globe, 2020](https://www.dglobe.com/lifestyle/ethiopian-orthodox-church-almost-ready-to-build)). In October 2024 the Associated Press found Worthington's Ethiopian Orthodox community of about 500 members holding six-hour services, beginning before dawn, in their own sanctuary in the former offices of the local newspaper, followed by prayers over lunch in the basement. Member Abebe Abetew recalled the Lutherans who had hosted them: "They're God people." He also said, "Church is basic for us, like eating the food" ([AP via WSLS, Oct. 31, 2024](https://www.wsls.com/news/national/2024/10/31/in-amharic-karen-and-spanish-worship-is-like-home-for-migrants-in-heartland-town/)). The AP story does not name the congregation, and whether the Minnesota 60 building plan went ahead was not reported.
- **Rochester.** Orthodox Christians there marked Meskel at Cascade Lake in 2021 and 2022 (section 6).
- **St. Mary Tigray Orthodox Tewahdo Church**, St. Paul, is the name on a YouTube channel ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/@st.maryeotcminnesota8903/about)). Nothing more about the congregation could be confirmed.

**Fasting** shapes the year. Grumdahl reports that observant Tewahedo Christians over age 7 fast about 180 days a year and clergy about 250, avoiding meat and dairy ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, 2024](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/the-new-ethiopian-scene/)). Stratis Health notes Wednesdays, Fridays and 55 days before Easter ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/ethiopians/)).

**Protestant.** The Evangelical Mekane Yesus Lutheran Church Fellowship in Minnesota, 1566 W. Thomas Ave., St. Paul, reported an average of 64 people a week for 2025 ([Lutheran Church-Missouri Synod locator](https://locator.lcms.org/church/C/1001496)). In Worthington in 2016, Anuak residents gathered at the Christian Reformed Church and Westminster Presbyterian ([The Globe via Grand Forks Herald, 2016](https://www.grandforksherald.com/newsmd/anuak-community-in-worthington-pleads-for-peace-in-ethiopian-homeland)). Samuel Deressa of Concordia University, St. Paul, presented in 2023 on how Ethiopian immigrant congregations in Minneapolis and St. Paul struggle to bring second and third generations into community life ([Concordia Seminary Multiethnic Symposium, 2023](https://scholar.csl.edu/mucu/2023/schedule/7/)).

**Muslim life.** MinnPost names Ethiopian Muslim communities without naming mosques. The Oromo page is the place for Oromo mosques. No mosque in Minnesota that describes itself as Ethiopian, Amharic-speaking, Harari or Silte could be found on any readable page. Listings for St. Paul mosques such as Al-Ihsan Islamic Center on West Minnehaha Avenue and Masjid Al-Wahyain on University Avenue describe serving Muslims generally and name no Ethiopian tie, so neither is called Ethiopian here ([Zabihah, Al-Ihsan](https://www.zabihah.com/mosques/136d5e85-776a-11ef-95ae-6045bdeb9f57/al-ihsan-islamic-center-st-paul-mn); [Zabihah, Al-Wahyain](https://www.zabihah.com/mosques/137fbbcc-776a-11ef-95ae-6045bdeb9f57/masjid-al-wahyain-saint-paul-mn)). Where Ethiopian Muslims who are not Oromo worship, and how they mark Ramadan and Eid, could not be established.

## 6. Celebrations and observances

- **Enkutatash, the Ethiopian New Year,** falls on Sept. 11, or Sept. 12 in some years ([Ethiopia Visitors Hub, for 2026](https://ethiopiavisitorshub.com/ethiopian-september-holidays-guide/)). The Ethiopian Community in Minnesota invited people to a free celebration at Highland Park Pavilion, St. Paul, on Sept. 13, 2026, with food, music and dance and an invitation to wear traditional dress ([Mefthe listing](https://mefthe.com/events/celebrate-ethiopian-new-year-in-minnesota/)). Its website also shows a 2011 New Year celebration at Matthews Park ([Ethiopian Community in Minnesota](https://ethiopiancommunitymn.org/)).
- **Meskel and Demera.** In 2022, more than 100 people in Rochester held a service, walked to Cascade Lake Beach and lit a bonfire topped by a cross, on the Sunday nearest the Sept. 27 feast so more could come. Eyob Adehane told the Post Bulletin that a cross the year before had been misread as a possible cross burning and was dismantled ([Post Bulletin, Sept. 26, 2022](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/local/smoke-leads-the-way-rochester-ethiopians-mark-the-finding-of-the-cross)). That small story says much about being visible in a Minnesota town.
- **Timket (Timkat),** Epiphany, Jan. 19. Chala writes that on Timket in 2026 many families watched immigration agents' movements on WhatsApp instead of walking to church, and a parishioner told him, "God understands. But it still hurts" ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)). Selassie Cathedral once announced a two-day Timket "in unity for the first time in Minnesota" on Jan. 17 and 18; the page gives no year ([Selassie Cathedral](http://minnesotaselassie.org/index.php/announcements/60-timket)).
- **The church year at one Minneapolis parish.** Debre Selam Medhanealem published its 2025-26 calendar with these dates: Genna (Christmas) on Jan. 7, 2026, after the Advent fast from Nov. 24; Timket on Jan. 19, 2026; the great Lenten fast (Abiy Tsom) from Feb. 16 to Easter (Fasika) on April 12, 2026, with Hosanna on April 5 and Good Friday on April 10; the apostles' fast from June 1 to July 12; the Filseta fast for the Virgin Mary from Aug. 7 to 22, 2026; and Debre Tabor (the Transfiguration) on Aug. 19. It also scheduled a children's Christmas program on Jan. 3 and a children's *begena* (harp) night on March 21 ([Debre Selam Medhanealem, Oct. 29, 2025](https://www.debreselam.net/?p=17205)). These are one parish's published dates. A short video is titled "This Minnesota Church Celebrates Christmas in January," though the page did not show which church ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/o2AkWDp4Ah4)).
- **Debre Selam Health Day.** The same church held its 11th annual Health Day, a 5K run and walk, on June 7, 2025, at Como Regional Park in St. Paul, with age-group medals ([Debre Selam Medhanealem](https://www.debreselam.net/?page_id=1363)). A 2026 date was not found.
- **Ethiopian Sports Federation in North America tournament.** The 43rd annual gathering ran June 27 to July 4, 2026, mostly at TCO Stadium in Eagan with some games at Saint Thomas Academy ([ESFNA](https://esfna.org/tournament/)). Board member Elias Meles described 32 teams, about 30 vendors, nonprofit and church booths, a concert at the Omni Hotel, and more than 20,000 expected visitors ([MPR News, June 25, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2026/06/25/ethiopian-soccer-tournament-twin-cities)). Gov. Tim Walz declared July 3 Ethiopian Day ([MPR News, June 30, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/06/30/ethiopian-soccer-tournament-draws-thousands-despite-immigration-fears)).
- **Ashenda,** which a 2026 report calls a holiday marking the end of the 15-day Filseta fast, matters to many Tigrayan people ([Africanews, Aug. 23, 2026](https://www.africanews.com/amp/2026/08/23/ethiopia-tigrayan-women-celebrate-ashenda-amid-fears-of-new-war/)). In 2026 Debre Selam listed the Filseta fast as Aug. 7 to 22 ([Debre Selam Medhanealem](https://www.debreselam.net/?p=17205)). No Minnesota Ashenda celebration could be confirmed.
- **Adwa Victory Day,** March 2. No Minnesota celebration turned up.
- **Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha and Mawlid.** Observed by Ethiopian Muslim Minnesotans in ways that could not be documented separately.
- **Irreecha,** the Oromo thanksgiving, belongs on the Oromo page.

## 7. Family, home and daily life

**Households.** Compass counts about 12,436 households averaging 3.30 people. Married couples with children under 18 are 33.1 percent, people living alone 26.1 percent, and single women with children under 18 are 10.4 percent, give or take 4.4 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). More than a quarter living alone is worth noticing beside the picture of large families. Compass does not say who they are.

**Food and coffee.** At Selam, guests can join a coffee ceremony in which beans are roasted, hand-ground and brewed in a clay jebena ([Spokesman-Recorder, 2024](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2024/11/29/selam-restaurant-ethiopian-gem/)). Coffee ceremonies also helped raise money for a church (section 5).

**Childhood and passing things on.** A congregation organizer in Worthington said in 2014 that boys are baptized at 40 days and girls at 80 ([The Globe, 2014](https://www.dglobe.com/news/christians-helping-christians-first-lutheran-church-lends-hand-to-worthington-based-ethiopian-orthodox-members)). Asbe Hailu, a federation board member, said of the soccer tournament, "We instill the love of their heritage" ([Power 104.7](https://www.power1047.fm/post/national-ethiopian-soccer-tournament-comes-to-minnesota)). Tensae Fayise, raised in Worthington, called her mother "the bridge for me to go home" ([YourClassical, Feb. 18, 2020](https://www.yourclassical.org/story/2020/02/18/fayise-cedar-commissions)). Stratis Health also notes that adoptions of Ethiopian children by white American families left some adoptees feeling a loss of homeland and identity ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/ethiopians/)).

**Aging.** Just 3.5 percent are 65 or older, so aging in a new country may be new ground for many families, though no source here measures that. The Ethiopian Community in Minnesota's center proposal includes a seniors' room and speaks of keeping elders from isolation ([HF 3908 project summary](https://www.house.mn.gov/comm/docs/herYhSCSj0izw9flFc5c4g.pdf)). Stratis Health says religious beliefs shape end-of-life preferences for many ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/ethiopians/)).

**Marriage and dress.** Little specific to Minnesota turned up. Yousuf's essay (section 9) touches on how some relatives viewed marriage to African Americans. Traditional dress appears in event invitations.

## 8. Arts, media, sports and culture

**Music.** Drummer and composer Abinnet Berhanu, born to Ethiopian parents and raised in Virginia, moved to Minneapolis in 2020. His band Ahndenet, Amharic for "unity," with vocalist Genet Abate, arranges traditional Ethiopian songs with jazz, funk, blues and soul, and played a free show at the Cedar on July 23, 2025 ([Sahan Journal, July 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/things-to-do-minneapolis-salsa-fest-ethiopian-jazz-film-block-party/)). Composer Tensae Fayise, born in Sioux Falls and raised in Worthington, plays the krar and wrote "Ye Terrarou Tenfash" for the Cedar Commissions in 2020 ([YourClassical](https://www.yourclassical.org/story/2020/02/18/fayise-cedar-commissions)). The Current named Yaba Gobana as a Minneapolis artist of Ethiopian origin in 2021 ([The Current, Sept. 28, 2021](https://www.thecurrent.org/feature/2021/09/28/the-come-up-a-summer-recap-and-early-fall-jams-from-11-minnesota-artists)). The Cedar Cultural Center and The Green Room have also hosted touring Ethiopian acts ([Sahan Journal, April 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/weekend-arts-ethiopian-music-jungle-theater-opera-cradle-will-rock/); [Jazz88, Sept. 21, 2023](https://www.jazz88.fm/2023/09/21/yohannes-tona-and-dibekulu-bring-ethio-jazz-to-the-twin-cities/)).

**Media.** ZeHabesha had a new post on Oct. 30, 2026; its own page says it was founded in 2005 ([ZeHabesha](https://zehabesha.com/)). Sahan Journal said in 2020 that Henok A. Degfu founded it in 2008 and that it was based in Burnsville, and reported that Ethiopian Airlines was suing it for $25 million over three interviews. The article's last update was July 15, 2020, with no ruling, and no outcome could be found ([Sahan Journal, July 2, 2020](https://sahanjournal.com/news/ethiopian-airlines-africas-largest-carrier-sues-minnesota-journalist-and-his-independent-newspaper/)). Stratis Health lists KFAI's Voices of Ethiopia; its current schedule could not be confirmed ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/ethiopians/)).

**Sports.** Minnesota's own team played in the 2026 federation tournament, in Division II Group C, beating Austin 7-0 and drawing San Diego and Phoenix ([ESFNA](https://esfna.org/tournament/)). Minnesota had been chosen to host in 2020 before COVID canceled it ([MPR News, June 25, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2026/06/25/ethiopian-soccer-tournament-twin-cities)). No Minnesota source turned up on Ethiopian-born runners or other athletes based here.

**Cultural centers.** No Ethiopian community center building could be confirmed (section 9). A planned African history and cultural museum was part of the Little Africa Plaza plan; AEDS's founder said nothing like it existed in Minnesota ([KSTP](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/cultural-economic-hub-little-africa-plaza-breaks-ground-in-st-paul/)). Visit Saint Paul now lists the African Museum, Arts, and Cultural Center inside the plaza ([Visit Saint Paul, Little Africa](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/things-to-do/neighborhoods/little-africa/)). It serves African communities broadly.

## 9. Civic and political life

**Elected office.** Oballa Oballa holds Austin's Ward 3 council seat. KAAL reports the council chose him 5 to 1 after Joyce Poshusta resigned, that he earlier served two and a half years in Ward 1, and that he announced he would seek reelection, naming housing, childcare, public safety and youth among his priorities. KAAL calls him Austin's first African American councilmember ([KAAL, appointment](https://www.kaaltv.com/?p=875617); [KAAL, reelection, about May 2026](https://www.kaaltv.com/?p=1107841)). No Ethiopian-born member of the Minnesota Legislature turned up, though this could only be checked in part.

In the statements found, Minnesota's members of Congress spoke about more than one of Ethiopia's peoples at once. In February 2021, Rep. Ilhan Omar called the Tigray conflict "a catastrophic humanitarian and human rights situation," asked that the communications blackout be lifted so people in Tigray could speak to their families, "including many of my own constituents," and in the same statement said she was alarmed by reports of targeted killings of Amhara people and by the treatment of Oromo political prisoners ([Rep. Ilhan Omar, Feb. 19, 2021](https://omar.house.gov/media/press-releases/rep-ilhan-omar-statement-situation-ethiopia)). In March 2024, KTTC reported that Sens. Tina Smith and Amy Klobuchar wrote to the Biden administration urging continued humanitarian aid to Ethiopia, citing violence in Tigray, Amhara and Oromia ([KTTC via ZeHabesha, March 15, 2024](https://zehabesha.com/minnesota-senators-urge-biden-administration-to-aid-ethiopia/)). Omar's October 2026 statement is in section 13.

**Organizations.**

- **Ethiopian Community in Minnesota,** 616 Rice St., St. Paul, with office hours Monday and Friday. It offers notary service, help with documents, referrals, references for first-time renters and cultural activities, and describes itself as open to Ethiopians of any religion, language or ethnicity ([ECM](https://ethiopiancommunitymn.org/contact/); [ECM home](https://ethiopiancommunitymn.org/); [GuideStar](https://www.guidestar.org/profile/41-1885932)). On GiveMN it reports about 400 registered members, though that page's latest dated event is from 2012 ([GiveMN](https://www.givemn.org/organization/Ethiopian-Community-In-Minnesota)). It told legislators it worked from an eight-by-ten-foot office and wanted a center on University Avenue ([HF 3908 project summary](https://www.house.mn.gov/comm/docs/herYhSCSj0izw9flFc5c4g.pdf)).
- **Ethiopian Tigray Community in Minnesota,** 1821 University Ave. W., St. Paul, a charity since 2012 with filings through 2024. It reports annual community events, recognition of high school graduates and college prep workshops ([GuideStar](https://www.guidestar.org/profile/47-0855873); [Instrumentl, 2024 filing](https://www.instrumentl.com/990-report/ethiopian-tigray-community-in-minnesota)).
- **Ethio American Foundation,** a 501(c)(3) nonprofit serving Ethiopian Americans in St. Paul, Minneapolis and beyond. It describes scholarships and mentoring for first-generation students, job training and business consulting, cultural programs, immigration information with legal referrals, and referrals for health and mental health care, with no fees ([Ethio American Foundation](https://ethioamericanfoundation.org/); [211 United Way listing, last assured Sept. 16, 2025](https://search.211unitedway.org/search/a933a5c6-0db7-5bc4-b8c0-81514f816d87)). It names CHS Inc. as a donor through a Backyard Giving grant for youth programs ([Ethio American Foundation, Community Heroes](https://ethioamericanfoundation.org/community-heroes/)).
- **Ethiopian Community Rochester Organization,** founded in 2020 by ten Ethiopians and serving any immigrant in similar need. Co-founder Zenebech Aly won a 2024 McKnight Foundation Heart of Community Honor ([Sahan Journal, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/rochester-minnesota-mcknight-foundation-zenebech-aly-immigrants/)).
- **Anuak groups.** Akuthi Okoth, who moved to Minnesota in 2000, co-founded the Anuak Women Community group in March 2003 ([Cultural Survival, May 7, 2010](https://www.culturalsurvival.org/node/3721)). Whether any Anuak organization is active today could not be confirmed.
- **The Consulate General of Ethiopia,** on University Avenue in St. Paul, opened in 2019. Consul Ewnetu Bilata Debela said he would serve people "without any prejudice to their ethnic background" and acknowledged favoritism had been a weakness of past consulates. Teshite Wako, an Oromo community leader, saw the opening as a sign of change ([MPR News via AP, April 27, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/04/27/ethiopia-consulate-stpaul)). MNopedia's account of Oromo history suggests a very different reading, describing its purpose as "ostensibly" to serve the diaspora ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)). The two accounts do not agree.

**Homeland politics, and how the Tigray war landed here.** Conflicts in Ethiopia are felt personally here, and Minnesotans do not agree about them. This page reports who said what and does not take a side.

- Abdul Dire, an Ethiopian immigrant in Minnesota and author of *Oromo Witness*, wrote in December 2020 that the diaspora remained split along ethnic lines and within groups, between supporters and opponents of Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed. "If you want to understand how the ethnic strife plays out here in Twin Cities, follow the flags," he wrote. He also described people being harassed by their own community online that summer, and urged restraint because posts can affect people in both countries. This is one writer's opinion ([Sahan Journal, Dec. 23, 2020](https://sahanjournal.com/oromo-in-minnesota/commentary-ethiopia-tigray-abiy-reform-peace-minnesota/)).
- A year into the war, Sahan Journal quoted Minnesotans, none of them Tigrayan. Mekfira Hussein, president of the St. Paul-based Oromo Sports Federation in North America, said she had lost family, held Abiy responsible for harms to Oromo people, and saw the Oromo Liberation Army's advance as positive. A Minneapolis addiction counselor said the Tigray People's Liberation Front was "attempting to come back to power" and warned that claims were hard to verify during a blackout. A Somali Minnesotan who once backed Abiy hoped the country's groups could stop the killing and talk ([Sahan Journal, Nov. 24, 2021](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/ethiopia-tigray-civil-war/)). The article describes no Tigrayan or Amhara Minnesotan.
- One Tigrayan voice that did reach print came from a college campus. In November 2022, Macalester senior Feven Gebresilassie, described by the student paper as the college's only Tigrayan student, held an information session and fundraiser on the war's second anniversary. Two days after the ceasefire she said, "It feels too soon to celebrate." She described waiting to speak until she felt ready, because the subject was so personal to her and her family, and said that doing it alone was "a very difficult and unique experience" ([The Mac Weekly, Nov. 10, 2022](https://themacweekly.com/2022/11/macalester-student-raises-awareness-for-the-war-on-tigray/)). She speaks for herself, not for Tigrayan Minnesotans as a group.
- No Amhara Minnesotan's own account of the wars in Tigray or Amhara was found in any readable source, and no Amhara community organization in Minnesota turned up. The nearest public statement is Rep. Omar's 2021 mention of killings of Amhara people (above). That is a gap in the coverage reached here, not a sign that Amhara Minnesotans have nothing to say.
- In July 2020, protesters tied by FOX 9 to the unrest after singer Hachalu Hundessa's killing briefly shut I-35W in Minneapolis, after an earlier march on I-94 in St. Paul ([FOX 9, July 10, 2020](https://www.fox9.com/news/protesters-shut-down-traffic-march-onto-i-35w-bridge-in-minneapolis)).
- In 2008, about 125 people attended a Minneapolis forum with Gambella's regional governor, while others boycotted it. Many wanted to talk about the 2003 killings. The governor called claims against him unfounded and said conditions had improved ([MPR News via AP, May 31, 2008](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2008/05/31/ethopia)). A Worthington Anuak refugee, using a pseudonym for his family's safety, asked in 2016 for prayers and pressure on senators: "We are bleeding" ([The Globe via Grand Forks Herald, 2016](https://www.grandforksherald.com/newsmd/anuak-community-in-worthington-pleads-for-peace-in-ethiopian-homeland)).

Common ground exists too. The soccer tournament, as Chala describes it, brings together Oromo, Amhara, Tigrayan, Somali and Gurage people and Eritrean neighbors, in Orthodox, Catholic, Evangelical and Muslim families ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)). That is his description, and it is worth setting beside Dire's flags rather than choosing between them.

**Race and belonging.** Eskender Yousuf, a second-generation Oromo Twin Citian and University of Minnesota doctoral student, wrote in 2020 that anti-Black attitudes inside African immigrant families, including Ethiopian ones, need to be faced, drawing on his own upbringing ([MinnPost, July 28, 2020](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2020/07/a-call-to-address-anti-blackness-within-african-immigrant-communities/)). Compass leaves people who give Ethiopian ancestry out of its African American profile ([Compass methodology](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/methodology)).

**The building that has not come.** No record turned up that the community center proposal passed. Its HF 3908 summary sought $2.5 million.

## 10. Education

**Attainment.** Among adults 25 and older: 20.5 percent did not finish high school, 23.8 percent have a diploma or GED, 31.6 percent have some college or an associate degree, 16.0 percent a bachelor's, and 8.1 percent a graduate degree ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). That spread runs from little formal schooling to advanced degrees.

**East African Magnet School.** St. Paul Public Schools opened it in 2023 in Frogtown, at 437 Edmund Ave. W. It supports Somali, Amharic, Oromo, Tigrinya, Arabic and Swahili speakers. Enrollment was 253 in late August 2023 against a projection of 244, and the district expected most families to be Somali. Roughly a third of teaching staff were of East African descent at the start ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 4, 2023](https://sahanjournal.com/education/east-african-elementary-magnet-school-st-paul-minnesota/); [CBS Minnesota, Sept. 12, 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/first-school-year-underway-at-st-pauls-new-east-african-magnet-school/)). The school's site shows 2026-27 activity and describes its grades inconsistently ([East African Magnet](https://eastafricanmagnet.spps.org/)).

**Language and faith schooling.** Selassie Cathedral lists Saturday language education, and its Abune Zekarias Scholarship for 2026 goes to graduating members bound for college ([Selassie Cathedral](https://minnesotaselassie.org/)).

**Scholars and young people.** Alul Yesak, a University of Minnesota doctoral candidate, studies identity among second-generation Ethiopian American students in K-12 schools ([University of Minnesota](https://olpd.umn.edu/doctoral-student/alul-yesak)). Dr. Dame Idossa moved to Richfield from Ethiopia at about 8, went through the University of Minnesota and its Minnesota Future Doctors program, and now studies breast cancer disparities at UCSF. "I'm the first in my family to pursue medicine," she said ([Mayo Clinic Alumni Association, October 2026](https://alumniassociation.mayo.edu/featured-alumni/dame-idossa-m-d/)).

Minnesota test scores or college enrollment reported for Ethiopian students as a group could not be found.

## 11. Health and wellbeing, broadly

**People.** Rahel Nardos treats pelvic floor conditions, researches obstetric fistula, spent a year as a staff surgeon at the Addis Ababa Hamlin Fistula Hospital and helped create Ethiopia's first formal urogynecology fellowship ([University of Minnesota Medical School](https://med.umn.edu/bio/rahel-nardos); [UMN Center for Global Health](https://globalhealthcenter.umn.edu/staff/rahel-nardos)). Tseganesh Selameab practices at HCMC ([University of Minnesota](https://med.umn.edu/dm_profile/2494)). Selassie Cathedral lists faith community nursing and social services ([Selassie Cathedral](https://minnesotaselassie.org/)).

**Community health efforts.** Debre Selam Medhanealem has run an annual Health Day 5K run and walk for 11 years as of June 2025 ([Debre Selam Medhanealem](https://www.debreselam.net/?page_id=1363)). The Ethio American Foundation lists therapy referrals and connections to "culturally competent" health and mental health resources ([211 United Way listing](https://search.211unitedway.org/search/a933a5c6-0db7-5bc4-b8c0-81514f816d87)).

**Access.** About 7.3 percent have no health insurance, give or take 2.5 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)).

**What one health guide says.** Stratis Health's undated guide names long-term effects of malnutrition, war trauma and infectious disease as common concerns for Ethiopian immigrants; says fasting may affect how medications are taken; and says data on African populations is often not broken out, which is exactly the problem ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/ethiopians/)). That guide is a starting point, not a measurement of Minnesotans. It uses Census figures from 2014.

**Newcomers.** The Minnesota Department of Health tracks screenings for arrivals, though the 2024 report's Ethiopia-specific count is an arrivals figure, not a health finding ([Minnesota Refugee Health Report 2024](https://www.web.health.state.mn.us/communities/rih/stats/24rhrpt.pdf)).

**Traditional practice.** No Minnesota source turned up on traditional healing, holy water use, or how families combine it with clinic care.

## 12. Disability

**Minnesota evidence is very thin.** Compass reports that 5.5 percent of people have a disability, give or take 1.5 points, and 4.5 percent of those under 65, give or take 1.4 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). It does not break that into types of disability.

The state's own economic chartbook shows how thin the evidence is. It reports disability for working-age adults, 18 to 64, by cultural group, covering serious difficulty with vision, hearing, walking, thinking and memory, self-care or living independently. For Ethiopian Minnesotans it prints no figure, because the survey sample for the group had fewer than 150 respondents, below its threshold for publishing ([Minnesota State Demographic Center, January 2026](https://mn.gov/admin/assets/The%20Economic%20Status%20of%20Minnesotans%202026_tcm36-717691.pdf)). No state source found reports disability for Ethiopian Minnesotans by age or by type.

The Department of Education reports how many English learners also receive special education and in which disability categories (specific learning disability, autism, speech or language impairment and others), but not by home language, so Amharic- or Oromo-speaking students cannot be picked out ([Minnesota Department of Education](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED676212.pdf)).

**Questions that number cannot answer.**

- How much of the low figure reflects a very young population, with only 3.5 percent 65 or older?
- Does a survey in English, or a translated word for "disability," capture what families mean? Compass does not say, and no Minnesota study of it turned up for Amharic, Afaan Oromoo, Tigrinya or Anuak speakers.
- Who decides what counts as disability in a given family: a parent, a priest, a school, a county worker, a doctor? No Minnesota source on this turned up.
- What does a label open or close? Does it open school services or a waiver? Does it also carry costs for a family, and which? No Ethiopian-specific Minnesota account turned up.
- What produced an impairment, when one exists? Stratis Health names war trauma and malnutrition in general terms ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/ethiopians/)). A Worthington organizer reported an injury at a meat plant ([The Globe, 2014](https://www.dglobe.com/news/christians-helping-christians-first-lutheran-church-lends-hand-to-worthington-based-ethiopian-orthodox-members)). Neither tells us how common these paths are here, and neither says anything about a people.
- Why does a group's number sit where it does? No source here has established the reason for this community.

**Autism.** The University of Minnesota's public reports name Somali and Hmong children; the April 2025 summary reports nothing on Ethiopian or other East African children ([Institute on Community Integration, April 2025](https://ici.umn.edu/news/new-autism-prevalence-findings-in-minnesota)). Somali findings must not be borrowed for Ethiopian families, and the fuller September 2025 briefing would not open.

As national background only: a 2023 study described itself as the first systematic look at Ethiopian and Eritrean families raising autistic children in the United States. Fifty-one parents answered an online survey. They reported little prior knowledge of autism in their communities, mixed experiences with doctors during diagnosis, provider shortages and costs, dissatisfaction with school and behavioral supports, and effects on their own ability to work; they asked for accessible support groups ([Ataro Adere, Dababnah and Habayeb, Journal of Autism and Developmental Disorders, Sept. 5, 2023, via Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1007/s10803-023-06102-7&resultType=core&format=json)). The abstract does not say how many parents lived in Minnesota, and its findings describe those 51 families, not Ethiopian Minnesotans.

**Services.** PACER Center, Minnesota's parent center for families of children with disabilities, began making short special education videos for parents in eight languages after a 2023 grant; a case study about that work names Amharic among the eight languages it calls most spoken in Minnesota after English, though the page does not list which languages the videos use ([Synthesia case study on PACER Center](https://www.synthesia.io/case-studies/pacer-center)). Disability Hub MN, a free statewide line at 1-866-333-2466, says it serves all languages; it does not name Amharic, so a family should ask ([Disability Hub MN](https://disabilityhubmn.org/)). A&A Reliable Home Health Care was serving seniors and disabled people in St. Paul as of 2021 ([American Immigration Council](https://www.americanimmigrationcouncil.org/story/ethiopian-immigrant-couple-start-home-care-business-in-minnesota/)). Selassie Cathedral's site lists a 2025 church accessibility project application, a small sign that a congregation is thinking about access ([Selassie Cathedral](https://minnesotaselassie.org/)). The Ethiopian Community in Minnesota's center plan does not mention disability by name ([HF 3908 project summary](https://www.house.mn.gov/comm/docs/herYhSCSj0izw9flFc5c4g.pdf)).

**People.** No publicly self-identified Ethiopian Minnesotan disability leader or advocate turned up. State directory pages and the Disabled Immigrants Association would not open, so this is a gap in what could be read and nothing more.

## 13. What is changing now

**Fighting in northern Ethiopia, fall 2026.** Human Rights Watch reported that fighting erupted on Sept. 22, 2026 in Tigray, Amhara and Afar between Ethiopia's military and forces aligned with the Tigray People's Liberation Front, after clashes and airstrikes in Tigray since August ([Human Rights Watch, Sept. 24, 2026](https://www.hrw.org/news/2026/09/24/ethiopia-renewed-tigray-fighting-shows-need-to-protect-civilians)). Al Jazeera reported on Oct. 15 that Ethiopian forces and allied fighters had entered Aksum and that the TPLF had withdrawn from Mekelle, while Tigrayan fighters held nearby mountains. Each side tells it differently: Ethiopia's foreign minister accused Eritrea of an "all-out invasion," Eritrea's information minister accused Ethiopia of aggression and denied backing the TPLF, and Ethiopia's accusations of Sudanese and Egyptian support are denied by those governments ([Al Jazeera, Oct. 9, 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/9/ethiopia-and-eritrea-accuse-one-another-of-launching-war); [Al Jazeera, Oct. 15, 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/15/ethiopian-forces-expand-control-in-tigray-as-militia-claims-eritrean-losses)). On Oct. 1, 2026, Rep. Ilhan Omar of Minneapolis, who chairs the U.S.-Africa Policy Working Group, joined Reps. John Garamendi and Jim McGovern in saying they were "deeply alarmed by increasing violence in Ethiopia," including a military strike on an aid convoy in Tigray. They said the 2022 Pretoria Agreement "has not been upheld," urged all parties to return to peace talks, and asked the State Department to work with all sides to end the violence and hold those responsible accountable ([Rep. Ilhan Omar, Oct. 1, 2026](https://omar.house.gov/media/press-releases/omar-garamendi-mcgovern-release-statement-hostilities-ethiopia)). The statement does not mention Minnesota constituents.

No Minnesota news story, community statement, vigil or rally about the fall 2026 fighting could be found on a readable page, and no Tigrayan, Amhara or Eritrean Minnesotan's response to it was found in print. That reflects what could be reached, not how families here feel. During the 2020-2022 war, writer Abdul Dire described the diaspora here as split along ethnic lines and within groups (section 9), so it is reasonable to ask, not assume, how a person is doing and where they stand.

**Immigration enforcement.** The federal Operation Metro Surge began in December 2025 and lasted about three months, Human Rights Watch reports ([Human Rights Watch, June 18, 2026](https://www.hrw.org/report/2026/06/18/a-manufactured-crisis/minnesota-communities-terrorized-by-the-federal-government)). Chala wrote that its public targets were Somali Minnesotans but "the chill landed across every East African community here" ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)). MPR News reported that the federation almost did not hold its tournament in Minnesota because of it; spokesperson Yared Negash said enforcement frightened many members, and that seeing protests against ICE gave them "that confidence" to come ([MPR News, June 30, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/06/30/ethiopian-soccer-tournament-draws-thousands-despite-immigration-fears)).

**Temporary Protected Status.** The Department of Homeland Security published a notice on Dec. 15, 2025 ending Ethiopia's designation effective Feb. 13, 2026 ([BAL](https://www.bal.com/immigration-news/united-states-termination-of-ethiopia-tps-designation-effective-feb-13-2026/)). A federal court in Massachusetts paused that in January 2026 and lifted the pause in August; Fragomen reports benefits ended Aug. 18, 2026 while a constitutional challenge continues ([Fragomen, Aug. 18, 2026](https://www.fragomen.com/insights/united-states-federal-district-court-allows-termination-of-tps-for-ethiopia-to-proceed.html)). No count of Minnesotans affected turned up. Chala also writes of partial or total immigrant visa bans covering 39 African countries, Ethiopia and Eritrea among them; that is his summary ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)).

**New buildings and a lost one.** In a single year, one congregation opened a nearly $11 million church in Seward and another lost its new Rosemount home to fire (section 5). Parcelle, the organic cafe group owned by Kamal Mohamed, lists two Minneapolis locations and has North Loop and Wayzata sites marked coming soon ([Parcelle](https://parcelleorganics.com); [KSTP, undated](https://kstp.com/?p=3507838)).

**Generations.** Deressa's presentation (section 5) and Yesak's research (section 10) both turn on how children born here will carry this inheritance. Ahndenet got a Minnesota Humanities Center grant to teach about Ethiopian music and history ([Sahan Journal, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/things-to-do-minneapolis-salsa-fest-ethiopian-jazz-film-block-party/)).

## 14. Strengths and contributions

- Members have built institutions through their own giving and time: $50 at a time at coffee ceremonies ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)), a Worthington congregation raising 40 percent of a building's cost by 2020 ([The Globe, 2020](https://www.dglobe.com/lifestyle/ethiopian-orthodox-church-almost-ready-to-build)), ten people founding a Rochester aid group, and thousands of volunteer hours by Zenebech Aly ([Sahan Journal, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/rochester-minnesota-mcknight-foundation-zenebech-aly-immigrants/)).
- People rallied after the Rosemount fire: another church offered space for worship, and donors gave more than $91,000 ([FOX 9](https://www.fox9.com/news/community-rallies-around-rosemount-ethiopian-orthodox-church-after-fire); [GoFundMe](https://www.gofundme.com/f/support-debre-bisrat-minnesota-to-rebuild)).
- The community brought the largest Ethiopian gathering outside Ethiopia, in Chala's words, to Eagan in a frightening year ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)).
- Among the people named on this page are those who care for Minnesotans in home care and hospitals, teach medicine, run neighborhood restaurants, and make new music from old songs.
- Bolé's owners, after crowdfunding to rebuild, shared some of that money with nearby businesses ([Racket, 2025](https://racketmn.com/brunch-buds-bole-ethiopian-cuisine-bowles-us-over)).
