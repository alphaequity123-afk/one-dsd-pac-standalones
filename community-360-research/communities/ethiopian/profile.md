# Ethiopians in Minnesota
Checked: October 30, 2026

"Ethiopian" here is a wide doorway, not one people. Oromo, Amhara, Tigrayan, Somali, Gurage and other Ethiopian peoples live in Minnesota ([MinnPost, June 23, 2026](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)), as do Anuak people from the Gambella region ([MPR News, May 30, 2008](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2008/05/30/anuak)). It is better to ask a person than to assume. Oromo and Amharic-speaking Minnesotans have their own pages. This page looks at the whole, at what people here have built together, and at the places where they differ.

## 1. People and place

**How many.** Minnesota Compass puts the 2024 total at 53,754, give or take 12,826, and calls that the Census Bureau's best estimate of the community's true size. For almost everything else it uses the pooled 2020 to 2024 American Community Survey, where the base is 45,159, give or take 5,404, because the larger sample is steadier ([Minnesota Compass, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). Two rules shape those numbers. The Census Bureau recodes Oromo answers to Ethiopian, leaving no trace of the original answer, so Oromo Minnesotans who answered that way sit inside this count. And people born in Ethiopia who give Somali ancestry are counted as Somali, not Ethiopian ([Compass methodology, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/methodology)).

Other estimates are all over the map, which is itself worth knowing:

- The Ethiopian Community in Minnesota told legislators that 45,000 to 50,000 people in and around the Twin Cities identify as Ethiopian or Ethiopian American. The document is undated and gives no method ([HF 3908 project summary, Minnesota House, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://www.house.mn.gov/comm/docs/herYhSCSj0izw9flFc5c4g.pdf)).
- Endalkachew Chala, a scholar and community member, wrote in June 2026 that between 35,000 and 50,000 Minnesotans identify as Ethiopian or Ethiopian American ([MinnPost, June 23, 2026](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)).
- Dara Moskowitz Grumdahl wrote of nearly 40,000 in the metro in 2024 ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, July 14, 2024](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/the-new-ethiopian-scene/)).
- An Associated Press story carried by MPR News in 2019 said about 100,000, with no source given. It is far above every other figure found ([MPR News, April 27, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/04/27/ethiopia-consulate-stpaul)).

No count separates Amhara, Oromo, Tigrayan or Anuak Minnesotans by a shared method. Separate community estimates exist. Sahan Journal in 2021 cited about 40,000 Oromo Minnesotans ([Sahan Journal, Nov. 24, 2021](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/ethiopia-tigray-civil-war/)). Akway Cham, then head of the Minneapolis-based Anywaa Community Association in North America, estimated 2,500 to 3,000 Anuak in Minnesota in 2008, thought to be the largest group outside Africa ([MPR News, May 30, 2008](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2008/05/30/anuak)). No Tigrayan, Amhara or Gurage count for Minnesota turned up at all.

**Where.** Compass places 89.7 percent in the Twin Cities and 10.3 percent in Greater Minnesota, each give or take 4.0 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). The International Institute of Minnesota's guide, last updated in January 2017, named Seward and Cedar-Riverside in Minneapolis as the neighborhoods with the largest Ethiopian populations ([Finding Common Ground, International Institute of Minnesota, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://iimn.org/publication/finding-common-ground/)). Chala writes that 26th Street and 30th Avenue South in Seward had no Ethiopian institutions 20 years ago and now holds what community members estimate is the state's largest purpose-built Ethiopian Orthodox church ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)).

Life has also moved outward. In 2023-24 schools in Dakota County reported 276 English learners whose first home language is Amharic, more than Ramsey County (247) or Hennepin County (138), with 72 in Washington County and 65 in Anoka County ([Minnesota Department of Education, English Learners in Minnesota 2023-24](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED676212.pdf)). That counts only English learners, not all Amharic speakers, and the report does not explain the pattern. An Orthodox congregation moved from St. Paul to Rosemount in April 2026 after outgrowing its space ([FOX 9, July 17, 2026](https://www.fox9.com/news/community-rallies-around-rosemount-ethiopian-orthodox-church-after-fire)), and Ethiopian restaurants operate in Maple Grove and New Brighton (section 4).

Beyond the metro:

- **Rochester.** Sahan Journal reported about 800 Ethiopian residents ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 3, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/rochester-minnesota-mcknight-foundation-zenebech-aly-immigrants/)). The Post Bulletin said about 500 in 2022 ([Post Bulletin, Sept. 26, 2022](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/local/smoke-leads-the-way-rochester-ethiopians-mark-the-finding-of-the-cross)).
- **Worthington.** An Orthodox congregation there reported 50 to 100 people on a usual Sunday and about 400 at special events in 2020, drawn from southwest Minnesota, northwest Iowa and southeast South Dakota ([The Globe, Oct. 28, 2020](https://www.dglobe.com/lifestyle/ethiopian-orthodox-church-almost-ready-to-build)). The Globe also reported nearly 300 Anuak refugees in Worthington in 2016, with hundreds more in Austin, the Twin Cities and Sioux Falls ([The Globe via Grand Forks Herald, Feb. 5, 2016](https://www.grandforksherald.com/newsmd/anuak-community-in-worthington-pleads-for-peace-in-ethiopian-homeland)).
- **Austin.** Oballa Oballa, who sits on the city council, has been a public figure here (section 9). A current count of Ethiopian-born or Anuak residents in Austin could not be confirmed.

**How people came.** The first Oromo settlers in Minnesota, Tasissa Moti and Teferi Fufa, arrived as students in 1972. More came after the 1974 coup, refugees from Somalia, Djibouti and Sudan arrived from 1980 and 1981, and more than 200 Oromo lived in the Twin Cities by the mid-1980s ([MNopedia, last modified Aug. 12, 2026](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)). The International Institute's 2017 guide says many professionals and students already in the United States gained asylum in the early 1980s and later arrivals came as refugees, asylees, relatives and through the Diversity Visa Lottery, with growing movement from other states for jobs and family ([International Institute of Minnesota](https://iimn.org/publication/finding-common-ground/)). The Ethiopian Community in Minnesota says Ethiopians have lived here since the 1970s and that it registered with the state in 1995 ([GiveMN, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://www.givemn.org/organization/Ethiopian-Community-In-Minnesota)).

Anuak Minnesotans came by a different road. A 2004 report said more than 2,000 Anuak refugees had come to Minnesota since the early 1990s ([Post Bulletin, May 5, 2004](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/ethiopia-carries-out-genocide-on-tribe)). Human rights groups say more than 400 Anuak were killed in attacks that began in Gambella town on Dec. 13, 2003, and that memory has shaped public life here ([MPR News, May 30, 2008](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2008/05/30/anuak)). Nationally, one 2018 report for the International Organization for Migration describes four waves: elites before 1974, people fleeing the Red Terror from 1974 to 1982, family reunification to 1991, and people fleeing ethnic violence and repression after that ([IOM mapping report, background, not Minnesota-specific](https://ethiopia.iom.int/sites/g/files/tmzbdl996/files/documents/Final%2520Mapping%2520of%2520Ethiopian.pdf)).

Arrivals continue. The Minnesota Department of Health counted 272 primary arrivals from Ethiopia with humanitarian immigration statuses in 2024, out of 4,602 statewide, the fourth-largest group ([Minnesota Refugee Health Report 2024](https://www.web.health.state.mn.us/communities/rih/stats/24rhrpt.pdf)). Compass reports that 22 percent of Ethiopian-born residents have been in the United States five years or less and 26 percent more than 20 years ([Minnesota Compass, 10 things to know about immigration](https://www.mncompass.org/data-insights/featured-trend/10-things-know-about-immigration-minnesota)).

**Generations.** This is a young community. Compass counts 43.8 percent age 17 or younger, 12.5 percent under 5, and only 3.5 percent 65 or older. About 59.8 percent were born abroad, give or take 3.9 points, so roughly four in ten were not ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)).

**Languages.** Among people 5 and older, 76.2 percent speak a language other than English at home and 30.2 percent speak English less than "very well" ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). Chala names Amharic, Oromo and Tigrigna among the languages heard at the 2026 soccer tournament ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)). Amharic and Oromo are each among the 12 most common home languages for Minnesota English learners ([Minnesota Department of Education](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED676212.pdf)). In Worthington in 2020, members spoke many languages but nearly all spoke Amharic, which the church used for services ([The Globe, 2020](https://www.dglobe.com/lifestyle/ethiopian-orthodox-church-almost-ready-to-build)). Anuak is its own language; a Gambella official who addressed Anuak Minnesotans in 2008 answered in Amharic and repeated himself in Anuak ([MPR News via AP, May 31, 2008](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2008/05/31/ethopia)).

## 2. All walks of life

All figures here come from Compass for 2020 to 2024, in 2024 dollars, unless noted ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). They cover the whole umbrella, including Oromo people recoded as Ethiopian. Margins are wide.

- **Comfortable and well-off households.** Median household income is $63,667, give or take $6,426. About 23.2 percent of households take in $100,000 or more, give or take 6.0 points. No wealth or net worth data for this community turned up. Visible signs of success include Bolé's restaurants in St. Paul and Maple Grove, owned by Solomon Haile and Rekik Abaineh ([Bolé, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://boleethiopiancuisine.com/)), and Rahel Nardos, born and raised in Addis Ababa, a University of Minnesota professor and Director of Global Women's Health ([University of Minnesota Medical School, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://med.umn.edu/bio/rahel-nardos)).
- **Professionals and owners.** Among adults 25 and older, 24.1 percent hold a bachelor's degree or more, including 8.1 percent with a graduate or professional degree. Abe and Aster Dalu, a toxicologist and a registered nurse, built A&A Reliable Home Health Care in St. Paul to about 70 employees as of 2021 ([American Immigration Council, Sept. 15, 2021](https://www.americanimmigrationcouncil.org/story/ethiopian-immigrant-couple-start-home-care-business-in-minnesota/)). Amy Tsegaye and Dereje Wudmatas own Erta Ale in Lowertown ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, 2024](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/the-new-ethiopian-scene/)). Tseganesh Selameab grew up in Ethiopia, has lived in Minnesota since middle school, earned her medical degree at the University of Minnesota, and practices at Hennepin County Medical Center ([University of Minnesota, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://med.umn.edu/dm_profile/2494)).
- **Middle-income and working families.** Another 40.5 percent of households earn between $50,000 and $99,999. About 35.6 percent of households own their homes, give or take 6.7 points. Employment among adults 18 to 64 is 78.9 percent: 83.0 percent for men and 74.4 percent for women. Median rent is $1,286. The largest single household type, 33.1 percent, is married couples with children.
- **Families with less.** About 18.2 percent of households take in under $35,000 and another 18.0 percent between $35,000 and $49,999. A further 34.5 percent of people live between the poverty line and twice that line, above it but not by much.
- **People in poverty.** About 10.9 percent of people live below the poverty line, give or take 3.5 points, including 10.4 percent of children (give or take 5.3). Among people 65 and older the figure is 34.2 percent, but the margin of 15.9 points means the true number could be far lower or higher. Renters are 64.4 percent of households, and 41.9 percent of them spend too much of their income on housing. About 20.5 percent of adults 25 and older did not finish high school, and 7.3 percent of people have no health insurance. Zenebech Aly's crisis response program in Rochester exists for families facing sudden costs and loss ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 3, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/rochester-minnesota-mcknight-foundation-zenebech-aly-immigrants/)).

These figures describe the umbrella and cannot show whether incomes or schooling differ among Amhara, Oromo, Tigrayan and Anuak Minnesotans.

## 3. Work and the economy

Compass does not report occupations on its Ethiopian profile, and the Census tables that would show them for Minnesota would not open. What follows comes from individual accounts and should not be read as a portrait of the whole.

**Food and hospitality** is the most visible trade (section 4). **Home care and nursing** recur. A&A's owners took a 10-week course at the Neighborhood Development Center and got technology and bookkeeping help from African Economic Development Solutions ([American Immigration Council](https://www.americanimmigrationcouncil.org/story/ethiopian-immigrant-couple-start-home-care-business-in-minnesota/)). The International Institute of Minnesota tells the story of a nurse from Ethiopia who came through its Medical Career Advancement program and now works in mental health and addiction services at M Health Fairview ([International Institute of Minnesota, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://iimn.org/news/kasahuns-story-a-voice-for-the-patient/)).

**Plants and rural work.** A congregation organizer told The Globe in 2014 that her path ran from John Morrell in Sioux Falls to Campbell's Soup and Swift (now JBS) in Worthington, then dishwashing at Perkins and a travel center, and later running the Queen of Sheba restaurant ([The Globe, March 24, 2014](https://www.dglobe.com/news/christians-helping-christians-first-lutheran-church-lends-hand-to-worthington-based-ethiopian-orthodox-members)). That is one person's story, but it shows how plant jobs and small businesses can sit in the same life. How many Ethiopian or Anuak Minnesotans work in meatpacking today could not be established.

**Getting to work.** Of about 18,700 workers, 77.7 percent travel by car and 10.7 percent by public transit ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)).

**Economic support.** African Economic Development Solutions, in St. Paul, has made business loans since 2008 and offers business development, financial coaching and homeownership education for African immigrants and others ([AEDS, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://www.aeds-mn.org/)). At the groundbreaking for its $4.5 million Little Africa Plaza, economist Bruce Corrie of Concordia University cited about $2.8 billion in buying power among African immigrants in Minnesota, based on a 2015 survey he later updated. That figure covers all African immigrants ([KSTP, undated, opening then expected April 2024](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/cultural-economic-hub-little-africa-plaza-breaks-ground-in-st-paul/)).

**Unions.** Nothing turned up on union membership or labor organizing specific to Ethiopian Minnesotans.

**A big week for the local economy.** Organizers expected a boost to hotels and nearby businesses from the 2026 soccer tournament, and vendor tent rentals and sponsorships helped pay for it ([Power 104.7, June 26](https://www.power1047.fm/post/national-ethiopian-soccer-tournament-comes-to-minnesota); [MPR News, June 30, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/06/30/ethiopian-soccer-tournament-draws-thousands-despite-immigration-fears)).

## 4. Businesses and commercial life

The full list is in `businesses.json`. These are the places a reader is most likely to meet.

**Restaurants.** The Red Sea on Cedar Avenue has served Ethiopian and Eritrean food since 1990, with a bar, dance floor and weekend live music, and claims to be the city's first Ethiopian restaurant ([Mpls With Love, updated October 2026](https://mplswithlove.com/guides/best-ethiopian-east-african-food); [Discover the Cities, June 2021](https://discoverthecities.com/best-ethiopian-food-restaurants-minneapolis-st-paul/)). Longfellow's stretch of Minnehaha Avenue holds Selam, Meseret and Katar River ([Mpls With Love](https://mplswithlove.com/guides/best-ethiopian-east-african-food)). Selam began as a coffee shop in 2007 and became a full restaurant in 2021; Belai Mergia and Rahel Tassew run it with their three daughters ([Minnesota Spokesman-Recorder, Nov. 29, 2024](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2024/11/29/selam-restaurant-ethiopian-gem/)).

Bolé is named for the Addis Ababa neighborhood where its owners grew up. Its original Midway site was destroyed in the 2020 unrest, and crowdfunding helped it reopen near Como Park, where Racket visited for brunch in 2025 ([Discover the Cities](https://discoverthecities.com/best-ethiopian-food-restaurants-minneapolis-st-paul/); [Racket, June 24, 2025](https://racketmn.com/brunch-buds-bole-ethiopian-cuisine-bowles-us-over)). Chef Tigist "TG" Feyisa saw a market among fasting Orthodox Christians and Minnesota vegans when she opened Lulu Ethiovegan, named for her daughter ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, 2024](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/the-new-ethiopian-scene/)). Erta Ale in Lowertown also sells hard-to-find ingredients and stays open late on weekends ([Visit Saint Paul, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/directory/erta-ale-ethiopian-restaurant/)).

**Groceries and bakeries.** Shabelle on East Franklin sells spices, teff flour, coffee beans and injera beside its counter ([Mpls With Love](https://mplswithlove.com/guides/best-ethiopian-east-african-food)). Katar River is both restaurant and bakery. Shega Foods sells takeout and spices ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, updated Feb. 25, 2025](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/black-owned-businesses-in-the-twin-cities/)). East African Bakery in St. Paul supplies injera to other stores and restaurants, and Mamas Market & Deli, run by nine Ethiopian mothers, is listed inside Little Africa Plaza; a current date could not be confirmed for either ([Visit Saint Paul, guide to East African food](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/east-african-food-in-saint-paul/); [Visit Saint Paul, food halls and markets](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/food-halls-and-markets/)).

**Coffee.** Limu Coffee in New Brighton is Ethiopian-owned ([Spokesman-Recorder, June 11, 2020](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2020/06/11/local-black-owned-coffee-house-alternatives-to-starbucks/)) and had a customer review as recently as January 2026 ([Wanderlog](https://www.wanderlog.com/place/details/6159408/limu-coffee)).

**Districts and support.** Little Africa, in St. Paul, is a branding and marketing effort for African immigrant business along transit corridors, centered near Snelling and LaFond avenues ([Visit Saint Paul, AEDS listing](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/directory/aeds-african-economic-development-solutions/); [Visit Saint Paul, food halls](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/blog/food-halls-and-markets/)). No Ethiopian chamber of commerce in Minnesota turned up.

**Professional firms.** Ethiopian-owned law, accounting, tax, insurance, travel, salon and clinic businesses could not be surveyed by name. Their absence from the list means they went unchecked, not that they are not here. See "What could not be established."

## 5. Religion and spiritual life

**Differences matter here.** Chala describes Orthodox, Catholic, Evangelical and Muslim communities in the Twin Cities ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)). Stratis Health's guide also lists Jewish believers and those who practice indigenous African spirituality ([Culture Care Connection, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/ethiopians/)). No count of Minnesotans by faith turned up. In Rochester, community leaders in 2022 estimated about 80 percent of the local Ethiopian community was Orthodox, which describes one city ([Post Bulletin, 2022](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/local/smoke-leads-the-way-rochester-ethiopians-mark-the-finding-of-the-cross)). Nothing turned up on Ethiopian Minnesotans who hold no faith.

**Orthodox Tewahedo.** Chala estimates about eight active Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox congregations in the metro ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)). Those that could be confirmed:

- **Debre Selam Medhanealem**, Seward, 2601 30th Ave. S. About 300 people came to an open house on April 18, 2026. The building cost nearly $11 million with the land; construction began in November 2024. The congregation had worshiped at 4401 Minnehaha Ave. since 1999. Sunday services run 7 to 11 a.m. in Ge'ez and Amharic with some English. Board Chair Debalke Gebeyehu called it "a living bridge" for immigrants ([Longfellow Nokomis Messenger, April 27, 2026](https://www.longfellownokomismessenger.com/stories/debre-selam-medhanealem-ethiopian-orthodox-tewahedo-churchfinds-new-home-in-seward,161305)). Chala says members raised money across two decades, $50 at a time, at coffee ceremonies ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)).
- **Re'ese Adbarat Tserha Aryam Kidist Selassie Cathedral**, 2601 Minnehaha Ave. The cathedral's own history says more than 500 parishioners chose on April 13, 2014 to join the structure of the Holy Synod in Ethiopia and held their first full liturgy outside "the old church building" that Easter ([Selassie Cathedral, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://minnesotaselassie.org/index.php/clergy/brief-history)). Its site lists Saturday language education, Friday faith education, faith community nursing, and a 2026 scholarship ([Selassie Cathedral](https://minnesotaselassie.org/)).
- **Debre Bisrat St. Gabriel and St. Arsema**, about 200 members. It moved from St. Paul to 3285 144th St. W., Rosemount, on April 19, 2026. A fire on July 13, 2026 did heavy damage to the building; police believed it was empty, no injuries were reported, and the cause had not been determined in these reports ([KSTP, July 13, 2026](https://kstp.com/?p=3974644); [FOX 9, July 13, 2026](https://www.fox9.com/news/authorities-respond-structure-fire-rosemount-church); [FOX 9, July 17, 2026](https://www.fox9.com/news/community-rallies-around-rosemount-ethiopian-orthodox-church-after-fire)). Deacon Bisrat Gebre said, "It's like a really bad dream." The congregation's fundraiser showed $91,227 toward a $1 million goal when checked ([GoFundMe, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://www.gofundme.com/f/support-debre-bisrat-minnesota-to-rebuild)).
- **Menbere Tsehay Sent Mary**, Worthington. Members once drove to Sioux Falls or Sioux City, then rented at First Lutheran from 2013. In 2020 they were raising money to build on Minnesota 60 ([The Globe, 2020](https://www.dglobe.com/lifestyle/ethiopian-orthodox-church-almost-ready-to-build)). Whether it was built could not be confirmed.
- **Rochester.** Orthodox Christians there marked Meskel at Cascade Lake in 2021 and 2022 (section 6).
- **St. Mary Tigray Orthodox Tewahdo Church**, St. Paul, is the name on a YouTube channel ([YouTube, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://www.youtube.com/@st.maryeotcminnesota8903/about)). Nothing more about the congregation could be confirmed.

**Fasting** shapes the year. Grumdahl reports that observant Tewahedo Christians over age 7 fast about 180 days a year and clergy about 250, avoiding meat and dairy ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, 2024](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/the-new-ethiopian-scene/)). Stratis Health notes Wednesdays, Fridays and 55 days before Easter ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/ethiopians/)).

**Protestant.** The Evangelical Mekane Yesus Lutheran Church Fellowship in Minnesota, 1566 W. Thomas Ave., St. Paul, reported an average of 64 people a week for 2025 ([Lutheran Church-Missouri Synod locator, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://locator.lcms.org/church/C/1001496)). In Worthington in 2016, Anuak residents gathered at the Christian Reformed Church and Westminster Presbyterian ([The Globe via Grand Forks Herald, 2016](https://www.grandforksherald.com/newsmd/anuak-community-in-worthington-pleads-for-peace-in-ethiopian-homeland)). Samuel Deressa of Concordia University, St. Paul, presented in 2023 on how Ethiopian immigrant congregations in Minneapolis and St. Paul struggle to bring second and third generations into community life ([Concordia Seminary Multiethnic Symposium, 2023](https://scholar.csl.edu/mucu/2023/schedule/7/)).

**Muslim life.** MinnPost names Ethiopian Muslim communities without naming mosques. The Oromo page is the place for Oromo mosques. A named mosque built around other Ethiopian Muslim communities in Minnesota could not be established, nor how they mark Ramadan and Eid as distinct from the wider Muslim community here.

## 6. Celebrations and observances

- **Enkutatash, the Ethiopian New Year,** falls on Sept. 11, or Sept. 12 in some years ([Ethiopia Visitors Hub, for 2026](https://ethiopiavisitorshub.com/ethiopian-september-holidays-guide/)). The Ethiopian Community in Minnesota invited people to a free celebration at Highland Park Pavilion, St. Paul, on Sept. 13, 2026, with food, music and dance and an invitation to wear traditional dress ([Mefthe listing, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://mefthe.com/events/celebrate-ethiopian-new-year-in-minnesota/)). Its website also shows a 2011 New Year celebration at Matthews Park ([Ethiopian Community in Minnesota, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://ethiopiancommunitymn.org/)).
- **Meskel and Demera.** In 2022, more than 100 people in Rochester held a service, walked to Cascade Lake Beach and lit a bonfire topped by a cross, on the Sunday nearest the Sept. 27 feast so more could come. Eyob Adehane told the Post Bulletin that a cross the year before had been misread as a possible cross burning and was dismantled ([Post Bulletin, Sept. 26, 2022](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/local/smoke-leads-the-way-rochester-ethiopians-mark-the-finding-of-the-cross)). That small story says much about being visible in a Minnesota town.
- **Timket (Timkat),** Epiphany, Jan. 19. Chala writes that on Timket in 2026 many families watched immigration agents' movements on WhatsApp instead of walking to church, and a parishioner told him, "God understands. But it still hurts" ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)). Selassie Cathedral once announced a two-day Timket "in unity for the first time in Minnesota" on Jan. 17 and 18; the page gives no year ([Selassie Cathedral](http://minnesotaselassie.org/index.php/announcements/60-timket)).
- **Genna,** Christmas, usually Jan. 7, and **Fasika,** Easter. A short video is titled "This Minnesota Church Celebrates Christmas in January," though the page did not show which church ([YouTube](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/o2AkWDp4Ah4)). No solid Minnesota details turned up for Fasika.
- **Ethiopian Sports Federation in North America tournament.** The 43rd annual gathering ran June 27 to July 4, 2026, mostly at TCO Stadium in Eagan with some games at Saint Thomas Academy ([ESFNA, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://esfna.org/tournament/)). Board member Elias Meles described 32 teams, about 30 vendors, nonprofit and church booths, a concert at the Omni Hotel, and more than 20,000 expected visitors ([MPR News, June 25, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2026/06/25/ethiopian-soccer-tournament-twin-cities)). Gov. Tim Walz declared July 3 Ethiopian Day ([MPR News, June 30, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/06/30/ethiopian-soccer-tournament-draws-thousands-despite-immigration-fears)).
- **Ashenda,** which a 2026 report calls a holiday marking the end of the 15-day Filseta fast, matters to many Tigrayan people ([Africanews, Aug. 23, 2026](https://www.africanews.com/amp/2026/08/23/ethiopia-tigrayan-women-celebrate-ashenda-amid-fears-of-new-war/)). No Minnesota celebration could be confirmed.
- **Adwa Victory Day,** March 2. No Minnesota celebration turned up.
- **Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha and Mawlid.** Observed by Ethiopian Muslim Minnesotans in ways that could not be documented separately.
- **Irreecha,** the Oromo thanksgiving, belongs on the Oromo page.

## 7. Family, home and daily life

**Households.** Compass counts about 12,436 households averaging 3.30 people. Married couples with children under 18 are 33.1 percent, people living alone 26.1 percent, and single women with children under 18 are 10.4 percent, give or take 4.4 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). More than a quarter living alone is worth noticing beside the picture of large families. Compass does not say who they are.

**Food and coffee.** At Selam, guests can join a coffee ceremony in which beans are roasted, hand-ground and brewed in a clay jebena ([Spokesman-Recorder, 2024](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2024/11/29/selam-restaurant-ethiopian-gem/)). Coffee ceremonies also helped raise money for a church (section 5).

**Childhood and passing things on.** A congregation organizer in Worthington said in 2014 that boys are baptized at 40 days and girls at 80 ([The Globe, 2014](https://www.dglobe.com/news/christians-helping-christians-first-lutheran-church-lends-hand-to-worthington-based-ethiopian-orthodox-members)). Asbe Hailu, a federation board member, said of the soccer tournament, "We instill the love of their heritage" ([Power 104.7](https://www.power1047.fm/post/national-ethiopian-soccer-tournament-comes-to-minnesota)). Tensae Fayise, raised in Worthington, called her mother "the bridge for me to go home" ([YourClassical, Feb. 18, 2020](https://www.yourclassical.org/story/2020/02/18/fayise-cedar-commissions)). Stratis Health also notes that adoptions of Ethiopian children by white American families left some adoptees feeling a loss of homeland and identity ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/ethiopians/)).

**Aging.** Just 3.5 percent are 65 or older, so aging in a new country may be new ground for many families, though no source here measures that. The Ethiopian Community in Minnesota's center proposal includes a seniors' room and speaks of keeping elders from isolation ([HF 3908 project summary](https://www.house.mn.gov/comm/docs/herYhSCSj0izw9flFc5c4g.pdf)). Stratis Health says religious beliefs shape end-of-life preferences for many ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/ethiopians/)).

**Marriage and dress.** Little specific to Minnesota turned up. Yousuf's essay (section 9) touches on how some relatives viewed marriage to African Americans. Traditional dress appears in event invitations.

## 8. Arts, media, sports and culture

**Music.** Drummer and composer Abinnet Berhanu, born to Ethiopian parents and raised in Virginia, moved to Minneapolis in 2020. His band Ahndenet, Amharic for "unity," with vocalist Genet Abate, arranges traditional Ethiopian songs with jazz, funk, blues and soul, and played a free show at the Cedar on July 23, 2025 ([Sahan Journal, July 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/things-to-do-minneapolis-salsa-fest-ethiopian-jazz-film-block-party/)). Composer Tensae Fayise, born in Sioux Falls and raised in Worthington, plays the krar and wrote "Ye Terrarou Tenfash" for the Cedar Commissions in 2020 ([YourClassical](https://www.yourclassical.org/story/2020/02/18/fayise-cedar-commissions)). The Current named Yaba Gobana as a Minneapolis artist of Ethiopian origin in 2021 ([The Current, Sept. 28, 2021](https://www.thecurrent.org/feature/2021/09/28/the-come-up-a-summer-recap-and-early-fall-jams-from-11-minnesota-artists)). The Cedar Cultural Center and The Green Room have also hosted touring Ethiopian acts ([Sahan Journal, April 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/weekend-arts-ethiopian-music-jungle-theater-opera-cradle-will-rock/); [Jazz88, Sept. 21, 2023](https://www.jazz88.fm/2023/09/21/yohannes-tona-and-dibekulu-bring-ethio-jazz-to-the-twin-cities/)).

**Media.** ZeHabesha had a new post on Oct. 30, 2026; its own page says it was founded in 2005 ([ZeHabesha, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://zehabesha.com/)). Sahan Journal said in 2020 that Henok A. Degfu founded it in 2008 and that it was based in Burnsville, and reported that Ethiopian Airlines was suing it for $25 million over three interviews. The article's last update was July 15, 2020, with no ruling, and no outcome could be found ([Sahan Journal, July 2, 2020](https://sahanjournal.com/news/ethiopian-airlines-africas-largest-carrier-sues-minnesota-journalist-and-his-independent-newspaper/)). Stratis Health lists KFAI's Voices of Ethiopia; its current schedule could not be confirmed ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/ethiopians/)).

**Sports.** Minnesota's own team played in the 2026 federation tournament, in Division II Group C, beating Austin 7-0 and drawing San Diego and Phoenix ([ESFNA](https://esfna.org/tournament/)). Minnesota had been chosen to host in 2020 before COVID canceled it ([MPR News, June 25, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2026/06/25/ethiopian-soccer-tournament-twin-cities)). No Minnesota source turned up on Ethiopian-born runners or other athletes based here.

**Cultural centers.** No Ethiopian community center building could be confirmed (section 9). A planned African history and cultural museum was part of the Little Africa Plaza plan; AEDS's founder said nothing like it existed in Minnesota ([KSTP](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/cultural-economic-hub-little-africa-plaza-breaks-ground-in-st-paul/)). Its status could not be confirmed.

## 9. Civic and political life

**Elected office.** Oballa Oballa holds Austin's Ward 3 council seat. KAAL reports the council chose him 5 to 1 after Joyce Poshusta resigned, that he earlier served two and a half years in Ward 1, and that he announced he would seek reelection, naming housing, childcare, public safety and youth among his priorities. KAAL calls him Austin's first African American councilmember ([KAAL, appointment](https://www.kaaltv.com/?p=875617); [KAAL, reelection, about May 2026](https://www.kaaltv.com/?p=1107841)). No Ethiopian-born member of the Minnesota Legislature turned up, though this could only be checked in part.

**Organizations.**

- **Ethiopian Community in Minnesota,** 616 Rice St., St. Paul, with office hours Monday and Friday. It offers notary service, help with documents, referrals, references for first-time renters and cultural activities, and describes itself as open to Ethiopians of any religion, language or ethnicity ([ECM, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://ethiopiancommunitymn.org/contact/); [ECM home](https://ethiopiancommunitymn.org/); [GuideStar](https://www.guidestar.org/profile/41-1885932)). On GiveMN it reports about 400 registered members, though that page's latest dated event is from 2012 ([GiveMN](https://www.givemn.org/organization/Ethiopian-Community-In-Minnesota)). It told legislators it worked from an eight-by-ten-foot office and wanted a center on University Avenue ([HF 3908 project summary](https://www.house.mn.gov/comm/docs/herYhSCSj0izw9flFc5c4g.pdf)).
- **Ethiopian Tigray Community in Minnesota,** 1821 University Ave. W., St. Paul, a charity since 2012 with filings through 2024. It reports annual community events, recognition of high school graduates and college prep workshops ([GuideStar](https://www.guidestar.org/profile/47-0855873); [Instrumentl, 2024 filing](https://www.instrumentl.com/990-report/ethiopian-tigray-community-in-minnesota)).
- **Ethiopian Community Rochester Organization,** founded in 2020 by ten Ethiopians and serving any immigrant in similar need. Co-founder Zenebech Aly won a 2024 McKnight Foundation Heart of Community Honor ([Sahan Journal, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/rochester-minnesota-mcknight-foundation-zenebech-aly-immigrants/)).
- **Anuak groups.** Akuthi Okoth, who moved to Minnesota in 2000, co-founded the Anuak Women Community group in March 2003 ([Cultural Survival, May 7, 2010](https://www.culturalsurvival.org/node/3721)). Whether any Anuak organization is active today could not be confirmed.
- **The Consulate General of Ethiopia,** on University Avenue in St. Paul, opened in 2019. Consul Ewnetu Bilata Debela said he would serve people "without any prejudice to their ethnic background" and acknowledged favoritism had been a weakness of past consulates. Teshite Wako, an Oromo community leader, saw the opening as a sign of change ([MPR News via AP, April 27, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/04/27/ethiopia-consulate-stpaul)). MNopedia's account of Oromo history suggests a very different reading, describing its purpose as "ostensibly" to serve the diaspora ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)). The two accounts do not agree.

**Homeland politics, and how the Tigray war landed here.** Conflicts in Ethiopia are felt personally here, and Minnesotans do not agree about them. This page reports who said what and does not take a side.

- Abdul Dire, an Ethiopian immigrant in Minnesota and author of *Oromo Witness*, wrote in December 2020 that the diaspora remained split along ethnic lines and within groups, between supporters and opponents of Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed. "If you want to understand how the ethnic strife plays out here in Twin Cities, follow the flags," he wrote. He also described people being harassed by their own community online that summer, and urged restraint because posts can affect people in both countries. This is one writer's opinion ([Sahan Journal, Dec. 23, 2020](https://sahanjournal.com/oromo-in-minnesota/commentary-ethiopia-tigray-abiy-reform-peace-minnesota/)).
- A year into the war, Sahan Journal quoted Minnesotans, none of them Tigrayan. Mekfira Hussein, president of the St. Paul-based Oromo Sports Federation in North America, said she had lost family, held Abiy responsible for harms to Oromo people, and saw the Oromo Liberation Army's advance as positive. A Minneapolis addiction counselor said the Tigray People's Liberation Front was "attempting to come back to power" and warned that claims were hard to verify during a blackout. A Somali Minnesotan who once backed Abiy hoped the country's groups could stop the killing and talk ([Sahan Journal, Nov. 24, 2021](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/ethiopia-tigray-civil-war/)). The article describes no Tigrayan or Amhara Minnesotan, and in the reporting that could be opened, those voices are largely missing. That is a gap in the coverage reached here, not a sign that those Minnesotans have nothing to say.
- In July 2020, protesters tied by FOX 9 to the unrest after singer Hachalu Hundessa's killing briefly shut I-35W in Minneapolis, after an earlier march on I-94 in St. Paul ([FOX 9, July 10, 2020](https://www.fox9.com/news/protesters-shut-down-traffic-march-onto-i-35w-bridge-in-minneapolis)).
- In 2008, about 125 people attended a Minneapolis forum with Gambella's regional governor, while others boycotted it. Many wanted to talk about the 2003 killings. The governor called claims against him unfounded and said conditions had improved ([MPR News via AP, May 31, 2008](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2008/05/31/ethopia)). A Worthington Anuak refugee, using a pseudonym for his family's safety, asked in 2016 for prayers and pressure on senators: "We are bleeding" ([The Globe via Grand Forks Herald, 2016](https://www.grandforksherald.com/newsmd/anuak-community-in-worthington-pleads-for-peace-in-ethiopian-homeland)).

Common ground exists too. The soccer tournament, as Chala describes it, brings together Oromo, Amhara, Tigrayan, Somali and Gurage people and Eritrean neighbors, in Orthodox, Catholic, Evangelical and Muslim families ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)). That is his description, and it is worth setting beside Dire's flags rather than choosing between them.

**Race and belonging.** Eskender Yousuf, a second-generation Oromo Twin Citian and University of Minnesota doctoral student, wrote in 2020 that anti-Black attitudes inside African immigrant families, including Ethiopian ones, need to be faced, drawing on his own upbringing ([MinnPost, July 28, 2020](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2020/07/a-call-to-address-anti-blackness-within-african-immigrant-communities/)). Compass leaves people who give Ethiopian ancestry out of its African American profile ([Compass methodology](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/methodology)).

**The building that has not come.** No record turned up that the community center proposal passed. Its HF 3908 summary sought $2.5 million.

## 10. Education

**Attainment.** Among adults 25 and older: 20.5 percent did not finish high school, 23.8 percent have a diploma or GED, 31.6 percent have some college or an associate degree, 16.0 percent a bachelor's, and 8.1 percent a graduate degree ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). That spread runs from little formal schooling to advanced degrees.

**East African Magnet School.** St. Paul Public Schools opened it in 2023 in Frogtown, at 437 Edmund Ave. W. It supports Somali, Amharic, Oromo, Tigrinya, Arabic and Swahili speakers. Enrollment was 253 in late August 2023 against a projection of 244, and the district expected most families to be Somali. Roughly a third of teaching staff were of East African descent at the start ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 4, 2023](https://sahanjournal.com/education/east-african-elementary-magnet-school-st-paul-minnesota/); [CBS Minnesota, Sept. 12, 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/first-school-year-underway-at-st-pauls-new-east-african-magnet-school/)). The school's site shows 2026-27 activity and describes its grades inconsistently ([East African Magnet, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://eastafricanmagnet.spps.org/)).

**Language and faith schooling.** Selassie Cathedral lists Saturday language education, and its Abune Zekarias Scholarship for 2026 goes to graduating members bound for college ([Selassie Cathedral](https://minnesotaselassie.org/)).

**Scholars and young people.** Alul Yesak, a University of Minnesota doctoral candidate, studies identity among second-generation Ethiopian American students in K-12 schools ([University of Minnesota, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://olpd.umn.edu/doctoral-student/alul-yesak)). Dr. Dame Idossa moved to Richfield from Ethiopia at about 8, went through the University of Minnesota and its Minnesota Future Doctors program, and now studies breast cancer disparities at UCSF. "I'm the first in my family to pursue medicine," she said ([Mayo Clinic Alumni Association, October 2026](https://alumniassociation.mayo.edu/featured-alumni/dame-idossa-m-d/)).

Minnesota test scores or college enrollment reported for Ethiopian students as a group could not be found.

## 11. Health and wellbeing, broadly

**People.** Rahel Nardos treats pelvic floor conditions, researches obstetric fistula, spent a year as a staff surgeon at the Addis Ababa Hamlin Fistula Hospital and helped create Ethiopia's first formal urogynecology fellowship ([University of Minnesota Medical School](https://med.umn.edu/bio/rahel-nardos); [UMN Center for Global Health](https://globalhealthcenter.umn.edu/staff/rahel-nardos)). Tseganesh Selameab practices at HCMC ([University of Minnesota](https://med.umn.edu/dm_profile/2494)). Selassie Cathedral lists faith community nursing and social services ([Selassie Cathedral](https://minnesotaselassie.org/)).

**Access.** About 7.3 percent have no health insurance, give or take 2.5 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)).

**What one health guide says.** Stratis Health's undated guide names long-term effects of malnutrition, war trauma and infectious disease as common concerns for Ethiopian immigrants; says fasting may affect how medications are taken; and says data on African populations is often not broken out, which is exactly the problem ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/ethiopians/)). That guide is a starting point, not a measurement of Minnesotans. It uses Census figures from 2014.

**Newcomers.** The Minnesota Department of Health tracks screenings for arrivals, though the 2024 report's Ethiopia-specific count is an arrivals figure, not a health finding ([Minnesota Refugee Health Report 2024](https://www.web.health.state.mn.us/communities/rih/stats/24rhrpt.pdf)).

**Traditional practice.** No Minnesota source turned up on traditional healing, holy water use, or how families combine it with clinic care.

## 12. Disability

**Minnesota evidence is very thin.** Compass reports that 5.5 percent of people have a disability, give or take 1.5 points, and 4.5 percent of those under 65, give or take 1.4 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). It does not break that into types of disability.

**Questions that number cannot answer.**

- How much of the low figure reflects a very young population, with only 3.5 percent 65 or older?
- Does a survey in English, or a translated word for "disability," capture what families mean? Compass does not say, and no Minnesota study of it turned up for Amharic, Afaan Oromoo, Tigrinya or Anuak speakers.
- Who decides what counts as disability in a given family: a parent, a priest, a school, a county worker, a doctor? No Minnesota source on this turned up.
- What does a label open or close? Does it open school services or a waiver? Does it also carry costs for a family, and which? No Ethiopian-specific Minnesota account turned up.
- What produced an impairment, when one exists? Stratis Health names war trauma and malnutrition in general terms ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/ethiopians/)). A Worthington organizer reported an injury at a meat plant ([The Globe, 2014](https://www.dglobe.com/news/christians-helping-christians-first-lutheran-church-lends-hand-to-worthington-based-ethiopian-orthodox-members)). Neither tells us how common these paths are here, and neither says anything about a people.
- Why does a group's number sit where it does? No source here has established the reason for this community.

**Autism.** The University of Minnesota's public reports name Somali and Hmong children; the April 2025 summary reports nothing on Ethiopian or other East African children ([Institute on Community Integration, April 2025](https://ici.umn.edu/news/new-autism-prevalence-findings-in-minnesota)). Somali findings must not be borrowed for Ethiopian families, and the fuller September 2025 briefing would not open.

**Services.** Disability Hub MN, a free statewide line at 1-866-333-2466, says it serves all languages; it does not name Amharic, so a family should ask ([Disability Hub MN, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://disabilityhubmn.org/)). A&A Reliable Home Health Care was serving seniors and disabled people in St. Paul as of 2021 ([American Immigration Council](https://www.americanimmigrationcouncil.org/story/ethiopian-immigrant-couple-start-home-care-business-in-minnesota/)). Selassie Cathedral's site lists a 2025 church accessibility project application, a small sign that a congregation is thinking about access ([Selassie Cathedral](https://minnesotaselassie.org/)). The Ethiopian Community in Minnesota's center plan does not mention disability by name ([HF 3908 project summary](https://www.house.mn.gov/comm/docs/herYhSCSj0izw9flFc5c4g.pdf)).

**People.** No publicly self-identified Ethiopian Minnesotan disability leader or advocate turned up. State directory pages and the Disabled Immigrants Association would not open, so this is a gap in what could be read and nothing more.

## 13. What is changing now

**Fighting in northern Ethiopia, October 2026.** Al Jazeera reported on Oct. 15 that Ethiopian forces and allied fighters had entered Aksum and that the TPLF had withdrawn from Mekelle, while Tigrayan fighters held nearby mountains. Each side tells it differently: Ethiopia's foreign minister accused Eritrea of an "all-out invasion," Eritrea's information minister accused Ethiopia of aggression and denied backing the TPLF, and Ethiopia's accusations of Sudanese and Egyptian support are denied by those governments ([Al Jazeera, Oct. 9, 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/9/ethiopia-and-eritrea-accuse-one-another-of-launching-war); [Al Jazeera, Oct. 15, 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/15/ethiopian-forces-expand-control-in-tigray-as-militia-claims-eritrean-losses)). No Minnesota reporting on how this is reaching families here could be found or opened. It is reasonable to ask, not assume, how a person is doing, and not to assume which side of it they are on.

**Immigration enforcement.** The federal Operation Metro Surge began in December 2025 and lasted about three months, Human Rights Watch reports ([Human Rights Watch, June 18, 2026](https://www.hrw.org/report/2026/06/18/a-manufactured-crisis/minnesota-communities-terrorized-by-the-federal-government)). Chala wrote that its public targets were Somali Minnesotans but "the chill landed across every East African community here" ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)). MPR News reported that the federation almost did not hold its tournament in Minnesota because of it; spokesperson Yared Negash said enforcement frightened many members, and that seeing protests against ICE gave them "that confidence" to come ([MPR News, June 30, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/06/30/ethiopian-soccer-tournament-draws-thousands-despite-immigration-fears)).

**Temporary Protected Status.** The Department of Homeland Security published a notice on Dec. 15, 2025 ending Ethiopia's designation effective Feb. 13, 2026 ([BAL](https://www.bal.com/immigration-news/united-states-termination-of-ethiopia-tps-designation-effective-feb-13-2026/)). A federal court in Massachusetts paused that in January 2026 and lifted the pause in August; Fragomen reports benefits ended Aug. 18, 2026 while a constitutional challenge continues ([Fragomen, Aug. 18, 2026](https://www.fragomen.com/insights/united-states-federal-district-court-allows-termination-of-tps-for-ethiopia-to-proceed.html)). No count of Minnesotans affected turned up. Chala also writes of partial or total immigrant visa bans covering 39 African countries, Ethiopia and Eritrea among them; that is his summary ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)).

**New buildings and a lost one.** In a single year, one congregation opened a nearly $11 million church in Seward and another lost its new Rosemount home to fire (section 5). Parcelle, the organic cafe group owned by Kamal Mohamed, lists two Minneapolis locations and has North Loop and Wayzata sites marked coming soon ([Parcelle, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://parcelleorganics.com); [KSTP, undated](https://kstp.com/?p=3507838)).

**Generations.** Deressa's presentation (section 5) and Yesak's research (section 10) both turn on how children born here will carry this inheritance. Ahndenet got a Minnesota Humanities Center grant to teach about Ethiopian music and history ([Sahan Journal, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/things-to-do-minneapolis-salsa-fest-ethiopian-jazz-film-block-party/)).

## 14. Strengths and contributions

- Members have built institutions through their own giving and time: $50 at a time at coffee ceremonies ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)), a Worthington congregation raising 40 percent of a building's cost by 2020 ([The Globe, 2020](https://www.dglobe.com/lifestyle/ethiopian-orthodox-church-almost-ready-to-build)), ten people founding a Rochester aid group, and thousands of volunteer hours by Zenebech Aly ([Sahan Journal, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/rochester-minnesota-mcknight-foundation-zenebech-aly-immigrants/)).
- People rallied after the Rosemount fire: another church offered space for worship, and donors gave more than $91,000 ([FOX 9](https://www.fox9.com/news/community-rallies-around-rosemount-ethiopian-orthodox-church-after-fire); [GoFundMe](https://www.gofundme.com/f/support-debre-bisrat-minnesota-to-rebuild)).
- The community brought the largest Ethiopian gathering outside Ethiopia, in Chala's words, to Eagan in a frightening year ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)).
- Among the people named on this page are those who care for Minnesotans in home care and hospitals, teach medicine, run neighborhood restaurants, and make new music from old songs.
- Bolé's owners, after crowdfunding to rebuild, shared some of that money with nearby businesses ([Racket, 2025](https://racketmn.com/brunch-buds-bole-ethiopian-cuisine-bowles-us-over)).
