# Puerto Ricans in Minnesota
Checked: October 31, 2026

A note on standing before the numbers. People born in Puerto Rico are U.S. citizens by birth. Congress made Puerto Ricans citizens on March 2, 1917, through the Jones-Shafroth Act ([Immigration History, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://immigrationhistory.org/item/jones-shafroth-act/)). Puerto Rico is a U.S. territory, ceded by Spain in 1898; people living on the island cannot vote in federal elections, and the territory has one House delegate with limited voting privileges and no senators ([Pew Research Center, Feb. 5, 2026, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/02/05/key-findings-about-puerto-rico/)). A move from San Juan to St. Paul is a move within the United States, like a move from Chicago. Once living in Minnesota, Puerto Ricans vote here like any other resident citizen. This profile is about Puerto Rican Minnesotans as their own people. Minnesota's Latino and Mexican communities have separate profiles.

## 1. People and place

**How many.** Minnesota Compass, using the American Community Survey (ACS) for 2020 to 2024, counts 22,925 Puerto Rican Minnesotans, give or take 2,722 ([Minnesota Compass, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/puerto-rican)). That margin is wide: the true number could reasonably be anywhere from about 20,200 to 25,600. The Minnesota Historical Society (MNHS) calls Puerto Ricans the state's second-largest Latino community, after people of Mexican heritage ([MNHS, Minnesota Unraveled episode 201, Oct. 9, 2025](https://www.mnhs.org/unraveled/episode-201)). For scale, Pew estimates about 6.1 million people of Puerto Rican origin in the 50 states and D.C. as of 2024, more than the roughly 3.2 million living in Puerto Rico itself ([Pew, Feb. 5, 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/02/05/key-findings-about-puerto-rico/)). Minnesota's share is small next to Florida, New York or Pennsylvania, which Pew lists as the largest.

**Growth, with caution.** Counts have risen across sources, but each uses a different survey and year. MPR News cited an estimate of about 13,000 Puerto Ricans in Minnesota in 2017 ([MPR News, Sept. 22, 2017](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2017/09/22/we-cannot-communicate-with-our-families-puerto-ricans-in-minnesota-push-for-aid)). A 2022 University of Minnesota StoryMap cited a 2020 Compass figure of about 16,943, with 72.4 percent in the seven-county Twin Cities ([Tabatha Cruz, "Becoming SotaRicans," 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). Sahan Journal cited about 17,700 from the 2022 one-year ACS ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 20, 2023](https://sahanjournal.com/climate-environment/copal-climate-trip-puerto-rico/)). Compass now reports 22,925 for 2020 to 2024. These numbers point in one direction, but they are not a precise growth curve.

**Where.** Compass places 67.1 percent in the Twin Cities and 32.9 percent in Greater Minnesota, each give or take 5 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/puerto-rican)). One in three living outside the metro is a large share. The 2022 StoryMap, drawing on ACS county data for 2020, named Olmsted, Stearns, Wright, Clay, Sherburne and Carlton counties as places with notable Puerto Rican populations, near Rochester, St. Cloud, Moorhead and Duluth ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). Within St. Paul, the West Side is a named home. Artist Tearra Oso was born and raised there, and María Isa Pérez-Vega has called herself a Puerto Rican "girl from the West Side" ([MNHS transcript, 2025](https://www.mnhs.org/hubfs/MNHS-Minnesota-Unraveled-EP201-Transcript.pdf); [Sahan Journal, Nov. 8, 2022](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/maria-isa-wins-minnesota-house-west-side-st-paul-elections-2022/)). News stories have also placed recent arrivals in Eagan, north Minneapolis, Long Prairie, Alexandria and Roseau ([MPR News/Sahan Journal, Aug. 21, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/08/21/in-hurricanes-wake-puerto-ricans-find-safe-harbor-in-minnesota); [Star Tribune via Roseau Civic and Commerce, March 7, 2020](https://goroseau.com/star-tribune-puerto-rican-workers-bring-diversity-to-roseau/)).

**A young community.** In the Compass profile, 38.6 percent are 17 or younger and only 4.9 percent are 65 or older. Children under 5 alone are 11.1 percent ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/puerto-rican)). This is a community of children, young adults and parents more than elders.

**Born here, born on the island, born elsewhere on the mainland.** Compass lists 1.3 percent as "foreign-born." That figure is low because the Census counts people born in Puerto Rico as U.S.-born, not because few were born on the island ([Pew fact sheet, 2021 data, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/race-and-ethnicity/fact-sheet/us-hispanics-facts-on-puerto-rican-origin-latinos/)). No Minnesota figure was found for the share born in Puerto Rico. Nationally, Pew reports that 69 percent of mainland Puerto Ricans were born on the mainland and 27 percent in Puerto Rico ([Pew, Feb. 5, 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/02/05/key-findings-about-puerto-rico/)). That is national background, not a Minnesota number.

**People move often.** Compass reports that 21.8 percent of Puerto Rican Minnesotans lived at a different U.S. address one year earlier, give or take 3.8 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/puerto-rican)). That category includes moves from Puerto Rico and moves within Minnesota alike.

**How people came.** MNHS historian Chantel Rodríguez traces the earliest documented Puerto Rican in Minnesota to the 1890 Census: a 20-year-old woman recorded as Johaquina, living in Duluth and married to a soldier. Through the 20th century, she says, Puerto Ricans came in small numbers as seasonal farmworkers, factory workers and college students, and in larger numbers after Hurricane María in 2017 ([MNHS transcript](https://www.mnhs.org/hubfs/MNHS-Minnesota-Unraveled-EP201-Transcript.pdf)). The 2022 StoryMap adds census records of a Puerto Rican-born teenager boarding in the same Duluth household and a blacksmith of Puerto Rican descent in St. Cloud in 1930. Drawing on MNHS's Latino Líderes oral histories, it also describes moves from New York's Lower East Side in the mid-1970s ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)).

College was one door. Carlos Mariani, raised on Chicago's West Side by Puerto Rican parents, came to Macalester College; he later represented St. Paul in the Minnesota House from 1991 through 2022 ([Minnesota Legislative Reference Library, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/legdb/fulldetail?ID=10399)). In his oral history, as quoted in the StoryMap, he recalled a 1975 Macalester orientation for Latino students, most of them Puerto Rican or Mexican ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). Former state Sen. Melisa López Franzen, who grew up in Aguadilla, moved to Minnesota for graduate and law school ([Twin Cities Business, 2024](https://tcbmag.com/notable/melisa-lopez-franzen/); [LRL](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/legdb/fulldetail?ID=15379)). Saxophonist José Antonio Zayas Cabán, who grew up in Mayagüez, describes Puerto Ricans arriving "to make a living, to get our education, to escape climate catastrophes" ([MNHS transcript](https://www.mnhs.org/hubfs/MNHS-Minnesota-Unraveled-EP201-Transcript.pdf)).

**After Hurricanes Irma and María (2017).** Hurricane María made landfall on September 20, 2017, two weeks after Irma ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). Pew reports that the largest net move from Puerto Rico to the mainland came in 2018, about 110,000 people ([Pew, Feb. 5, 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/02/05/key-findings-about-puerto-rico/)). In Minnesota, the counts vary by who is counting:

- In December 2017 the Coalition of Boricuas in Minnesota knew of about 50 families, close to 200 people, who had arrived ([FOX 9, Dec. 23, 2017](https://www.fox9.com/news/minnesota-helps-puerto-ricans-relocate-after-disaster)).
- By August 2019, local support groups estimated more than 300 families ([MPR News/Sahan Journal, Aug. 21, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/08/21/in-hurricanes-wake-puerto-ricans-find-safe-harbor-in-minnesota)).
- The 2022 StoryMap found far smaller federal counts: survey microdata showing 85 people moving from Puerto Rico to Minnesota in 2017 to 2019, and 78 people placed in Minnesota by FEMA. It notes that these leave out Puerto Ricans who first moved to Florida or New York ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)).

The gap may come from small survey samples, second moves through other states, and local groups counting families rather than people. No source settles it.

**Recruited for work.** In 2019 Polaris hired a Puerto Rican recruiting firm to staff its plant in Roseau, a town of about 2,600 near the Canadian border. The Star Tribune reported in March 2020 that 30 Puerto Rican workers and their families had arrived ([Star Tribune via Roseau Civic and Commerce, March 7, 2020](https://goroseau.com/star-tribune-puerto-rican-workers-bring-diversity-to-roseau/)). That story also describes a pattern of Minnesota employers seeing island workers, who need no visa, as an answer to rural labor shortages.

**Languages.** Among Puerto Rican Minnesotans 5 and older, 62.0 percent speak only English at home, 38.0 percent speak another language, and 10.7 percent speak English less than "very well" ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/puerto-rican)). Compass does not name the other language, though Spanish is the likely one. Households differ. Tearra Oso, raised in St. Paul, said she did not grow up speaking Spanish at home and learned it in school. Zayas Cabán said some newcomers were encouraged to "lose the accent," and some interviewees said they felt they belonged neither here nor there ([MNHS transcript](https://www.mnhs.org/hubfs/MNHS-Minnesota-Unraveled-EP201-Transcript.pdf)). The word "SotaRican" for Puerto Rican Minnesotans is credited to María Isa, whose record label is called SotaRico ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)).

## 2. All walks of life

All figures here are from the Compass 2020 to 2024 profile, in 2024 dollars, unless noted ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/puerto-rican)). Margins are wide because the survey sample is small.

- **Comfortable and well-off households.** About a third of households, 32.3 percent, take in $100,000 or more. Among adults 25 and older, 41.4 percent hold a bachelor's degree or more, including 18.3 percent with a graduate or professional degree. In a 2024 analysis, Compass described Puerto Rican adults as more likely than Hispanic and Latino Minnesotans overall to finish high school and earn a degree ([Minnesota Compass, Sept. 18, 2024](https://www.mncompass.org/data-insights/articles/importance-data-disaggregation-minnesotas-hispanic-and-latino-communities)).
- **Professionals and owners in public view.** They include a neurologist and University of Minnesota professor, Dr. Miguel Fiol, trained at the University of Puerto Rico ([University of Minnesota Medical School, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://med.umn.edu/bio/miguel-fiol)); a former Senate minority leader turned University of Minnesota executive and Minnesota Hispanic Bar Association president, López Franzen ([Twin Cities Business, 2024](https://tcbmag.com/notable/melisa-lopez-franzen/)); Alberto Monserrate, "Puerto Rican by birth," who built a Spanish-language media and marketing business and was the first Latino on the Minneapolis school board ([Twin Cities Business, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://tcbmag.com/tcb-100-people/alberto-monserrate/)); and owner-artists such as Ricardo Levins Morales, the McKnight Foundation's 2024 Distinguished Artist ([McKnight Foundation, 2024](https://www.mcknight.org/news-ideas/mcknight-names-ricardo-levins-morales-2024-distinguished-artist/)).
- **Middle-income and working families.** The median household income is $61,985, give or take $16,011. Owners live in 46.4 percent of households and renters in 53.6 percent. Among adults 18 to 64, 80.6 percent are employed: 86.5 percent of men and 74.1 percent of women. The Roseau factory workers profiled in 2020, one earning almost $20 an hour after a promotion, are one face of this group ([Star Tribune via Roseau Civic and Commerce](https://goroseau.com/star-tribune-puerto-rican-workers-bring-diversity-to-roseau/)).
- **Families under strain.** About 28.2 percent of households take in less than $35,000. Overall, 10.7 percent of people live below the poverty line, give or take 3.8 points, and another 21.1 percent live between the line and twice that amount. Among people 65 and older, the poverty rate is 17.6 percent, with a very wide margin of 10.9 points. Among renters, 44.2 percent are cost-burdened, meaning they spend 30 percent or more of income on housing. One in six households, 16.4 percent, has no vehicle. And 8.1 percent of adults did not finish high school.

**Over time.** Compass's 2024 article, using an earlier survey period, put Puerto Rican home ownership at 35 percent ([Compass, Sept. 18, 2024](https://www.mncompass.org/data-insights/articles/importance-data-disaggregation-minnesotas-hispanic-and-latino-communities)). The 2020 to 2024 profile shows 46.4 percent, give or take 7.8. The survey periods overlap and the margins are wide, so this may or may not be a real change.

**Hardship by arrival, not only by income.** People who came after María described specific barriers. A Puerto Rican couple in Eagan told Sahan Journal that a Walmart employee refused to cash their checks without a "U.S." address, though Puerto Rico is part of the United States. Minnesota public assistance required 30 days of residency, with a hardship exception that required a written statement. Volunteers met families at the airport in subzero weather with coats ([MPR News/Sahan Journal, Aug. 21, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/08/21/in-hurricanes-wake-puerto-ricans-find-safe-harbor-in-minnesota)). In Roseau, a former network engineer from San Juan whose house had been paid off before he lost his job started over on a factory line ([Star Tribune via Roseau Civic and Commerce](https://goroseau.com/star-tribune-puerto-rican-workers-bring-diversity-to-roseau/)). Middle-class standing on the island did not always travel with people.

## 3. Work and the economy

**What the numbers show.** Employment is high: 80.6 percent of adults 18 to 64 work, and most commute by car (82.5 percent of workers) ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/puerto-rican)). Compass does not break out occupations or industries for Puerto Rican Minnesotans, and no other Minnesota source did.

**Early work.** MNHS describes 20th-century Puerto Rican arrivals as seasonal farmworkers, manufacturing workers and students ([MNHS transcript](https://www.mnhs.org/hubfs/MNHS-Minnesota-Unraveled-EP201-Transcript.pdf)). A 1999 song by the Minnesota bomba and plena group Proyecto La Plena, "Llegué de Puerto Rico," describes arriving cold, without direction, searching for work, and overcoming isolation, nostalgia and "winter in Minnesota" (in the StoryMap's translation) ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)).

**Manufacturing recruitment.** Polaris's Roseau plant had about 70 openings at a time when it began recruiting in Puerto Rico. Workers described pay about double what factory work paid at home, with benefits. One had earned $10.81 an hour at a medical device plant on the island before taking 12-hour night shifts bending tubes at Polaris. Newcomers also took jobs at a hotel and a grocery bakery in town ([Star Tribune via Roseau Civic and Commerce, March 7, 2020](https://goroseau.com/star-tribune-puerto-rican-workers-bring-diversity-to-roseau/)). In 2019, Sahan Journal reported families choosing Long Prairie and Alexandria for higher-paying jobs; the Coalition of Boricuas had pointed families toward Long Prairie, St. Cloud and Rochester ([MPR/Sahan, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/08/21/in-hurricanes-wake-puerto-ricans-find-safe-harbor-in-minnesota); [FOX 9, 2017](https://www.fox9.com/news/minnesota-helps-puerto-ricans-relocate-after-disaster)). No source named the Long Prairie or Alexandria employers.

**Professions, public service and the arts.** Public careers include medicine and research ([Fiol](https://med.umn.edu/bio/miguel-fiol)), law and government relations ([López Franzen](https://tcbmag.com/notable/melisa-lopez-franzen/)), public relations and media ([Monserrate](https://tcbmag.com/tcb-100-people/alberto-monserrate/)), and nonprofit leadership, such as Zayas Cabán at the transportation group Our Streets ([Our Streets, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.ourstreetsmn.org/about/staff/jose-antonio-zayas-caban/)).

**Labor history.** Ricardo Levins Morales co-founded the Northland Poster Collective after moving to Minnesota in 1976. McKnight says it worked for more than 30 years alongside unions and other movements ([McKnight Foundation, 2024](https://www.mcknight.org/news-ideas/mcknight-names-ricardo-levins-morales-2024-distinguished-artist/)). Racket reported that staff at Bichota, a Puerto Rican-owned coffee shop, unionized almost immediately after it opened in 2024 ([Racket, 2024](https://racketmn.com/best-new-restaurants-minneapolis-st-paul-2024)). No source was found on Puerto Rican union membership as a group in Minnesota.

**Entrepreneurship.** See section 4. Recorded businesses are few and small. Several are owner-artist ventures rather than storefront restaurants.

## 4. Businesses and commercial life

No Puerto Rican commercial district was found in Minnesota, and Minnesota sources name only a handful of Puerto Rican-owned businesses. The list below is short because few Minnesota sources name such businesses.

- **Bichota Coffee**, 3740 Chicago Ave., Minneapolis, in George Floyd Square. Racket reported in October 2024 that the owner, C. Anderson, is Puerto Rican and that the name comes from the Puerto Rican slang term "bichote" ([Racket, Oct. 7, 2024](https://racketmn.com/a-new-coffee-shop-bichota-is-open-in-george-floyd-square)). KSTP covered the grand opening ([KSTP, 2024](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/new-coffee-shop-at-george-floyd-square-holds-grand-opening/)). Its own site lists current coffees and says the café is open at that address ([Bichota Coffee, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://bichotacoffee.com/)).
- **Ricardo Levins Morales Art Studio**, 3260 Minnehaha Ave., Minneapolis, sells the artist's prints, posters and buttons. Its site shows 2026 items, including a "Global South: Puerto Rico" collection ([RLM Art Studio, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.rlmartstudio.com/)).
- **NewPublica**, 2909 Bryant Ave. S., Minneapolis, is a multicultural public relations firm with 2026 posts on its site ([NewPublica, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://newpublica.com/)). Twin Cities Business said in 2024 that López Franzen had been its president ([TCB, 2024](https://tcbmag.com/notable/melisa-lopez-franzen/)).
- **La Borinqua**, a Puerto Rican food trailer based in Albert Lea and run by Amanda Velazquez, served plantain nachos and a mostly gluten-free menu built on her late father-in-law's recipes ([Owatonna People's Press, 2022](https://www.southernminn.com/owatonna_peoples_press/news/new-food-truck-brings-a-gluten-free-taste-of-puerto-rico-to-downtown-thursday/article_0ec38406-fc95-11ec-a8f5-ebbe049f14fe.html)). No 2025 or 2026 sign of operation was found.
- **El Jibarito**, a family-owned Puerto Rican restaurant and food truck, was listed in the 2022 StoryMap ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). Its current status could not be confirmed.
- **Sosa Foods & Deli**, a small Minneapolis takeout spot with mofongo at the center of its menu, was reviewed in 2012 ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, July 1, 2012](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/quick-and-tasty-bites/)). No current sign of operation was found.
- **SotaRico**, the production company and record label founded by María Isa ([Sahan Journal, Nov. 8, 2022](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/maria-isa-wins-minnesota-house-west-side-st-paul-elections-2022/); [Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). Its current activity could not be confirmed.

**Wider Latino commerce.** El Burrito Mercado on St. Paul's West Side has a mural of Latino leaders that includes the Puerto Rican baseball player Roberto Clemente ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). Spanish-language media such as La Prensa de Minnesota, which still publishes, serve Latino readers broadly rather than Puerto Ricans in particular ([La Prensa de Minnesota, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://laprensademn.com/)).

**Not found.** No Minnesota source was found for a Puerto Rican bakery, grocery, law or accounting firm, travel agency or chamber of commerce. Several people in news stories said they hoped to open restaurants: a Roseau worker hoped to serve mofongo and alcapurrias, and an Eagan couple planned a restaurant ([Star Tribune via Roseau Civic and Commerce](https://goroseau.com/star-tribune-puerto-rican-workers-bring-diversity-to-roseau/); [MPR/Sahan, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/08/21/in-hurricanes-wake-puerto-ricans-find-safe-harbor-in-minnesota)). Whether those plans happened was not found.

## 5. Religion and spiritual life

**What Minnesota sources show is limited.** No Minnesota source was found naming a Puerto Rican congregation, a Puerto Rican-led parish, or survey data on the faith of Puerto Rican Minnesotans. What does exist:

- In Roseau, Puerto Rican newcomers worshiped at local churches. The pastor of Roseau Evangelical Covenant Church spoke publicly in favor of their arrival ([Star Tribune via Roseau Civic and Commerce, 2020](https://goroseau.com/star-tribune-puerto-rican-workers-bring-diversity-to-roseau/)).
- After María, Minnesota church groups did rebuilding work on the island. The Minnesota Conference of the United Methodist Church coordinated trips, and an Owatonna group worked on houses near Arecibo in March 2019 ([Steele County Times, 2019](https://www.steelecountytimes.com/mission-possible)).
- For Tearra Oso, bomba is "like my spirituality," and she describes drumming as a way of connecting with ancestors ([MNHS transcript, 2025](https://www.mnhs.org/hubfs/MNHS-Minnesota-Unraveled-EP201-Transcript.pdf)).
- Christmas and Three Kings Day are the most visible religious-cultural observances in Minnesota sources (section 6).

Practices often associated with the island, such as Catholic devotions, Pentecostal and evangelical churches, Espiritismo or Santería, were not documented in any Minnesota source found. Their presence here, and the share of people with no religion, could not be established.

## 6. Celebrations and observances

**Christmas season and the Parranda Boricua.** Kenneth Ortiz, board co-chair of the BORIKEN Cultural Center, told AMPERS radio that Puerto Rico has "the longest Christmas celebrated in the world." The center gathers that season into one free day, La Parranda Boricua, with live music, the custom of visiting friends with music, and the Three Kings bringing candy and gifts for children ([AMPERS North Star Stories, Dec. 18, 2025](https://ampers.org/la-parranda-boricua-brings-a-puerto-rican-christmas-to-minnesota/)). Executive director Marisol Chiclana-Ayala told AMPERS the center often uses Academia César Chávez in St. Paul for youth programs and hosts bigger gatherings in Shoreview. The Minnesota State Arts Board awarded the center $35,000 in its 2026 grants for its annual Parranda Boricua ([Minnesota State Arts Board, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.arts.state.mn.us/grants/2026/2026-awarded-ce.htm)). No date was found for the 2026 Parranda.

**Three Kings Day (January 6).** January 6 is a public holiday in Puerto Rico ([Platea PR, 2026 calendar](https://www.plateapr.com/en/what-to-know/see-the-2026-puerto-rico-holiday-calendar); [El Nuevo Día, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.elnuevodia.com/dias-feriados-en-puerto-rico/)). In Minnesota, the Three Kings appear at the Parranda Boricua ([AMPERS](https://ampers.org/la-parranda-boricua-brings-a-puerto-rican-christmas-to-minnesota/)). One Roseau family told the Star Tribune that in their first Minnesota winter, Three Kings Day, celebrated loudly at home, passed without notice in town ([Star Tribune via Roseau Civic and Commerce](https://goroseau.com/star-tribune-puerto-rican-workers-bring-diversity-to-roseau/)).

**Island civic days.** Puerto Rico's 2026 public calendar includes American Citizenship Day (March 2, marking the 1917 Jones-Shafroth Act), Abolition of Slavery Day (March 22), Good Friday, Puerto Rico Constitution Day (July 25), José Celso Barbosa Day (July 27) and November 19, now called Día de la Puertorriqueñidad y el Descubrimiento de Puerto Rico ([El Nuevo Día](https://www.elnuevodia.com/dias-feriados-en-puerto-rico/); [Platea PR](https://www.plateapr.com/en/what-to-know/see-the-2026-puerto-rico-holiday-calendar)). No Minnesota celebration of any of these days was found.

**Remembering Hurricane María (September 20).** On the fifth anniversary, September 20, 2022, about two dozen people held a vigil beside the Puerto Rican mural at El Colegio High School in Minneapolis and raised money after Hurricane Fiona ([MPR News via Echo Press, Sept. 2022](https://www.echopress.com/news/minnesota/minnesotans-with-puerto-rico-ties-remember-maria-work-to-help-recovery-from-fiona)). No 2025 or 2026 Minnesota vigil was found.

**Encuentro Boricua.** The 2022 StoryMap describes a summer gathering begun in 2002 that draws hundreds of Puerto Ricans from across the state, and says it marked its 20th year in 2022 ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). No date or announcement for 2025 or 2026 was found.

**Other community events.** BORIKEN lists a family baseball and games day, "Jugando Pelota a lo Boricua," among its free events ([GiveMN, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.givemn.org/organization/Boriken-Cultural-Center)). Pedacito de Tierra had its world premiere at the Minnesota History Center on January 15, 2026, a free event that sold out, with a second show at the Cedar Cultural Center on January 16 ([MNHS event page](https://www.mnhs.org/events/36171090195); [MNHS release, Jan. 6, 2026](https://www.mnhs.org/media/news/pedacito-concert-0)). The release's "When" line prints the year as 2025, but the release is dated January 6, 2026, and the event page confirms 2026.

**Hispanic Heritage Month.** This national month is a Latino umbrella observance that begins in mid-September. As a vice-presidential candidate in 2024, Gov. Tim Walz said Minnesota had a long history of being home to Puerto Rican and Latino families ([Puerto Rico Report, 2024](https://puertoricoreport.com/puerto-rico-and-minnesota/)). No Puerto Rican-specific Minnesota event tied to the month was found.

**Not found in Minnesota.** No Minnesota event was found for the Noche de San Juan (June 23 to 24), a Puerto Rican parade, or a Puerto Rican festival like those in Chicago or Milwaukee.

## 7. Family, home and daily life

**Households.** Compass counts 7,115 Puerto Rican households, averaging 2.32 people. About 35 percent are married-couple households, roughly half of them with children under 18; 14.7 percent are unmarried couples; 6.0 percent are single mothers with children; and 31.5 percent are people living alone, give or take 9.1 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/puerto-rican)). The large share of young adults and of people living alone fits a community that includes many students and recent movers, but no source has studied that directly.

**Ties to the island.** After María, Puerto Rican Minnesotans spoke of sleepless nights waiting for word from relatives, such as an 82-year-old great-aunt or a 94-year-old grandmother on oxygen ([MPR News, Sept. 22, 2017](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2017/09/22/we-cannot-communicate-with-our-families-puerto-ricans-in-minnesota-push-for-aid)). Families brought relatives north. FOX 9 described a Minnesota family welcoming parents from Aibonito and an adult brother who relies on oxygen, after friends raised money for charter flights ([FOX 9, 2017](https://www.fox9.com/news/heartfelt-reunion-minnesota-family-welcomes-puerto-rican-loved-ones-thanks-to-community-effort)). Some arrivals said they planned to return; others said their children now wanted to stay ([MPR/Sahan, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/08/21/in-hurricanes-wake-puerto-ricans-find-safe-harbor-in-minnesota)). Richfield council member Simon Trautmann, of Puerto Rican descent, described the diaspora's role in supporting island recovery outside official channels ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 20, 2023](https://sahanjournal.com/climate-environment/copal-climate-trip-puerto-rico/)).

**Food.** Dishes named in Minnesota stories include arroz con gandules, mofongo, tostones, alcapurrias, plantain chips and roast pork ([FOX 9](https://www.fox9.com/news/heartfelt-reunion-minnesota-family-welcomes-puerto-rican-loved-ones-thanks-to-community-effort); [Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, 2012](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/quick-and-tasty-bites/); [Star Tribune via Roseau Civic and Commerce](https://goroseau.com/star-tribune-puerto-rican-workers-bring-diversity-to-roseau/); [Owatonna People's Press, 2022](https://www.southernminn.com/owatonna_peoples_press/news/new-food-truck-brings-a-gluten-free-taste-of-puerto-rico-to-downtown-thursday/article_0ec38406-fc95-11ec-a8f5-ebbe049f14fe.html)). A Puerto Rican newcomer cooked 80 pounds of pork for Roseau's welcome party.

**Winter and adjustment.** Newcomers described first snowmen and sledding, a winter survival kit in the car, and a quiet New Year's Eve ([Star Tribune via Roseau Civic and Commerce](https://goroseau.com/star-tribune-puerto-rican-workers-bring-diversity-to-roseau/)). Rafael Ortega recalled that he "froze to death" on his first visit from New York, and Alberto Monserrate remembered how quiet and orderly Minnesota seemed ([oral histories quoted in Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)).

**Names and identity.** Zayas Cabán asks people to use all four of his names because they say where he is from ([MNHS transcript](https://www.mnhs.org/hubfs/MNHS-Minnesota-Unraveled-EP201-Transcript.pdf)). Some Puerto Rican Minnesotans also have other heritages: Oso is Puerto Rican and Mexican, with an Afro-Puerto Rican grandmother ([MNHS transcript](https://www.mnhs.org/hubfs/MNHS-Minnesota-Unraveled-EP201-Transcript.pdf)).

**Not found.** No Minnesota source covered marriage customs, dress, childhood or aging in Puerto Rican families as a group.

## 8. Arts, media, sports and culture

**Bomba and plena in Minnesota.** Bomba and plena are Afro-Puerto Rican drum, song and dance traditions. Tearra Oso describes bomba as created by enslaved West Africans on Puerto Rico's sugar plantations, played on drums made from barrels, with a call-and-response chorus and a "conversation" between lead drummer and dancer ([MNHS transcript, 2025](https://www.mnhs.org/hubfs/MNHS-Minnesota-Unraveled-EP201-Transcript.pdf)). These traditions have been taught in St. Paul for decades:

- Oso learned bomba from age 7 on the West Side, from Mila Llaugher, who led an Afro-Boricua dance, drum and vocal school called El Arco Iris ([MNHS transcript](https://www.mnhs.org/hubfs/MNHS-Minnesota-Unraveled-EP201-Transcript.pdf); [Voyage Minnesota, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://voyageminnesota.com/interview/meet-tearra-oso-of-minneapolis-world/)). The 2022 StoryMap describes El Arco Iris Center for the Arts as established in 1993 and later renamed the BORIKEN Cultural Center ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). BORIKEN's own fundraising page gives its founding as 2019 ([GiveMN](https://www.givemn.org/organization/Boriken-Cultural-Center)); the two dates may describe the earlier school and the current nonprofit.
- Puerto Rican-Minnesotan artist and musician Ricardo Gómez led the bomba and plena group Proyecto La Plena. MNHS holds two vejigante masks he made around 1995 for the group's parades and performances ([MNopedia, "Minnesotanos," last modified Jan. 14, 2026](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-minnesota)), and the group's 1999 CD, "¡Bomba & plena en Minnesota!" ([MNHS, episode 201](https://www.mnhs.org/unraveled/episode-201)).
- BORIKEN's performing group plays bomba and plena at schools, libraries and festivals, including CLUES's AfroFusión exhibition opening in St. Paul in February 2024 ([CLUES, 2024](https://clues.org/join-us-for-the-opening-reception-of-the-new-art-exhibition-afrofusion/)).
- Oso created "BombaPop," mixing bomba with pop, funk and R&B in English and Spanish. MNHS notes that her 2022 album PREZ was named one of the state's top 10 albums by the Star Tribune ([MNHS, episode 201](https://www.mnhs.org/unraveled/episode-201); [The Current, May 10, 2022](https://www.thecurrent.org/feature/2022/05/10/new-music-from-minnesota-musicians-to-pump-up-in-may)). She leads weekly drum circles at St. Paul's Black Youth Healing Arts Center and Indigenous Roots Cultural Arts Center, where people of many African-diaspora backgrounds play together ([MNHS transcript](https://www.mnhs.org/hubfs/MNHS-Minnesota-Unraveled-EP201-Transcript.pdf)).

**Concert music.** Zayas Cabán, a multi-Grammy-nominated saxophonist, McKnight Fellow and co-founder of the trio {trés}, commissioned composer Angélica Negrón to build Pedacito de Tierra from interviews with Puerto Ricans in the Twin Cities. Eleven people were interviewed, including the two artists ([MNHS, episode 201](https://www.mnhs.org/unraveled/episode-201); [MNHS transcript](https://www.mnhs.org/hubfs/MNHS-Minnesota-Unraveled-EP201-Transcript.pdf)). The January 2026 premiere included Grammy-winning saxophonist Miguel Zenón, a companion exhibit with art by Ricardo Levins Morales, and a reading of the children's book "Daniela y Mateo Viajan a Puerto Rico" ([MNHS release, Jan. 6, 2026](https://www.mnhs.org/media/news/pedacito-concert-0)). MNHS described it as part of Zayas Cabán's final tour.

**Hip-hop and popular music.** María Isa built a career as a rapper and singer before entering the Legislature, and owns the production company SotaRico ([Sahan Journal, 2022](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/maria-isa-wins-minnesota-house-west-side-st-paul-elections-2022/)). Tufawon (Rafael Gonzalez), a Minneapolis rapper whom The Current described as Puerto Rican and Dakota, turned a 2017 show into a benefit for Puerto Rico. He said "the voices of our small Boricua community are clear and they're loud" ([The Current, Oct. 4, 2017](https://www.thecurrent.org/feature/2017/10/04/minnesota-musicians-come-together-to-help-puerto-rico)).

**Visual art.** Levins Morales, born into Puerto Rico's anti-colonial movement, moved to Minnesota in 1976 and makes posters and prints for social movements from his south Minneapolis studio. McKnight named him its 2024 Distinguished Artist ([McKnight Foundation](https://www.mcknight.org/news-ideas/mcknight-names-ricardo-levins-morales-2024-distinguished-artist/); [RLM Art Studio](https://www.rlmartstudio.com/about/about-the-artist/)). At El Colegio High School in Minneapolis, the mural "Ritmo y Raíces de Resistencia" by Olivia Levins Holden and Maria Robinson centers Afro-Boricua roots, Puerto Rican women in history and the nationalist leader Pedro Albizu Campos ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). CLUES's 2024 exhibition included Maelo Cruz, a cartoonist born in Puerto Rico and living in Duluth, and Raye Cordes, a Puerto Rican fiber sculptor ([CLUES, 2024](https://clues.org/join-us-for-the-opening-reception-of-the-new-art-exhibition-afrofusion/)).

**Museums and archives.** MNHS holds Latino Líderes oral histories that include five Puerto Rican narrators ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)), along with a 1968 "Puertorriqueños" button supporting Hubert Humphrey's presidential campaign and the Proyecto La Plena masks ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-minnesota)). Its podcast, Minnesota Unraveled, opened its second season with an episode on Puerto Rican music in Minnesota ([MNHS, Oct. 9, 2025](https://www.mnhs.org/unraveled/episode-201)).

**Baseball.** Hall of Famer Orlando Cepeda, from Ponce, played for the St. Cloud Rox in 1956 ([Northwoods League, St. Cloud Rox history](https://northwoodsleague.com/st-cloud-rox/team/history-of-the-rox/)). The Minnesota Twins have had many Puerto Rican players. In 2023 El Nuevo Día counted seven "Bori-Twins" headed to the World Baseball Classic for Puerto Rico, and Carlos Correa was the main attraction at TwinsFest that January ([El Nuevo Día, Feb. 23, 2023](https://www.elnuevodia.com/deportes/beisbol/notas/con-mucha-bulla-siete-boricuas-de-los-twins-entrenan-a-todo-vapor-para-el-clasico-mundial/); [Primera Hora, Jan. 28, 2023](https://www.primerahora.com/deportes/beisbol/notas/carlos-correa-recibe-el-calor-de-los-fanaticos-en-minnesota/)). Players are visiting professionals rather than long-term residents. Closer to home, Roseau's town ball manager recruited Puerto Rican newcomers ([Star Tribune via Roseau Civic and Commerce](https://goroseau.com/star-tribune-puerto-rican-workers-bring-diversity-to-roseau/)), and BORIKEN teaches baseball and dominoes as Puerto Rican pastimes ([GiveMN](https://www.givemn.org/organization/Boriken-Cultural-Center)). Miguel Ramos, then senior director of diversity, equity and inclusion for the Twins, spoke at the 2022 María vigil ([Echo Press/MPR, 2022](https://www.echopress.com/news/minnesota/minnesotans-with-puerto-rico-ties-remember-maria-work-to-help-recovery-from-fiona)).

**Media.** No Puerto Rican news outlet based in Minnesota was found. The 2022 StoryMap lists Facebook groups such as Puertorriqueños en Minnesota and Boricuas en Minnesota as everyday connectors ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). Spanish-language outlets such as La Prensa de Minnesota serve Latinos broadly.

## 9. Civic and political life

**Representation beyond the numbers.** MNopedia notes that, given their small numbers, Puerto Rican Minnesotans have been "particularly adept" at winning office at the legislative, county and local levels ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesotanos-latino-journeys-minnesota)).

- **Carlos Mariani** (DFL, St. Paul) served in the Minnesota House from 1991 to 2022 in District 65B, with long service on education committees. The Legislative Reference Library records his Puerto Rican ancestry ([LRL](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/legdb/fulldetail?ID=10399)).
- **Melisa López Franzen** (DFL, Edina) served in the Senate from 2013 to 2022 and became the first Latina and first person of color to lead a legislative caucus in Minnesota ([LRL](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/legdb/fulldetail?ID=15379)). She entered the 2026 U.S. Senate race in March 2025 and left it in May 2025 ([Inforum/MPR, May 18, 2025](https://www.inforum.com/news/minnesota/former-state-legislator-lopez-franzen-ends-campaign-for-u-s-senate)).
- **María Isa Pérez-Vega** (DFL, St. Paul) won Mariani's former seat in 2022 with 78 percent of the vote ([Sahan Journal, Nov. 8, 2022](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/maria-isa-wins-minnesota-house-west-side-st-paul-elections-2022/)). The LRL records her as the first openly LGBTQ Latina in the Legislature ([LRL](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/legdb/fulldetail?id=15574)). In March 2026 she wrote that, "as a proud Boricua," she had welcomed former Illinois Congressman Luis Gutiérrez to the Capitol to discuss how Minnesota could support the Puerto Rican diaspora, and that she stood with efforts to dismantle PROMESA, the federal fiscal oversight board for Puerto Rico ([Minnesota House newsletter, March 9, 2026](https://www.house.mn.gov/members/profile/news/15574/41317)).
- **Rafael Ortega** has represented District 5 on the Ramsey County Board since his 1995 election and announced in December 2025 that he would retire ([Community Reporter, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://communityreporter.org/noecker-perez-vega-announce-runs-for-ramsey-county-board-seat-long-held-by-rafael-ortega/)). He is among the Puerto Rican narrators in MNHS's Latino Líderes oral histories ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)).
- **Simon Trautmann**, a Richfield City Council member of Puerto Rican descent ([Sahan Journal, 2023](https://sahanjournal.com/climate-environment/copal-climate-trip-puerto-rico/)), and **Alberto Monserrate**, the first Latino on the Minneapolis school board ([Twin Cities Business](https://tcbmag.com/tcb-100-people/alberto-monserrate/)), add to the local record.

**Organizations.**

- **Puerto Ricans in Minnesota Committee (PRIM)**, organized in 2013 to educate about Puerto Rico's political status. The StoryMap says its members hold varying views on status ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). The Current named María Isa among its founders ([The Current, 2017](https://www.thecurrent.org/feature/2017/10/04/minnesota-musicians-come-together-to-help-puerto-rico)).
- **Coalición de Boricuas en Minnesota**, formed in fall 2017 by Puerto Rican professionals for hurricane relief and resettlement help ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015); [FOX 9, 2017](https://www.fox9.com/news/minnesota-helps-puerto-ricans-relocate-after-disaster)). A related Coalition of Puerto Ricans in Minnesota packed supplies at El Colegio in October 2017 ([WCCO, Oct. 1, 2017](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/minnesota-coalition-puerto-rico-hurricane-victims/)).
- **El Fondo Boricua**, which the StoryMap says Elsa Pérez Vega founded in 2005, ran hurricane funds through the Saint Paul Foundation in 2017 and again after Fiona in 2022 ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015); [MPR News, 2017](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2017/09/22/we-cannot-communicate-with-our-families-puerto-ricans-in-minnesota-push-for-aid); [Echo Press/MPR, 2022](https://www.echopress.com/news/minnesota/minnesotans-with-puerto-rico-ties-remember-maria-work-to-help-recovery-from-fiona)).
- **Minnesota Alliance of Communities for Puerto Rico**, which organized a civilian airdrop of Minnesota supplies to San Juan in October 2017 ([MPR News, Oct. 13, 2017](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2017/10/13/counter-stories-puerto-rico)).
- **BORIKEN Cultural Center** (sections 6 and 8), whose executive director, Marisol Chiclana-Ayala, says it grew from welcoming families who arrived after Irma and María ([AMPERS, 2025](https://ampers.org/la-parranda-boricua-brings-a-puerto-rican-christmas-to-minnesota/)).
- An earlier **Puerto Rican Civic and Social Association**, remembered by Carmen Robles as a handful of families and Macalester students, is now defunct ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)).

**Ties with the wider Latino movement.** COPAL, a Latino organizing group, led an August 2023 delegation to Puerto Rico that included Pérez-Vega, Rep. Samakab Hussein and Sen. Bobby Joe Champion ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 20, 2023](https://sahanjournal.com/climate-environment/copal-climate-trip-puerto-rico/)). López Franzen serves on the board of CLUES ([Twin Cities Business, 2024](https://tcbmag.com/notable/melisa-lopez-franzen/)).

**Debates.** Status (statehood, independence or the current commonwealth) is argued within the community; PRIM's members disagree among themselves ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). Pew notes that the most recent status vote, in 2024, was nonbinding ([Pew, 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/02/05/key-findings-about-puerto-rico/)). In 2017 Mariani argued that donations were not enough and that federal action on the island's debt crisis was needed ([MPR News, 2017](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2017/09/22/we-cannot-communicate-with-our-families-puerto-ricans-in-minnesota-push-for-aid)). On a 2023 trip, Trautmann said he saw deep political cynicism on the island alongside strong grassroots work ([Sahan Journal, 2023](https://sahanjournal.com/climate-environment/copal-climate-trip-puerto-rico/)). Displaying the flag has been contested: in January 2024 the Worthington school board ordered teacher José Morales Collazo to take down both a Pride flag and a Puerto Rican flag. The board said it objected to any flags other than those of the United States and Minnesota ([Sahan Journal, March 6, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/education/minnesota-bill-leigh-finke-school-districts-lgbtq-pride-flag/)).

**Voting.** Island-born Puerto Ricans who move here can vote for president for the first time. An adult education teacher in Roseau helped newcomers fill out Minnesota voter registration forms ([Star Tribune via Roseau Civic and Commerce](https://goroseau.com/star-tribune-puerto-rican-workers-bring-diversity-to-roseau/)). No Minnesota turnout data for Puerto Ricans was found.

## 10. Education

**Attainment.** Among Puerto Rican Minnesotans 25 and older, 91.9 percent finished high school and 41.4 percent hold a bachelor's degree or more ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/puerto-rican)). That is well above the national Puerto Rican figure of 24 percent with a bachelor's degree in 2021 ([Pew fact sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/race-and-ethnicity/fact-sheet/us-hispanics-facts-on-puerto-rican-origin-latinos/)). The two sources use different years, and no source explains the gap. One possible factor is that college and professional jobs drew many people to Minnesota. Another is that the state receives fewer people from the lower-income stream that first went to New York, Florida or Connecticut. Neither has been established.

**Colleges.** Macalester College recruited Latino students, Mariani among them, in the 1970s ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). At the University of Minnesota, Dr. Miguel Fiol is a professor of neurology, and López Franzen joined the university's cabinet ([UMN Medical School](https://med.umn.edu/bio/miguel-fiol); [Twin Cities Business, 2024](https://tcbmag.com/notable/melisa-lopez-franzen/)). Tabatha Cruz wrote "Becoming SotaRicans" as a University of Minnesota doctoral student ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). Monserrate studied at the university after arriving from Puerto Rico, as he recalled in his oral history (quoted there).

**Schools.** El Colegio High School in Minneapolis has taught students in English and Spanish since 2000 ([El Colegio, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.el-colegio.org/)) and carries the Puerto Rican mural described in section 8. BORIKEN runs youth programs at Academia César Chávez in St. Paul ([AMPERS, 2025](https://ampers.org/la-parranda-boricua-brings-a-puerto-rican-christmas-to-minnesota/)). Pérez-Vega wrote in 2026 that she worked on a bipartisan bill for the state's first dual-immersion task force ([House newsletter, March 2026](https://www.house.mn.gov/members/profile/news/15574/41317)). In Roseau, an adult basic education teacher taught English to Puerto Rican families ([Star Tribune via Roseau Civic and Commerce](https://goroseau.com/star-tribune-puerto-rican-workers-bring-diversity-to-roseau/)).

**Not found.** No Minnesota data was found on Puerto Rican students' test scores, graduation rates, English learner status or special education. No current information was found on a Puerto Rican student group at a Minnesota college; a 2017 Minnesota Daily story on University of Minnesota students' relief work could not be opened.

## 11. Health and wellbeing, broadly

**Coverage.** Compass reports that 5.7 percent of Puerto Rican Minnesotans lack health insurance, give or take 1.9 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/puerto-rican)).

**Data gaps.** Minnesota's 2011 report on state health data shows that the Hispanic ethnicity question in some state forms included a "Puerto Rican" box ([Minnesota Department of Health, 2011](https://www.health.mn.gov/communities/equity/reports/racialethnicdata2011.pdf)). No published Minnesota health measure for Puerto Rican residents, such as asthma, diabetes, maternal health or mental health, was found. National studies exist, but they are not Minnesota evidence and are not repeated here.

**Moving and benefits.** In 2019, families arriving after the hurricane faced Minnesota's 30-day residency rule for public assistance. State Sen. López Franzen, born in Puerto Rico, was asked to seek a waiver; the Department of Human Services allowed hardship exceptions only with a written request ([MPR News/Sahan Journal, Aug. 21, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/08/21/in-hurricanes-wake-puerto-ricans-find-safe-harbor-in-minnesota)). The same 2019 report described FEMA helping pay for a hotel for a newly arrived father who had been in a hospital bed when the storm hit.

**Clinicians and healers.** Dr. Miguel Fiol, a University of Minnesota neurologist trained in Puerto Rico, studies epilepsy and lists Latino neurology among his interests. His university profile says he returned to the island after María to give medical care, especially to children ([UMN Medical School](https://med.umn.edu/bio/miguel-fiol)). Tearra Oso uses bomba drumming for healing and has presented it at an adolescent mental health institute with the Black Youth Healing Arts Center ([MNHS transcript](https://www.mnhs.org/hubfs/MNHS-Minnesota-Unraveled-EP201-Transcript.pdf)). Rep. Pérez-Vega has said publicly that she has type 1 diabetes and helped pass Minnesota's insulin affordability law ([Sahan Journal, 2022](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/maria-isa-wins-minnesota-house-west-side-st-paul-elections-2022/); [Community Reporter, 2026](https://communityreporter.org/2026-election-guide-ramsey-county-commissioner-district-5-maria-isa-perez-vega/)).

**Belonging as wellbeing.** The StoryMap argues that groups like BORIKEN and PRIM support wellbeing and mental health by giving people a place to belong ([Cruz, 2022](https://storymaps.arcgis.com/stories/5dadf38cccd64776a9da479542b30015)). Oso recalls that bomba classes in the 1980s and 1990s gave her mentors to confide in while drugs and violence harmed her neighborhood ([MNHS transcript](https://www.mnhs.org/hubfs/MNHS-Minnesota-Unraveled-EP201-Transcript.pdf)).

## 12. Disability

**The Minnesota number.** Compass reports that 13.3 percent of Puerto Rican Minnesotans have a disability, give or take 3.4 points, out of 22,764 people for whom the survey determined disability status. Among those under 65, the share is 12.5 percent, give or take 3.4 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/puerto-rican)). In the same Compass series for 2020 to 2024, the figures are 8.1 percent for Mexican Minnesotans, 5.4 percent for Guatemalan and 3.3 percent for Salvadoran ([Compass Mexican](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/mexican); [Guatemalan](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/guatemalan); [Salvadoran](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/salvadoran)).

**Questions this raises, not answers.**

- The Puerto Rican population is young, with fewer than 5 percent 65 or older, yet its disability rate is higher than its Latino neighbors'. Age does not seem to explain the difference. What does?
- Can people move freely as citizens, and do they come for care? NBC News reported in 2018 that a Cornell University estimate put disability among Puerto Rico's population above 21 percent, higher than any state, and described a mother on the island sending her deaf-blind son to the mainland for care after María ([NBC News, 2018](https://www.nbcnews.com/storyline/puerto-rico-crisis/hurricane-maria-exposed-big-gaps-help-puerto-rico-s-disabled-n903921)). In Minnesota, FOX 9 described a family bringing a brother who uses oxygen north from Aibonito in 2017 ([FOX 9, Oct. 20, 2017](https://www.fox9.com/news/heartfelt-reunion-minnesota-family-welcomes-puerto-rican-loved-ones-thanks-to-community-effort)). Whether moving for care shapes Minnesota's numbers has not been studied.
- Citizens can move without a visa, and no immigration screening affects who arrives. Does that make a difference compared with immigrant groups? This, too, is a question.
- The survey asks about functional difficulties. It does not record diagnoses, and a small sample gives a wide margin. The true rate could be closer to 10 percent or above 16 percent.

**Who defines disability, and what a label does.** No Minnesota source was found on how Puerto Rican families here understand disability, special education labels or benefits. Two Minnesota efforts reach Latino families broadly. In 2018 DHS and the University of Minnesota's Institute on Community Integration made a Spanish-language film on autism services for the state's Latino community ([ICI, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://ici.umn.edu/products/oSoIi1YoR92u22wV3xMs-A)). In 2024 PACER Center and Family Voices of Minnesota began DHS-funded Spanish-language support groups for families of children with autism and other disabilities ([PACER Center, 2024](https://www.pacer.org/press-release/new-pacer-center-project-will-support-latino-and-somali-families-with-children-on-the-autism-spectrum/)). Because 62 percent of Puerto Rican Minnesotans speak only English at home, Spanish-only outreach may miss many families, and English-only outreach may miss others. No source has tested this.

**What produced an impairment.** No Minnesota source ties disability among Puerto Rican residents to a cause. Hurricane María's aftermath, including long power outages that endangered people who rely on electrically powered equipment, is documented on the island ([NBC News, 2018](https://www.nbcnews.com/storyline/puerto-rico-crisis/hurricane-maria-exposed-big-gaps-help-puerto-rico-s-disabled-n903921)), but its effect on people who later moved to Minnesota is unknown.

**People and organizations.** No Minnesota organization serving disabled Puerto Rican residents in particular was found. Rep. Pérez-Vega, who has spoken of needing county help as a person with type 1 diabetes, pledged in 2026 to include disabled people in Ramsey County decisions ([Community Reporter, 2026](https://communityreporter.org/2026-election-guide-ramsey-county-commissioner-district-5-maria-isa-perez-vega/)).

## 13. What is changing now

- **A county seat changes hands (November 2026).** Rafael Ortega is retiring after 31 years on the Ramsey County Board. Pérez-Vega, who gave her House retirement speech on May 18, 2026, is running for his District 5 seat against St. Paul City Council President Rebecca Noecker, with the general election on November 3 ([Community Reporter](https://communityreporter.org/noecker-perez-vega-announce-runs-for-ramsey-county-board-seat-long-held-by-rafael-ortega/); [Community Reporter election guide](https://communityreporter.org/2026-election-guide-ramsey-county-commissioner-district-5-maria-isa-perez-vega/); [LRL](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/legdb/fulldetail?id=15574)). Either way, she is leaving House District 65B, a St. Paul seat held by Puerto Rican legislators since 1991, first Mariani and then Pérez-Vega.
- **Citizens and the January 2026 enforcement surge.** During the federal immigration operation in the Twin Cities in early 2026, WCCO reported U.S. citizens of Mexican heritage being stopped and detained (Jan. 13, 2026), and CBS News reported citizens carrying passports for fear of being mistaken for immigrants (Jan. 29, 2026) ([WCCO](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/us-citizens-arrested-by-ice-minneapolis/); [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/americans-carrying-passports-minneapolis-minnesota/)). No report was found naming a Puerto Rican Minnesotan stopped in that operation. Being taken for a non-citizen is an old experience, though: the Eagan couple in 2019 was told a Puerto Rican ID did not count as American ([MPR/Sahan, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/08/21/in-hurricanes-wake-puerto-ricans-find-safe-harbor-in-minnesota)). Pérez-Vega has spoken out against ICE actions in Minnesota ([Community Reporter, 2026](https://communityreporter.org/2026-election-guide-ramsey-county-commissioner-district-5-maria-isa-perez-vega/)), and Levins Morales's studio sells a 2026 zine of Twin Cities movement elders reflecting on what it calls the "2026 ICE Occupation" ([RLM Art Studio](https://www.rlmartstudio.com/)).
- **The island keeps emptying.** Puerto Rico's population fell 17 percent from its 2004 peak to about 3.2 million in 2025. About 38,000 people moved from the island to the mainland in 2024 ([Pew, Feb. 5, 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/02/05/key-findings-about-puerto-rico/)). Minnesota's count keeps rising (section 1).
- **New public culture.** Between October 2025 and January 2026, MNHS released a podcast episode and hosted a sold-out premiere centered on Puerto Rican Minnesotans ([MNHS](https://www.mnhs.org/unraveled/episode-201); [MNHS event](https://www.mnhs.org/events/36171090195)). The Arts Board funded the 2026 Parranda Boricua ([Arts Board](https://www.arts.state.mn.us/grants/2026/2026-awarded-ce.htm)).
- **Status politics.** In 2026 Pérez-Vega joined efforts against PROMESA ([House newsletter](https://www.house.mn.gov/members/profile/news/15574/41317)). Puerto Rico's 2024 status vote was nonbinding ([Pew](https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2026/02/05/key-findings-about-puerto-rico/)).
- **Generations.** The second generation is visible in leaders like Pérez-Vega and Oso, both raised in St. Paul. Interviewees in Pedacito de Tierra described having to choose, intentionally, to stay connected to the island ([MNHS transcript](https://www.mnhs.org/hubfs/MNHS-Minnesota-Unraveled-EP201-Transcript.pdf)).

## 14. Strengths and contributions

- **Civic leadership.** For three decades, a small community has held state House, state Senate, county board, city council and school board seats, including the first Latina caucus leader (sections 9 and 13).
- **Mutual aid at scale.** In 2017 Puerto Rican Minnesotans created coalitions, funds, benefit concerts, a civilian airdrop, and airport welcomes with winter coats for strangers (sections 1 and 9).
- **Keeping Afro-Puerto Rican culture alive.** Bomba and plena have been taught in St. Paul since at least the 1990s, by El Arco Iris, Proyecto La Plena, BORIKEN and Tearra Oso, and are shared with people of many backgrounds (section 8).
- **Education and work.** High rates of college completion and employment (sections 2 and 10).
- **Rural renewal.** Workers recruited from the island helped staff a major employer in a shrinking northwestern county, and town leaders called it a win for everyone ([Star Tribune via Roseau Civic and Commerce](https://goroseau.com/star-tribune-puerto-rican-workers-bring-diversity-to-roseau/)).
- **Art for movements.** Levins Morales's four decades of movement art earned McKnight's highest artist honor, and Zayas Cabán pairs albums about Puerto Rico and the United States with reparative justice campaigns on Twin Cities streets ([McKnight](https://www.mcknight.org/news-ideas/mcknight-names-ricardo-levins-morales-2024-distinguished-artist/); [Mapping Prejudice, University of Minnesota](https://mappingprejudice.umn.edu/jose-antonio-zayas-caban); [Our Streets](https://www.ourstreetsmn.org/about/staff/jose-antonio-zayas-caban/)).

## What could not be established

- **Births on the island.** The share of Puerto Rican Minnesotans born in Puerto Rico, on the mainland or in Minnesota. Compass's "foreign-born" figure does not answer this.
- **Arrivals after María.** A reliable count. Local estimates (50 to more than 300 families) and federal figures (85 movers, 78 FEMA placements) disagree, and no source reconciles them.
- **Work.** Occupations and industries for Puerto Rican Minnesotans, and the employers in Long Prairie and Alexandria named in 2019.
- **Businesses.** Current operation of La Borinqua, El Jibarito, Sosa Foods & Deli and SotaRico. No Puerto Rican bakery, grocery, law, accounting, insurance, tax or travel business, and no Puerto Rican chamber, was found in Minnesota sources.
- **Religion.** Any named Puerto Rican congregation in Minnesota, the faith makeup of the community, and Minnesota evidence on Espiritismo, Santería or Pentecostal churches among Puerto Ricans.
- **Health.** Any Minnesota health statistic for Puerto Rican residents beyond insurance coverage and disability.
- **Disability.** Why the disability rate is higher than among other Latino groups; any Minnesota organization or study focused on disabled Puerto Rican residents.
- **Events and dates.** Dates for the 2026 Parranda Boricua and for any 2025 or 2026 Encuentro Boricua; any Minnesota celebration of Constitution Day, Abolition Day, Día de la Puertorriqueñidad or Noche de San Juan; any 2025 or 2026 María vigil.
- **Families.** Marriage, childhood, aging and dress customs among Puerto Rican Minnesotans.
- **Sites that could not be reached.** The BORIKEN Cultural Center's own website (borikenmn.org) did not respond. Minnesota state agency pages on mn.gov, including the Council on Latino Affairs page for Puerto Rican families who relocated after María, blocked access. Also unavailable: a 2017 MinnPost piece on PRIM bringing Oscar López Rivera to the Twin Cities, a Sahan Journal story on Pedacito de Tierra, KARE 11 coverage of the 2022 vigil and post-María relief, Minnesota Daily coverage of University of Minnesota Puerto Rican students, and Minnesota Legacy grant records.
