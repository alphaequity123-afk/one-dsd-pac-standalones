# Russian-speaking Minnesotans
Checked: October 31, 2026

This page is about people who share a language, not one nationality. Russian was the common language of the Russian Empire and the Soviet Union, so people who speak it at home in Minnesota trace their roots to Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, Moldova, Kazakhstan, Uzbekistan and other Central Asian states, the Caucasus and the Baltic states. They include Soviet Jewish refugees and their children, Orthodox Christians, evangelical Protestants, Muslims from Central Asia, and many people raised without religion. A counselor at a Minnesota Russian-language camp, who grew up in Belarus and married a Jewish man from Lithuania, put it this way in 2022: their family has "not a drop of Russian blood, but it's our common language" ([Christian Science Monitor, Nov. 21, 2022, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.csmonitor.com/Arts-Culture/2022/1121/How-a-US-camp-helps-Russian-speaking-kids-in-time-of-war)). Ukrainian Minnesotans, including those who speak Russian, have their own page; this one points to them only where the two histories meet.

## 1. People and place

**How many people speak Russian at home.** No single count describes this community, and the counts disagree because they measure different things.

- *Language at home.* The American Community Survey for 2020 to 2024 counts 13,851 Minnesotans age 5 and older who speak Russian at home, give or take 1,218. Of these, 8,692 speak English "very well" and 5,159 speak it less than "very well." The same table counts 5,249 more who speak "Ukrainian or other Slavic languages," a separate line ([ACS 2020-2024 5-year, table B16001, via Census Reporter, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B16001&geo_ids=04000US27)). The single-year 2024 survey gives a higher figure, 15,466 Russian speakers, but its margin is wide at about 3,448 ([ACS 2024 1-year, table B16001, via Census Reporter](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B16001&geo_ids=04000US27)). Hennepin County alone had about 5,032 Russian speakers in 2024, with a margin of about 2,238 ([ACS 2024 1-year, Hennepin County, via Census Reporter](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_1yr?table_ids=B16001&geo_ids=05000US27053)). Smaller counties and cities are not published in that table for a single year.
- *Place of birth.* In the 2020 to 2024 survey, Minnesota had about 6,301 residents born in Russia (give or take 665), 5,269 born in Ukraine (720), 1,755 born in Belarus (378), 1,633 born in Moldova (402), 431 born in Uzbekistan (291), 397 born in Kazakhstan (142), 294 born in Azerbaijan (144), 135 born in Georgia (83), 370 born in Latvia and 222 born in Lithuania ([ACS 2020-2024, table B05006, via Census Reporter](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B05006,B04006&geo_ids=04000US27)). The 2024 single-year figures show more people born in Ukraine (6,647, give or take 2,021) and Belarus (3,272, give or take 1,437), which fits recent arrivals but carries wide margins ([ACS 2024 1-year, table B05006, via Census Reporter](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B05006&geo_ids=04000US27)). Minnesota Compass lists 6,483 "Russian immigrants" and 5,782 "Ukrainian immigrants" on its immigrant-group page, which does not show the survey years beside the figures ([Minnesota Compass, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/immigration)).
- *Ancestry.* About 31,548 Minnesotans report Russian ancestry (give or take 1,795), and 17,897 report Ukrainian ancestry ([ACS 2020-2024, table B04006, via Census Reporter](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B05006,B04006&geo_ids=04000US27)). Many people with Russian ancestry are descendants of much older migrations and do not speak Russian.
- *Community estimates.* People inside the community give much larger numbers for everyone with roots in the former Soviet Union. Community leaders told MinnPost in 2016 that there were about 60,000 Russian speakers, against a State Demographic Center estimate of 43,000 people of Russian ancestry ([MinnPost, June 21, 2016, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)). The director of Russian-American Jews in Minnesota cited a study of about 45,000 Minnesotans from the former Soviet Union in 2017 ([TC Jewfolk, Jan. 12, 2017](https://tcjewfolk.com/2017/01/12/energizing-connecting-tcs-russianjews/)). The publisher of the Plymouth Russian-language paper cited 56,000 immigrants from the region in the Twin Cities in 2023 ([CCX Media, Feb. 2023](https://ccxmedia.org/news/plymouth-newspaper-keeps-russian-speaking-immigrants-informed-on-ukraine/)), and the same company's website says about 52,000 Russian-speaking people live in Minnesota ([Zerkalo, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.zerkalomn.com/)). None of these estimates says how it was made. The larger numbers seem to count everyone with roots in the former Soviet Union, including people who may not speak Russian at home, but the sources do not say so.

**Where people live.** There is no single Russian-speaking neighborhood. A guide written by Macalester College's Russian Studies program says there is "no single neighborhood" where Russian speakers live or own businesses ([Macalester College Russian Studies, Twin Cities Guide for Russian Speakers, updated May 31, 2026, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.arcgis.com/sharing/rest/content/items/74be859446ff445bae1afeebcf66a5b7/data?f=json)). After 1991, many new arrivals first lived in St. Louis Park; by the end of the decade families spread to Minneapolis, Plymouth, Golden Valley, Hopkins, Anoka and Blaine ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)). The St. Louis Park Historical Society notes a Russian community around the Aquila area in the early 1990s, with a Russian grocery and a Russian doctor ([St. Louis Park Historical Society, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://slphistory.org/jewishmigration/)). Culture Care Connection adds the Highland Park area of St. Paul as an early place of settlement for Jewish refugees ([Culture Care Connection, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/russian/)). Today the businesses, churches and schools named in this profile sit in Plymouth, Anoka, Eagan, Brooklyn Park, Fridley, Savage, Burnsville, Eden Prairie, Edina, Shakopee and Minneapolis (sections 4, 5 and 10). Students at the one Russian-focused public school come from at least 16 cities, from St. Louis Park to Elk River and Otsego ([Nasha Shkola Charter School, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://nashashkolamn.org/)). The Ukrainian cultural community, which overlaps with this one, is 71.9 percent in the Twin Cities and 28.1 percent in Greater Minnesota, according to Compass ([Minnesota Compass, Ukrainian population, 2020-2024](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ukrainian)). No comparable Greater Minnesota figure exists for Russian speakers.

**How people came.** Russian speakers arrived in layers.

- *Before 1917.* Carpatho-Rusyn immigrants from villages north of Prešov, in today's eastern Slovakia, founded St. Mary's in northeast Minneapolis in 1887. They joined the Russian Orthodox Church in 1891, and the first Russian Orthodox seminary in America opened at St. Mary's in 1905 ([St. Mary's Orthodox Cathedral, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://stmarysoca.org/about.html)). Many Jews from the Russian Empire also came in this era; about 4,500 "mostly Russian, Lithuanian, and Polish Jews" had settled in North Minneapolis by one historical count ([St. Louis Park Historical Society](https://slphistory.org/jewishmigration/)). Their descendants are mostly English speakers today.
- *After World War II.* St. Panteleimon Russian Orthodox Church in Minneapolis was founded in 1956 and marked its 70th anniversary in August 2026 ([St. Panteleimon Church, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://spro.church/celebration-of-patronal-feast-and-70th-anniversary-of-st-panteleimon-parish/)). The Macalester guide describes it as a postwar church "visited by so many immigrants" ([Macalester College Russian Studies guide](https://www.arcgis.com/sharing/rest/content/items/74be859446ff445bae1afeebcf66a5b7/data?f=json)).
- *Soviet Jewish emigration, 1970s to 1990s.* MNopedia records a wave of Soviet Jewish settlers "permitted to emigrate after years of refusal" in the 1970s, with resettlement programs continuing through the 1980s and 1990s ([MNopedia, Jewish Social Welfare Groups, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/jewish-social-welfare-groups-1871-2012)). Jewish Family and Children's Service of Minneapolis and Jewish Family Service of St. Paul resettled "well over 1,000 families" from the late 1980s through the 1990s, by the agency's own count ([JFCS, February 2017](https://jfcsmpls.org/desk-ceo-judy-halper-february-2017/)). A 1980 St. Louis Park High School paper described students who had waited months to leave the Soviet Union ([St. Louis Park Historical Society](https://slphistory.org/jewishmigration/)). Linguist Anatoly Liberman, who left the Soviet Union in the 1970s and joined the University of Minnesota in 1975, told MinnPost the earlier groups were largely Jews escaping discrimination ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)).
- *After 1991.* Macalester's Russian Studies program, citing a survey of Hispanic, Hmong, Russian and Somali immigrants in Minneapolis-Saint Paul, says 84 percent of Russian immigrants in Minnesota arrived in the 1990s, 9 percent in the 1980s and 7 percent in the 1970s, and that people from Belarus, Ukraine and other former republics also came after the fall of communism ([Macalester College Russian Studies, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.macalester.edu/russian-studies/about/inminnesota/)). Another group came in the 2000s to join family or to work for the state's medical and multinational companies ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)). Evangelical Christians also came; a St. Louis Park church of about 115 members in the 1990s was about 90 percent Ukrainian and 10 percent Russian, with Moldovan members ([St. Louis Park Historical Society, First Ukrainian Evangelical Baptist Church](https://slphistory.org/firstukrainian/)).
- *After 2022.* The full-scale invasion of Ukraine brought people from both Ukraine and Russia ([Macalester College Russian Studies](https://www.macalester.edu/russian-studies/about/inminnesota/)). Ukrainians came through a sponsorship program. Russians who opposed the war or fled the draft mostly came through the southern border to seek asylum; one longtime Russian American organizer estimated at least 50 new Russian asylum seekers in the Twin Cities in early 2023 ([Star Tribune via Stars and Stripes, April 12, 2023, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.stripes.com/theaters/us/2023-04-12/antiwar-russians-flee-homeland-resettle-minnesota-9781406.html)).

**Generations and languages.** Many households are bilingual or multilingual. Culture Care Connection notes that people may also speak Belarusian, Ukrainian, Uzbek or another language of their home republic, and that Yiddish is now mostly limited to the oldest Jewish generation ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/russian/)). The publisher of Zerkalo said in 2016 that young people "can speak Russian" but are "completely absorbed into American culture" ([Twin Cities Business, 2016, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://tcbmag.com/mirror-on-the-midwest-russian-language-zerkalo-celebrates-25-years-in-minnesota/)). The director of the Russian Educational Center told a local paper in 2021 that many Russian Americans marry people of other backgrounds ([Eden Prairie Local News, Aug. 31, 2021, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://eplocalnews.org/2021/08/31/states-quality-of-life-schools-draw-russians/)). About 37 percent of Russian speakers counted in 2020 to 2024 said they speak English less than "very well" ([ACS 2020-2024, B16001](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B16001&geo_ids=04000US27)).

## 2. All walks of life

There is no public Minnesota table of income, poverty or home ownership for people who speak Russian at home or for people born in the former Soviet Union. What exists is older and partial, and it shows both comfort and hardship.

- **Well-off and professional households.** MinnPost described the Russian-speaking community in 2016 as one of the most affluent immigrant communities in Minnesota, with many families in west-metro suburbs and engineering and technology jobs at companies such as Medtronic, Boston Scientific and St. Jude Medical ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)). Culture Care Connection, citing 2018 figures, reports median earnings of $54,700 for Russian Minnesotans, second-highest among the population groups it compared, and says 98 percent had at least a high school diploma ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/russian/)). Publicly visible careers include physicist Mikhail Shifman, who earned his doctorate in Moscow and holds the Ida Cohen Fine Chair at the University of Minnesota ([University of Minnesota, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://cse.umn.edu/physics/mikhail-shifman)), and linguist Anatoly Liberman, a professor at the university since the 1970s ([University of Minnesota College of Liberal Arts, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://cla.umn.edu/about/directory/profile/aliber); [SlavFile, 2021](https://www.ata-divisions.org/SLD/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/SlavFile-2021-3-Summer-Fall.pdf)).
- **Business owners.** The Liberman family has run Moscow on the Hill in St. Paul since the 1990s; Eugene Liberman said in 2022 that the family had been there 28 years ([KSTP, Feb. 28, 2022, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/russian-institutions-in-minnesota-show-support-for-ukraine/)). Leonid Grichener arrived from Moldova in 1990 with $100 and built a publishing company ([Twin Cities Business, 2016](https://tcbmag.com/mirror-on-the-midwest-russian-language-zerkalo-celebrates-25-years-in-minnesota/)). Gene and Elena Bersten own Dance With Us America in Edina, home studio of Alan Bersten of *Dancing with the Stars* ([Dance With Us America, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.dancewithusamerica.com/)). More owners appear in section 4.
- **Middle-income and working families.** The 2016 MinnPost report quoted Igor Fridman describing how families left St. Louis Park as people "got jobs" and "moved to the suburbs and bought houses" ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)). Culture Care Connection says later arrivals have often worked in manufacturing, trade and services, and that many professionals ran into licensing barriers ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/russian/)). A Russian-speaking couple who arrived after 2022 both found work at a Minnesota nursing home, NPR reported in 2026 ([NPR, Feb. 26, 2026, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.npr.org/2026/02/26/nx-s1-5684806/as-trump-dismantles-asylum-programs-russians-and-ukrainians-fear-for-future)).
- **People with low incomes.** The same Culture Care figures put 9 percent below the poverty line in 2018, compared with 11 percent of all Minnesotans ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/russian/)). In 2016, most residents of Booth Manor, an affordable senior apartment building near Loring Park in Minneapolis, were immigrants from the former Soviet Union ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)). Federal school data cited by GreatSchools counted 79 percent of students at Nasha Shkola, the Russian-focused charter school, as coming from low-income families, though that figure is from 2019 ([GreatSchools, citing NCES 2019, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.greatschools.org/minnesota/brooklyn-park/nasha-shkola-charter-school/)). Asylum seekers who arrived after 2022 described selling homes below value and starting over with help from strangers found on social media ([Stars and Stripes, 2023](https://www.stripes.com/theaters/us/2023-04-12/antiwar-russians-flee-homeland-resettle-minnesota-9781406.html)).

**Older adults.** Culture Care Connection reports that Russian immigrants in Minnesota are generally older than other major immigrant groups, with 83 percent age 50 or older, a figure it does not date ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/russian/)). Igor Fridman told MinnPost that people who came as adults in the 1990s and 2000s are often culturally isolated "even if they're economically stable" ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)).

## 3. Work and the economy

Many people who came in the 1970s through the 1990s had university training. Liberman told MinnPost, "I don't think there was anyone without any formal education," speaking of the people he knew ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)). Culture Care Connection lists physicians, engineers and teachers among common professions, along with difficulty getting foreign credentials recognized ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/russian/)). The director of the Russian Educational Center said in 2021 that professionals are drawn by high-tech industries ([Eden Prairie Local News, 2021](https://eplocalnews.org/2021/08/31/states-quality-of-life-schools-draw-russians/)). The director of Russian-American Jews in Minnesota said in 2017 that there was "nothing special" about Minnesota beyond jobs and family reunification ([TC Jewfolk, 2017](https://tcjewfolk.com/2017/01/12/energizing-connecting-tcs-russianjews/)).

Work in care and services is visible too. A personal care assistant at Booth Manor, born in Ukraine and raised in Russia, was profiled in 2016 ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)); the post-2022 couple in Eagan worked at a nursing home ([NPR, 2026](https://www.npr.org/2026/02/26/nx-s1-5684806/as-trump-dismantles-asylum-programs-russians-and-ukrainians-fear-for-future)); and a state aging directory lists a Savage adult day program for Russian-speaking clients (section 12). Entrepreneurship shows up in groceries, restaurants, a publishing company, dance and language schools, a child care center and a translation bureau (section 4). The American Russian Center's leader describes owning a translation bureau, Prime Language Services, since 2008 ([American Russian Center, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://americanrussiancenter.org/)). No Minnesota source was found on union membership or labor history specific to Russian speakers.

## 4. Businesses and commercial life

Russian-speaking commerce is spread across the suburbs rather than in one district. The Zerkalo company has published a Russian Yellow Pages of Minnesota since 1994, according to the Macalester guide ([Macalester College Russian Studies guide](https://www.arcgis.com/sharing/rest/content/items/74be859446ff445bae1afeebcf66a5b7/data?f=json)), and still advertises it ([Zerkalo](https://www.zerkalomn.com/)). MinnPost reported in 2016 that the community had restaurants, pharmacies and grocery shops across Minnesota ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)). The full list is in businesses.json. Highlights:

- **Moscow on the Hill**, 371 Selby Ave., St. Paul. A Russian restaurant on Cathedral Hill run by the Liberman family, who bought a French restaurant and switched to their native cuisine ([Macalester guide](https://www.arcgis.com/sharing/rest/content/items/74be859446ff445bae1afeebcf66a5b7/data?f=json)). Its site lists pelmeni, vareniki and house-infused vodkas ([Moscow on the Hill, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.moscowonthehill.com/)), and Visit Saint Paul lists current hours ([Visit Saint Paul, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.visitsaintpaul.com/directory/moscow-on-the-hill/)). In 2022 the owners reported receiving hate mail after the invasion of Ukraine, along with strong support from customers ([KSTP, 2022](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/russian-institutions-in-minnesota-show-support-for-ukraine/)).
- **Samarkand**, 16 Nathan Lane N., Plymouth. Described as Minnesota's only Uzbek restaurant, run by a family from Samarkand who moved from New York in 2019 and chose an area "with a large Russian community" ([CCX Media, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://ccxmedia.org/news/minnesotas-only-uzbek-restaurant-serves-up-central-asian-cuisine-in-plymouth/)). Macalester Russian classes ate there in April 2026 ([The Mac Weekly, April 2026](https://themacweekly.com/2026/04/a-glimpse-inside-minnesotas-only-uzbek-restaurant/)). The Macalester guide says the family has opened an adjoining Uzbek-Russian market. Sources name different family members as owners (Aziz Radjabov and his uncle in CCX Media; Djafar Murimov in the Macalester guide).
- **Tasteful Delights Cafe and Tea Room**, 3726 N. 7th Ave., Anoka. Russian, Ukrainian, Moldovan and American food, including Moldovan mamaliga, run by a mother and daughter of Russian and Ukrainian heritage ([Macalester guide](https://www.arcgis.com/sharing/rest/content/items/74be859446ff445bae1afeebcf66a5b7/data?f=json); [Tasteful Delights, 2026 site](https://www.tastefuldelightscafe.com/)).
- **Groceries.** Smak, 3710 N. 7th Ave. in Anoka, has operated for more than 25 years and was renamed from "Russian Supermarket" in 2017; the Macalester guide says its customers include Russian, Ukrainian, Moldovan and Bulgarian families ([Macalester guide](https://www.arcgis.com/sharing/rest/content/items/74be859446ff445bae1afeebcf66a5b7/data?f=json)). Minsk Market in Eagan, opened in 2000 by a woman from Minsk, was "recently sold to a new owner and closed for renovations" as of an Oct. 8, 2026 note in the same guide. Arkady's Market, 3435 Highway 169 N. (a Minneapolis 55441 address), is also listed there, with fresh-baked bread and Russian-speaking staff. Current operation of Smak and Arkady's could not be confirmed from a dated public page.
- **Media.** Zerkalo, Inc., in Plymouth, publishes the magazine *Zerkalo*, the newspaper *North Star* and the Yellow Pages; its site lists *North Star* issues from early 2026 ([Zerkalo](https://www.zerkalomn.com/); [Twin Cities Business, 2016](https://tcbmag.com/mirror-on-the-midwest-russian-language-zerkalo-celebrates-25-years-in-minnesota/)).
- **Children and families.** Sunflower Daycare in Burnsville is a bilingual child care center where many teachers are native Russian speakers ([Macalester guide](https://www.arcgis.com/sharing/rest/content/items/74be859446ff445bae1afeebcf66a5b7/data?f=json)). Dance With Us America in Edina runs free lessons once or twice a year for boys who have recently arrived and cannot afford training, according to the guide.

No Russian-speaking chamber of commerce, business association or shopping center was found. No clinic, law office, accounting firm or insurance agency serving Russian speakers could be confirmed from a current public page.

## 5. Religion and spiritual life

There is no single faith. Culture Care Connection says most Russian immigrants practice Judaism or Eastern Orthodox Christianity, with smaller numbers of Catholics and members of the Armenian church, and that many grew up as atheists under Soviet policy ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/russian/)). A Jewish community organizer wrote in 2010 that most Soviet Jewish arrivals grew up in "a secular, atheist society" ([TC Jewfolk, June 2, 2010](https://tcjewfolk.com/2010/06/02/voices-minnesotas-soviet-jews-inspiration-film/)). Evangelical Protestants and Central Asian Muslims are also part of the picture.

**Orthodox Christianity.** Orthodox parishes differ in language and in which church they belong to.
- *St. Panteleimon Russian Orthodox Church*, 2210 Franklin Ave. SE, Minneapolis, belongs to the Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia. It posts services in Russian and English, has a sisterhood and is raising money for a renovation ([St. Panteleimon Church](https://spro.church/); [East-West Connections](https://east-westconnections.com/russianlanguage-churches)). The Macalester guide says women are asked to wear dresses and head scarves and that the parish holds Maslenitsa events ([Macalester guide](https://www.arcgis.com/sharing/rest/content/items/74be859446ff445bae1afeebcf66a5b7/data?f=json)).
- *Russian Orthodox Church and Skete of the Resurrection of Christ*, 1201 Hathaway Lane NE, Fridley (mailing address Minneapolis), also Russian Orthodox Church Outside Russia. It serves in Church Slavonic and English with sermons in Russian and English, and posted services for Oct. 31 and Nov. 1, 2026 ([Resurrection Skete, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.resurrectionskete.org/); [ROCOR directory](https://directory.synod.com/listing/411/resurrection-of-christ-russian-orthodox-church/)). Its rector concelebrated at St. Panteleimon's anniversary in August 2026 ([St. Panteleimon Church](https://spro.church/celebration-of-patronal-feast-and-70th-anniversary-of-st-panteleimon-parish/)).
- *St. Mary's Orthodox Cathedral*, 1701 Fifth St. NE, Minneapolis, Orthodox Church in America. Founded by Carpatho-Rusyns, consecrated by St. Tikhon in 1906 ([St. Mary's](https://stmarysoca.org/about.html); [Orthodox Church in America](https://www.oca.org/parishes/oca-mw-minsmk)).
- *Holy Trinity Church*, St. Paul's East Side. Co-founded by working-class Belarusians and Serbs and known until 1990 as the "Russian-Serbian Orthodox Church"; its liturgical language is now English ([Orthodox Church in America](https://www.oca.org/parishes/oca-mw-stphtc)).
- *St. Herman's*, 5355 38th Ave. S., Minneapolis, the first Orthodox Church in America mission in Minnesota (1976), serves in English and says most parishioners are converts ([St. Herman's, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.sthermanmpls.org/)).

**Evangelical Protestants.** Slavic Baptist and Pentecostal-style churches are an important but poorly documented part of community life. Grace Slavic Church in Eagan, which holds services primarily in Russian, lost its sanctuary to a fire on Dec. 19, 2025; investigators pointed to Christmas lights. FOX 9 reported that its members are "primarily Russian—Ukrainian," that it had welcomed many Ukrainian immigrants and that it had been a drop-off site for Operation Christmas Child for 15 years ([FOX 9, December 2025, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.fox9.com/news/eagans-grace-slavic-church-fire-leaves-community-heartbroken); [ChurchLeaders, Dec. 23, 2025](https://churchleaders.com/news/2210916-after-devastating-fire-minnesota-church-celebrates-christmas-in-borrowed-space.html)). A 2011 Mennonite Brethren report counted two Slavic congregations of that denomination in Minnesota and noted that Slavic churches commonly call themselves Baptist or Pentecostal whatever their formal affiliation ([Christian Leader, Sept. 1, 2011](https://christianleadermag.com/borisov-joins-usmb-staff/)). A full current list of Minnesota Slavic churches could not be assembled.

**Jewish life.** Russian-American Jews in Minnesota (RAJMN) began in 2003 under the Jewish Federation of Greater St. Paul and, by 2017, had held more than 300 events and a teen fellowship ([TC Jewfolk, 2017](https://tcjewfolk.com/2017/01/12/energizing-connecting-tcs-russianjews/)). The VOICE initiative, a joint effort of Jewish Community Action, the Minneapolis Jewish Federation and the Sabes JCC, worked with older Russian-speaking Jews from about 2004 ([TC Jewfolk, 2010](https://tcjewfolk.com/2010/06/02/voices-minnesotas-soviet-jews-inspiration-film/); reader comment in [MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)). RAJMN's director said in 2017 that many of her peers had "stuck together away from the Jewish community," and that her work aimed to bridge that gap ([TC Jewfolk, 2017](https://tcjewfolk.com/2017/01/12/energizing-connecting-tcs-russianjews/)).

**Islam.** Families from Uzbekistan, Azerbaijan, Kazakhstan and other Central Asian or Caucasian countries may be Muslim, and the director of the Russian Educational Center said Muslim children attend alongside Christian and Jewish ones ([Eden Prairie Local News, 2021](https://eplocalnews.org/2021/08/31/states-quality-of-life-schools-draw-russians/)). No Minnesota mosque or Muslim organization specific to Russian speakers was found.

## 6. Celebrations and observances

Holidays divide along faith and history more than language. These feed the calendar file.

- **New Year (Novy God), Jan. 1.** In Soviet times New Year's was the main secular winter holiday. Workers at Smak told the Macalester guide that although New Year's is traditionally the biggest holiday, families in Minnesota often adopt Christmas-season habits, and the store is busiest at Thanksgiving and Christmas ([Macalester guide](https://www.arcgis.com/sharing/rest/content/items/74be859446ff445bae1afeebcf66a5b7/data?f=json)). No public Minnesota New Year (yolka) event with a dated announcement was found.
- **Orthodox Christmas, Jan. 7.** Russian Orthodox parishes keep the Julian calendar, so Christmas falls on Jan. 7. Both Russian Church Outside Russia parishes post Russian-and-English service schedules ([St. Panteleimon](https://spro.church/); [Resurrection Skete](https://www.resurrectionskete.org/)), but no 2027 Christmas schedule was posted when checked.
- **Maslenitsa (Butter Week).** The week before Orthodox Lent. The Museum of Russian Art held "Maslenitsa: Russian Pancakes & Songs" with its folk group Nitka at its Education Center, 5904 Pleasant Ave. S., on Feb. 17, 2026 ([TMORA, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://tmora.org/programs/maslenitsa-russian-pancakes-songs/)), and St. Panteleimon holds Maslenitsa events ([Macalester guide](https://www.arcgis.com/sharing/rest/content/items/74be859446ff445bae1afeebcf66a5b7/data?f=json)).
- **Orthodox Easter (Pascha).** April 12 in 2026, May 2 in 2027 ([American Carpatho-Russian Orthodox Diocese, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.acrod.org/prayercorner/movablefeasts/)) and April 16 in 2028 ([TrueCalendar](https://truecalendar.com/orthodox-easter-dates/2028)).
- **Parish feast days.** St. Panteleimon's patronal feast is celebrated in early August; in 2026 it fell on Aug. 8 and 9 with the parish's 70th anniversary ([St. Panteleimon](https://spro.church/celebration-of-patronal-feast-and-70th-anniversary-of-st-panteleimon-parish/)). St. Herman's will mark its 50th anniversary on Nov. 21 and 22, 2026 ([St. Herman's](https://www.sthermanmpls.org/)).
- **Victory Day, May 9.** Marks the end of World War II in Europe in Soviet memory. A 2010 documentary by Minnesota's VOICE program focused on Soviet Jewish soldiers' role in that victory ([TC Jewfolk, 2010](https://tcjewfolk.com/2010/06/02/voices-minnesotas-soviet-jews-inspiration-film/)), and a 2016 issue of *Zerkalo* carried readers' World War II memories ([Twin Cities Business, 2016](https://tcbmag.com/mirror-on-the-midwest-russian-language-zerkalo-celebrates-25-years-in-minnesota/)). No public Minnesota Victory Day event was found for 2025 or 2026. Since 2022 the day carries very different meanings in different households.
- **Jewish holidays.** For Jewish households, Hanukkah, Passover and the High Holidays matter more than any "Russian" holiday; RAJMN's Hanukkah celebration in 2016 was described as very successful ([TC Jewfolk, 2017](https://tcjewfolk.com/2017/01/12/energizing-connecting-tcs-russianjews/)).
- **Feb. 24.** On the first anniversary of the invasion of Ukraine in 2023, Zerkalo's publisher joined a candlelight vigil at the Lowry Avenue Bridge in Minneapolis ([CCX Media, 2023](https://ccxmedia.org/news/plymouth-newspaper-keeps-russian-speaking-immigrants-informed-on-ukraine/)). This is a remembrance for some, not a celebration.

## 7. Family, home and daily life

Sources here are few and general; they describe patterns some families report, not rules.

**Family and generations.** Culture Care Connection describes grandparents helping raise grandchildren while parents work and children expected to care for elders, and says family members are often consulted in health care planning ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/russian/)). Some young parents work hard to keep Russian alive: a Maple Grove mother sent her son to a Russian camp partly so he could talk with his grandmother, who does not speak English ([Christian Science Monitor, 2022](https://www.csmonitor.com/Arts-Culture/2022/1121/How-a-US-camp-helps-Russian-speaking-kids-in-time-of-war)). In other families the gap between generations is painful. Igor Fridman told MinnPost that parents "don't get it" when children adopt American habits, and a community leader said shame keeps some parents from seeking help when a teenager uses drugs ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)).

**Elders.** In 2016 a 90-year-old woman from Moldova said of her senior building, where residents chatted in Russian by the elevators, "This place feels like home" ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)). The director of the Russian Educational Center said in 2021 that older Russian Americans with limited English can be found in St. Louis Park, Minnetonka and Minneapolis, though they are not typical of those cities' Russian speakers ([Eden Prairie Local News, 2021](https://eplocalnews.org/2021/08/31/states-quality-of-life-schools-draw-russians/)).

**Food.** Shops sell herring, Russian-style rye bread, sausages, cakes and imported sweets ([Macalester guide](https://www.arcgis.com/sharing/rest/content/items/74be859446ff445bae1afeebcf66a5b7/data?f=json); [Atly](https://www.atly.com/location/SMAK)), and menus range from borscht and pelmeni to Uzbek plov and Moldovan mamaliga (section 4). Some import brands have been hard to get, according to customer reviews summarized on a listing for Smak ([Atly, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.atly.com/location/SMAK)).

**Names.** No Minnesota source was found documenting how agencies record patronymics (names formed from a father's given name) or family names whose endings change by gender, so it is worth asking each person how they want their name written.

**Childhood.** Weekend schools, summer camps and the charter school described in section 10 give many children a Russian-speaking world outside home. In one Shakopee family that sought asylum after 2022, all three children had Ukrainian refugee classmates, and the 16-year-old wrote school papers against the war ([Stars and Stripes, 2023](https://www.stripes.com/theaters/us/2023-04-12/antiwar-russians-flee-homeland-resettle-minnesota-9781406.html)).

## 8. Arts, media, sports and culture

**Museums and theater.** The Museum of Russian Art (TMORA), in a former church at 5500 Stevens Ave. S. in Minneapolis, was founded in 2002 by Minnesota collectors Raymond and Susan Johnson, who are not Russian speakers; it began in Bloomington and moved to Minneapolis in 2005 ([TMORA, History, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://tmora.org/about/history/)). A Russian Cultural Center founder pointed out in 2016 that the museum "owes its existence" to the Johnsons, not to Soviet immigrants (reader comment in [MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)). Days after the 2022 invasion, the museum painted a banner in Ukraine's colors and said it "stands with the people of Ukraine" ([KSTP, 2022](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/russian-institutions-in-minnesota-show-support-for-ukraine/); [NPR, March 1, 2022](https://www.npr.org/2022/03/01/1083694731/u-s-russian-cultural-organizations-ukraine)). It opened "Say No to War," cartoons by Ukrainian and Russian artists, on April 20, 2022 ([300 Magazine, July 16, 2022](https://300magazine.com/tmora-say-no-to-war-exhibition-goes-through-to-the-second-round/)), and in 2023 curator Maria Zavialova, from St. Petersburg, paired a Ukrainian American artist's war paintings with Soviet-era work ([MPR News, March 8, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/03/08/ukraine-defiant-a-russian-art-museum-grapples-with-a-russian-war)). Its folk group Nitka performs village songs ([TMORA](https://tmora.org/programs/maslenitsa-russian-pancakes-songs/)).

Theatre Novi Most, founded in 1998 by Russian director Vladimir Rovinsky and American director Lisa Channer, makes new international work. In 2026 it reported that a $10,000 National Endowment for the Arts grant was withdrawn and that it was raising money for legal aid for Ukrainian refugees facing immigration enforcement ([Theatre Novi Most, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.theatrenovimost.org/); [Macalester College Russian Studies](https://www.macalester.edu/russian-studies/about/inminnesota/)).

**Community arts groups.** The American Russian Center, formerly Russian Soul, has been a 501(c)(3) since 2009. It says its members come from the U.S., Russia, Kazakhstan, Ukraine, Belarus, Armenia, Georgia and other countries, states that it condemns "the aggression of the Russian leadership against Ukraine," and names older adults and people with disabilities in its mission ([American Russian Center](https://americanrussiancenter.org/)). Its founder wrote in 2016 that Russian Soul had performed and demonstrated folk crafts at the Festival of Nations ([MinnPost reader comment, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)). That festival returned in April 2026 as the International Festival of Minnesota ([Yahoo News, July 31, 2025](https://www.yahoo.com/news/articles/festival-nations-return-st-paul-210800721.html)); no list of its 2026 exhibitors was found.

**Dance.** Kirill Bak-Stepanoff and Svetlana Bak-Gavrilova direct the Minnesota Ballet Theatre and School in Eden Prairie, teaching the Vaganova method ([Eden Prairie Local News, 2021](https://eplocalnews.org/2021/08/31/states-quality-of-life-schools-draw-russians/)). Dance With Us America in Edina says it has trained 17 national champions ([Dance With Us America](https://www.dancewithusamerica.com/)).

**Media.** *Zerkalo* and *North Star* (Plymouth) are the long-running local Russian-language publications (section 4). Their publisher said in 2023 that he opposes the war but tries to give readers facts rather than take sides, with a section for readers' opinions ([CCX Media, 2023](https://ccxmedia.org/news/plymouth-newspaper-keeps-russian-speaking-immigrants-informed-on-ukraine/)). A Minnesota Russian speaker told WCCO in 2022 that members of his group are bound by language, but "it gets down to what news are you following, who are you trusting" ([WCCO/CBS Minnesota, Feb. 2022](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/its-a-lot-of-tears-russians-and-ukrainians-in-minnesota-ache-as-war-breaks-out/)).

**Sports.** The Minnesota Wild's 2026 playoff roster included three Russian-born forwards: Kirill Kaprizov of Novokuznetsk, Vladimir Tarasenko of Yaroslavl and Yakov Trenin of Chelyabinsk ([NHL.com, 2026](https://www.nhl.com/news/topic/playoffs/minnesota-wild-2026-stanley-cup-playoff-roster-at-a-glance)). Tarasenko, acquired in June 2025, scored 23 goals in 2025-26, and the Wild named Kaprizov among its 40-goal scorers ([Minnesota Wild, April 2026](https://www.nhl.com/wild/news/tarasenko-takes-his-shot-041726)). Their contracts after the 2025-26 season could not be confirmed. Fans and players are not the same as the community, but these players are among the most visible Russian speakers in the state. The Russian Educational Center runs a chess section ([Russian Educational Center, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.studyrussian.org/)).

## 9. Civic and political life

**Organizations.** Civic life runs through many small organizations rather than one center. Besides the churches, synagogue programs and arts groups above:

- The Slavic Community Center, founded in 2003 by Gedaly Meerovich in St. Paul, offered early childhood education, ESL, financial literacy and chemical-dependency prevention, and created Nasha Shkola in 2012, according to MinnPost in 2016 ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)). Its old website did not load, and its current status could not be confirmed.
- BelarusianAmericans.org, a Minneapolis-based nonprofit, supports Belarusian refugees and immigrants, promotes Belarusian language and culture, and opposes the war in Ukraine ([BelarusianAmericans.org profile on Benevity, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://causes.benevity.org/causes/840-823189245)).
- Russians Against War, founded by longtime Russian American Elena Mityushina, organized anti-war protests at the State Capitol in 2023 ([Stars and Stripes, 2023](https://www.stripes.com/theaters/us/2023-04-12/antiwar-russians-flee-homeland-resettle-minnesota-9781406.html)).
- East-West Connections promotes citizen diplomacy and keeps lists of Russian-language churches and programs; its church list ends "We stand with Ukraine" ([East-West Connections, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://east-westconnections.com/russianlanguage-churches)).

**Visibility.** Gedaly Meerovich told MinnPost in 2016 that foundations and state officials overlooked Russian-speaking needs: "Our community needs help as well." MinnPost called the community "invisible immigrants," noting that, unlike Hmong and Somali Minnesotans, Russian speakers had not built a strong presence in electoral politics ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)). The founder of Russian Soul disagreed in a comment, writing that Russian speakers are "probably the most disassociated immigrant community" she knows and that the opportunities are there "for those, who want to seek them." No Minnesota elected official who has publicly described growing up in a Russian-speaking home was found.

**Language access.** The Minnesota Secretary of State publishes voting materials in Russian among its top languages ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 22, 2025, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/minnesota-non-english-languages-census-data/)). A 2007 Minnesota Department of Human Services language plan listed Russian among 10 languages for interpreter standards and among seven on its multilingual phone line ([DHS Limited English Proficiency Plan, DHS-4210, April 2007, hosted by Migration Policy Institute](https://www.migrationpolicy.org/sites/default/files/language_portal/Limited%20English%20Proficiency%20Plan.pdf)).

**Debate since 2022.** The war split some friendships and families. A St. Paul restaurant owner told MPR in 2022 that she had fallen out with a few longtime friends who, the reporter noted, watch Kremlin-controlled TV and support Putin; she said, "I am their enemy now" ([NPR/MPR News, March 2022](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/1084800707)). Russian Camp MN organizers said "opposing views of the war" had brought tension among some expatriates, though not inside the camp ([Christian Science Monitor, 2022](https://www.csmonitor.com/Arts-Culture/2022/1121/How-a-US-camp-helps-Russian-speaking-kids-in-time-of-war)). Several organizations, including the American Russian Center and Russian Camp MN, now state on their websites that they condemn the Russian leadership's aggression ([American Russian Center](https://americanrussiancenter.org/); [Russian Camp MN](https://russiancampmn.com/)). These are institutional statements; individual views vary and no Minnesota survey of opinion was found.

## 10. Education

**Public school.** Nasha Shkola ("Our School") Charter School, 6717 85th Ave. N., Brooklyn Park, is a K-8 public charter school with daily Russian classes; all other classes are in English. It says it is the only public school in Minnesota teaching Russian language and culture to elementary students and that 95 percent of students speak Russian at home or have a Russian-speaking parent ([Nasha Shkola](https://nashashkolamn.org/)). It enrolled about 120 students in 2018, when about 75 percent were learning English as a second language and staff described climbing out of state "priority school" status ([CCX Media, 2018, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://ccxmedia.org/news/school-spotlight-nasha-shkola-embraces-slavic-culture/)). GreatSchools lists 138 students and 60 percent English learners ([GreatSchools](https://www.greatschools.org/minnesota/brooklyn-park/nasha-shkola-charter-school/)). The school hosts the American Council of Teachers of Russian spoken-Russian Olympiada for grades 6 to 8 ([Nasha Shkola](https://nashashkolamn.org/)).

**Weekend and summer programs.** The Russian Educational Center of Minnesota, founded in 2002 by parent volunteers, teaches children from age 3½, runs a course to prepare high school students for an AP-style Russian exam, and offers theater, art, chess and cooking clubs ([Russian Educational Center](https://www.studyrussian.org/); [Macalester guide](https://www.arcgis.com/sharing/rest/content/items/74be859446ff445bae1afeebcf66a5b7/data?f=json)). In 2021 tuition was $240 per trimester ([Eden Prairie Local News, 2021](https://eplocalnews.org/2021/08/31/states-quality-of-life-schools-draw-russians/)). Russian Camp MN ("Igra. Unplugged"), led by Tamara von Schmidt-Pauli since 2015, ran overnight sessions on the St. Croix River; in 2022 it served 400 children from across the U.S., including newly arrived Ukrainians ([Christian Science Monitor, 2022](https://www.csmonitor.com/Arts-Culture/2022/1121/How-a-US-camp-helps-Russian-speaking-kids-in-time-of-war)). Its website now describes it as a day camp ([Russian Camp MN](https://russiancampmn.com/)). Concordia Language Villages runs Lesnoe Ozero, a Russian language village for young people ([Concordia Language Villages, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.concordialanguagevillages.org/languages/russian/)).

**Students.** Macalester's Russian Studies program reports that more than 2,300 Minnesota public school students speak Russian at home; it does not give a year ([Macalester College Russian Studies](https://www.macalester.edu/russian-studies/about/inminnesota/)). Current Minnesota Department of Education language counts were not reachable.

**Higher education.** The University of Minnesota's Department of German, Nordic, Slavic and Dutch includes Anatoly Liberman ([University of Minnesota](https://cla.umn.edu/about/directory/profile/aliber)), and Macalester's Russian Studies students and faculty wrote the Twin Cities Guide for Russian Speakers with community partners ([Macalester guide](https://www.arcgis.com/sharing/rest/content/items/74be859446ff445bae1afeebcf66a5b7/data?f=json)).

## 11. Health and wellbeing, broadly

Minnesota health data broken out for Russian speakers are scarce. Culture Care Connection, a Minnesota health equity resource, cautions that data on Russian Americans "is limited and may not apply" in Minnesota ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/russian/)). It reports:

- In 2017, 88 percent of Minnesota patients who listed Russian as their preferred language were screened for adolescent mental health or depression, above the state average of 73 percent.
- A 2013 study found immigrants age 50 to 84 from the former Soviet Union reported more limits in daily activities than U.S.-born white people, but less smoking and heavy drinking.
- Some patients may expect a warmer, closer relationship with doctors than U.S. practice offers, and may use home remedies such as cupping (*banki*), which can leave marks.

These are generalizations from one resource and should be checked with each person. A Rutgers dissertation based on interviews with 20 Russian-speaking immigrants on the East Coast found that beliefs about health were tied to experiences with health care in both countries ([Rutgers University, 2019, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://rucore.libraries.rutgers.edu/rutgers-lib/59923/record/)); it is background, not Minnesota evidence.

**Care in Russian.** Jewish Family and Children's Service, which resettled many Soviet Jewish families and traces its founding to a 1910 meeting to help Jewish refugees, remains a door for some families ([JFCS, 2017](https://jfcsmpls.org/desk-ceo-judy-halper-february-2017/); [JFCS, About](https://www.jfcsmn.org/about-us/)). Its own public pages did not describe current Russian-language services. The Minnesota JCC formed in 2021 from the St. Paul JCC and Sabes JCC ([Minnesota JCC, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://minnesotajcc.org/)). No Minnesota clinic run specifically for Russian speakers could be confirmed from a current public page.

**Substance use and mental health.** Community leader Gedaly Meerovich described rising drug use among young people in 2016 and parents' reluctance to seek help because of shame ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)). The war has added stress: people in Minnesota described relatives under fire in Ukraine and family in Russia facing sanctions and the draft ([WCCO/CBS Minnesota, 2022](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/its-a-lot-of-tears-russians-and-ukrainians-in-minnesota-ache-as-war-breaks-out/); [NPR/MPR, 2022](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/1084800707)).

## 12. Disability

**Minnesota evidence is thin.** No Minnesota study, Department of Health report or Department of Human Services report was found that counts disability among Russian speakers or people born in the former Soviet Union. The Census table used in section 1 does not cross language with disability. What follows is what could be found.

**Who defines disability here.** For many families, the first definitions came from Soviet or post-Soviet systems. Alfiya Battalova, who spent a year as an exchange student at a Minnesota high school, wrote that it "felt like a revelation" to see ramps, elevators and dignity for disabled people, and described Russian approaches as based on a medical model ([Center for Disability Rights, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://cdrnys.org/blog/advocacy/new-advocacy-intern-from-russia-shares-perspective-on-disability-rights-in-the-u-s/)). That is one person's view. A 2006 Macalester College honors thesis studied mothers of disabled children in one Siberian village ([Macalester College, Digital Commons](https://digitalcommons.macalester.edu/anth_honors/1/)); it describes a place in Russia, not Minnesota families. Culture Care Connection says "some Russian immigrants believe that disability or illness is caused by something the individual did" ([Culture Care Connection](https://culturecareconnection.org/cultural-responsiveness/russian/)). It gives no Minnesota source for that statement, and it should be treated as a question to ask, not a description of anyone.

**What a label opens or closes.** Questions worth asking include whether a family fears a diagnosis will follow a child, how earlier experiences with institutions shape trust, and whether an immigration case makes a family cautious about public programs. One family's story shows how these meet. A Russian couple in Eagan who fled after 2022 told NPR that their younger daughter was born with spina bifida and uses a walker, and that the cost of her care and the military draft pushed them to leave Russia; the father was detained by immigration authorities in December 2025 while their asylum case was pending ([NPR, Feb. 26, 2026](https://www.npr.org/2026/02/26/nx-s1-5684806/as-trump-dismantles-asylum-programs-russians-and-ukrainians-fear-for-future)).

**Services in Russian.** Disability Hub MN, the state's free disability information line, says "We serve all languages" ([Disability Hub MN, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://disabilityhubmn.org/)). The American Russian Center names people with disabilities and older adults in its mission ([American Russian Center](https://americanrussiancenter.org/)). A state aging-resources listing describes a Savage adult day center as catering to Russian-speaking clients, but that listing could not be reached, so the center is not confirmed here. Nasha Shkola, as a public school, carries special education duties for its students. No Minnesota disability advocacy or parent group run by and for Russian speakers was confirmed.

**What produced an impairment.** No Minnesota source documents causes of disability among Russian speakers. Plausible questions include aging in a group that skews older, war injuries and trauma among recent arrivals, and work injuries in care and manufacturing jobs, but none of these is established by Minnesota evidence.

## 13. What is changing now

- **Asylum and enforcement (2023 to 2026).** Russians who fled the war or the draft had no sponsorship program like Ukrainians and waited years in the asylum system ([Stars and Stripes, 2023](https://www.stripes.com/theaters/us/2023-04-12/antiwar-russians-flee-homeland-resettle-minnesota-9781406.html)). NPR reported in February 2026 that hundreds of Russians and Ukrainians were being returned under the current administration, and profiled the Eagan family above ([NPR, 2026](https://www.npr.org/2026/02/26/nx-s1-5684806/as-trump-dismantles-asylum-programs-russians-and-ukrainians-fear-for-future)). A Moscow Times opinion piece reported that mass deportations of Russians who lost asylum cases began in summer 2025 and that some deportees were questioned or arrested on arrival ([The Moscow Times, Sept. 4, 2025](https://www.themoscowtimes.com/2025/09/04/for-russian-asylum-seekers-the-united-states-is-not-the-land-of-the-free-a90426)); this is national background. Theatre Novi Most said in 2026 that Ukrainian refugees in Minneapolis faced legal costs after contact with ICE ([Theatre Novi Most](https://www.theatrenovimost.org/)). No count of Minnesota detentions by nationality was found.
- **Ukrainian newcomers.** Arrivals from Ukraine since 2022 have joined Russian-speaking churches and camps; Grace Slavic Church welcomed many Ukrainian immigrants before its 2025 fire ([FOX 9, 2025](https://www.fox9.com/news/eagans-grace-slavic-church-fire-leaves-community-heartbroken)). Some Ukrainians prefer not to be addressed in Russian at all; this page does not speak for them.
- **Losing and rebuilding places.** Minsk Market in Eagan was sold and closed for renovation as of October 2026 ([Macalester guide](https://www.arcgis.com/sharing/rest/content/items/74be859446ff445bae1afeebcf66a5b7/data?f=json)); Grace Slavic Church is rebuilding; St. Panteleimon is raising money for an expansion ([St. Panteleimon](https://spro.church/)).
- **Generational change.** Organizations built for 1990s arrivals now serve grandchildren. Russian Camp MN shifted to a day-camp format ([Russian Camp MN](https://russiancampmn.com/)), and the Russian Soul group renamed itself the American Russian Center ([American Russian Center](https://americanrussiancenter.org/)).
- **Identity.** Camp organizers said in 2022 that families talk more about their regional and religious roots and that they had stopped playing Battleship ([Christian Science Monitor, 2022](https://www.csmonitor.com/Arts-Culture/2022/1121/How-a-US-camp-helps-Russian-speaking-kids-in-time-of-war)). The museum devoted to Russian art has made room for Ukrainian voices ([MPR News, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/03/08/ukraine-defiant-a-russian-art-museum-grapples-with-a-russian-war)).

## 14. Strengths and contributions

- **Skill and science.** People trained in Soviet universities have taught and researched at the University of Minnesota for decades ([University of Minnesota, Shifman](https://cse.umn.edu/physics/mikhail-shifman); [University of Minnesota, Liberman](https://cla.umn.edu/about/directory/profile/aliber)), and many work in Minnesota's medical-technology industry ([MinnPost, 2016](https://www.minnpost.com/new-americans/2016/06/how-russian-speaking-community-became-minnesotas-invisible-immigrants/)).
- **Self-built institutions.** Parents built a weekend school (2002), a charter school (2012), summer camps and a publishing company with almost no outside help ([Russian Educational Center](https://www.studyrussian.org/); [Nasha Shkola](https://nashashkolamn.org/); [Twin Cities Business, 2016](https://tcbmag.com/mirror-on-the-midwest-russian-language-zerkalo-celebrates-25-years-in-minnesota/)).
- **Mutual aid.** Russian Americans vouched for, hosted and co-signed leases for newly arrived asylum seekers they met online ([Stars and Stripes, 2023](https://www.stripes.com/theaters/us/2023-04-12/antiwar-russians-flee-homeland-resettle-minnesota-9781406.html)), and more than 15 churches offered space to Grace Slavic Church after its fire ([ChurchLeaders, 2025](https://churchleaders.com/news/2210916-after-devastating-fire-minnesota-church-celebrates-christmas-in-borrowed-space.html)).
- **Arts.** Ballet, ballroom dance, folk song, theater and a nationally known art museum all have Russian-speaking roots or leadership here (section 8).
- **Bridging.** Several groups explicitly try to hold people of different nations, faiths and politics together, such as the camp counselor's "common language" ([Christian Science Monitor, 2022](https://www.csmonitor.com/Arts-Culture/2022/1121/How-a-US-camp-helps-Russian-speaking-kids-in-time-of-war)) and Nasha Shkola teachers who described "building bridges between people speaking different languages" ([CCX Media, 2018](https://ccxmedia.org/news/school-spotlight-nasha-shkola-embraces-slavic-culture/)).

## What could not be established

- **A reliable population count.** Census counts of Russian speakers (about 13,900 to 15,500) and community estimates (45,000 to 60,000 people from the former Soviet Union) are far apart, and no source explains the community estimates. No Minnesota Compass cultural-community profile exists for Russian speakers.
- **Income, poverty, home ownership and education for Russian speakers today.** The figures in section 2 are from 2018 or earlier, from a secondary source. No current Minnesota table was found.
- **City and county detail.** Single-year Census language data were available only for Hennepin County. Minnesota Department of Education home-language counts could not be reached.
- **Central Asian, Caucasian, Moldovan and Baltic communities.** Apart from one Uzbek restaurant and one Belarusian nonprofit, no Minnesota organizations for these groups were found.
- **Evangelical churches.** No complete, current list of Russian-language Baptist and Pentecostal churches in Minnesota was found.
- **Disability.** No Minnesota data, study or Russian-language disability organization was found. A state aging-resources directory listing a Russian-focused adult day center could not be reached.
- **Professional businesses.** Law, accounting, insurance, medical, pharmacy and travel businesses serving Russian speakers could not be confirmed from current public pages. The Russian Yellow Pages directory was not readable online.
- **Public holiday events.** No dated public New Year (yolka), Orthodox Christmas or Victory Day event in Minnesota was found for 2025 or 2026.
- **Elected officials.** No Minnesota elected official with a publicly stated Russian-speaking background was confirmed.
- **Blocked or unreachable pages:** Minnesota state agency pages on mn.gov (including the State Demographic Center and the aging-resources directory), the MDH refugee data downloads, Russian-American Jews in Minnesota's website (rajmn.org), Sunflower Daycare's website, the Slavic Community Center and Russian Soul's former websites, the Migration Policy Institute state language page, the Minnesota Reformer, PuckPedia, and the Wilder Research 2000 survey PDF.
