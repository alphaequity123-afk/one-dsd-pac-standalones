# African American in Minnesota
Checked: October 31, 2026

This page is about Black Minnesotans born in the United States, many with family roots here going back generations and others who came from Illinois, the South or elsewhere within a lifetime. African-born communities and their children have their own pages (Somali, Ethiopian, Oromo, Liberian, Nigerian, Kenyan and others). The two overlap in neighborhoods, churches, mosques, schools, marriages and the way strangers and institutions treat them. They differ in history, and in many of the numbers below.

## 1. People and place

**How many.** Minnesota Compass, using the American Community Survey for 2020 to 2024, counts 272,870 African American Minnesotans, give or take 9,028. It places 79.6 percent in the Twin Cities and 20.4 percent in Greater Minnesota ([Minnesota Compass, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/african-american)).

**Who that number counts.** Compass counts people born in the United States who select Black or African American, alone or with another race, including Hispanic respondents. It leaves out people whose foreign-born parents live in the same household and people who name Somali or Ethiopian ancestry. It does include people of Liberian ancestry, and it notes that respondents may undercount themselves ([Compass, how the profiles are built, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/methodology)). So the figure is a close stand-in for this page's community, not an exact count of it. A U.S.-born adult child of immigrants living on their own may be inside it, and no public count separates people by lineage.

**The wider Black population.** Compass said in March 2024 that Minnesota has more than 450,000 Black residents, nearly 8 percent of the state, without stating a year for that figure ([Minnesota Compass, March 14, 2024](https://mncompass.org/data-insights/articles/importance-data-disaggregation-minnesotas-black-communities)). Its quiz page reports the non-Hispanic Black share growing from 3.5 percent in the 2000 census to 7 percent in 2020 ([Minnesota Compass, Feb. 14, 2022](https://www.mncompass.org/data-insights/quizzes/compass-quiz-minnesotas-black-population)). Compass lists Somali (85,533), Ethiopian (45,159), Liberian (22,571), Nigerian (21,206) and Kenyan (20,222) profiles beside the African American one, each from pooled survey years ([Compass, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities)). The Census Bureau's 2020 to 2024 survey counts 514,603 Minnesotans who list Black alone or with another race, give or take 2,213 ([Census Reporter, table B02009, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B02009&geo_ids=05000US27053,05000US27123,04000US27)). Most statistics published about "Black Minnesotans" combine all of these people. This page says so wherever that is the case.

**Young, and English-speaking at home.** In the Compass profile, 36.0 percent are 17 or younger and 6.4 percent are 65 or older. The largest adult age group is 25 to 34, at 16.5 percent. English only is spoken at home by 96.3 percent of those 5 and older. The foreign-born row is suppressed, as expected from the way the group is defined ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/african-american)).

**How people came.** Black people lived in what became Minnesota before statehood. MNopedia, the Minnesota Historical Society's encyclopedia, names George Bonga, born near Duluth in 1802; James Thompson, enslaved at Fort Snelling in 1827 and freed in 1837; and Dred and Harriet Scott, who lived at Fort Snelling in the 1830s. In May 1863, the steamboats Northerner and Davenport brought people who had escaped slavery in Missouri to St. Paul, the Davenport with more than 200 people. Later arrivals came for railroad, hotel and shipping work. In 1898, 85 people moved from Kentucky to Fergus Falls, and many of those families left during the Depression ([MNopedia, modified Oct. 5, 2026](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/african-americans-minnesota)). Census counts in the same entry run from 3,683 in 1890 to 53,344 in 1980.

**Where U.S.-born Black Minnesotans were born.** The most recent state report found on this, from the former Council on Black Minnesotans using 2007 to 2011 survey data, found about 56 percent of U.S.-born Black Minnesotans were born in Minnesota and 44 percent elsewhere. Illinois was the most common other birthplace, at about 14 percent or 33,200 people, followed by Indiana, Missouri and Wisconsin ([Council on Black Minnesotans report, via Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2014/mandated/140004.pdf)). These numbers are old. They show a community made up of both Minnesota-born people and people who arrived from other states. Newer survey data point the same way: among Minnesotans who list Black as their only race and were born in the United States, about 58 percent were born in Minnesota (see the counts below).

**Three old neighborhoods, then many places.** Housing discrimination, including restrictive covenants, shaped where people could live. MNopedia describes restrictive deeds and refusal to sell as producing three distinct Black neighborhoods in Minneapolis ([MNopedia, Southside, modified Nov. 7, 2025](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/southside-african-american-community-minneapolis)).

- **Rondo, St. Paul.** Roughly University Avenue to Selby Avenue and Rice Street to Lexington Avenue, with Dale Street dividing Upper and Lower Rondo. By the 1930s, half of St. Paul's Black population lived there. Construction of Interstate 94 began in 1956, and the road opened December 9, 1968 ([MNopedia, Rondo, modified Nov. 12, 2025](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/rondo-neighborhood-st-paul)). Sources do not agree on how many people lost homes: MNopedia's overview says more than 600 residents ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/african-americans-minnesota)), the AARP profile of Rondo elder Marvin Anderson says more than 1,000 families and more than 100 Black-owned businesses ([AARP, Feb. 1, 2023](https://www.aarp.org/livable-communities/getting-around/info-2023/before-the-highway-rondo-saint-paul-minnesota-marvin-anderson.html)), and the Minnesota Spokesman-Recorder and MPR News say thousands of residents ([Spokesman-Recorder, Feb. 22, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/02/22/pilgrim-baptist-church-minnesota-history/); [MPR News, June 23, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/06/23/160-years-of-sanctuary-at-pilgrim-baptist-church)). Rev. Floyd Massey's Pilgrim Baptist lobbied successfully for a pedestrian bridge so members could still reach church ([Spokesman-Recorder](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/02/22/pilgrim-baptist-church-minnesota-history/)).
- **Near North, Minneapolis.** A Jewish neighborhood in the early 1900s, especially along Plymouth Avenue, that became the city's largest Black community. By 1960 about a third of the city's Black residents lived in its six neighborhoods. Sumner Field public housing opened in 1938, segregated but with Black and Jewish neighbors who generally got along, MNopedia says. The 1995 Hollman v. Cisneros case led to its demolition ([MNopedia, Near North, modified Oct. 23, 2025](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/near-north-african-american-community-minneapolis)). In July 1967 unrest along Plymouth Avenue followed police treatment of Black teenagers at the Aquatennial parade. The National Guard came in, more than 30 people were arrested, and an all-white grand jury found no evidence of police brutality ([Spokesman-Recorder, June 6, 2020](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2020/06/06/minneapolis-long-hot-summer-of-67-and-parallels-to-todays-protests-over-police-brutality/)). In May 2026 a business coalition leader spoke of one of the metro's highest concentrations of Black residents, and of a recovery from 2020 damage that has been uneven ([BuyBlack.org, May 27, 2026](https://www.buyblack.org/articles/west-broadway-s-future-empowering-north-minneapolis-black-community)).
- **Southside, Minneapolis.** East 34th to East 46th Streets, Nicollet to Chicago Avenues, thriving from the 1930s to the 1970s and historically including Central, Bryant and Regina. Edward Tilsen built Tilsenbilt Homes for sale to Black buyers from 1954 to 1956. Construction of I-35W cleared more than fifty square blocks from 1959 and split the neighborhood, and Central High School closed in 1982 ([MNopedia, Southside](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/southside-african-american-community-minneapolis)). Streets.mn describes it as a middle-class, mixed-race area with little political power to stop the highway ([Streets.mn, Sept. 29, 2025](https://streets.mn/2025/09/29/community-and-connection-a-history-of-35th-and-36th-streets/)). In 1931, Arthur and Edith Lee, a Black family, faced two years of mob harassment after buying a house in a white South Minneapolis neighborhood ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/african-americans-minnesota)).
- **Duluth.** Black residents came for railroad, shipping and mining work, and U.S. Steel recruited Southern Black laborers in the 1920s, though many left over low wages, segregated housing and cold. MNopedia gives a Black population of 1,946 in 2015 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/african-americans-minnesota)). On June 15, 1920, a mob estimated at 10,000 broke into the jail and lynched Elias Clayton, Elmer Jackson and Isaac McGhie, circus workers falsely accused of rape. A doctor had found no evidence of rape. Thirty-seven white men were indicted, three were convicted of rioting, and no one was convicted of murder. The Legislature passed an anti-lynching law on April 21, 1921, after lobbying by Nellie Francis. The graves received headstones in 1991, a monument across the street from the site was dedicated in 2003, and Max Mason received the state's first posthumous pardon on June 12, 2020 ([MNopedia, Duluth Lynchings, modified April 9, 2025](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/duluth-lynchings)). A Duluth NAACP branch formed afterward. Duluth also has an old A.M.E. congregation: the Minnesota Historical Society holds St. Mark A.M.E. parish records from 1893 to 1957 ([MNHS library guide](https://libguides.mnhs.org/aafh/church)). No current source on its status was found.

**Today.** The Spokesman-Recorder reports serving Black communities in the Twin Cities, Duluth and Rochester ([WCCO, Aug. 13, 2024](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/minnesota/news/minnesota-spokesman-recorder-90th-anniversary)). The 2026 list of Black-owned business finalists runs from Minneapolis, St. Paul and Brooklyn Park to Roseville, Apple Valley, Blaine and Rochester ([Spokesman-Recorder, July 17, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/07/17/best-of-black-business-2026-finalists/)). 

**County and city counts.** The newest five-year Census survey (2020 to 2024) counts 402,812 Minnesotans who list Black as their only race, give or take 4,507. Of them, 150,704 were born in Minnesota, 102,365 in another state and 8,182 in a U.S. territory or abroad to American parents, for about 261,250 U.S.-born Black residents; 141,561 were born outside the country ([Census Reporter, American Community Survey table B06004B, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B06004B&geo_ids=04000US27,05000US27053,05000US27123,05000US27003,05000US27037,05000US27163,05000US27109,05000US27145,05000US27137,05000US27139,05000US27013,05000US27027,16000US2743000,16000US2758000,16000US2707966,16000US2707948,16000US2754880,16000US2717000,16000US2706616,16000US2754214,16000US2708794,16000US2756896,16000US2713114,16000US2743252)). These counts leave out people who list Black with another race, which is one reason they are lower than Compass's figure above, and "U.S.-born" here includes children of African and Caribbean immigrants. With that in mind, U.S.-born Black residents who list Black alone number about:

- **Counties:** Hennepin 111,600; Ramsey 45,200; Dakota 23,000; Anoka 20,000; Washington 9,900; Stearns 7,600; Olmsted 7,200; Scott 6,000; St. Louis 2,800; Clay 2,200; Blue Earth 1,700.
- **Cities:** Minneapolis 56,700; St. Paul 34,000; Brooklyn Park 13,600; Brooklyn Center 7,600; St. Cloud 7,600; Rochester 7,000; Burnsville 5,900; Bloomington 5,600; Eagan 4,800; Woodbury 4,000; Columbia Heights 3,800; Coon Rapids 3,700; Maple Grove 3,200; New Hope 3,000; Duluth 2,000; Moorhead 2,000 ([Census Reporter, same table](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B06004B&geo_ids=04000US27,05000US27053,05000US27123,05000US27003,05000US27037,05000US27163,05000US27109,05000US27145,05000US27137,05000US27139,05000US27013,05000US27027,16000US2743000,16000US2758000,16000US2707966,16000US2707948,16000US2754880,16000US2717000,16000US2706616,16000US2754214,16000US2708794,16000US2756896,16000US2713114,16000US2743252); [Census Reporter, more cities](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=B06004B&geo_ids=16000US2771428,16000US2718188,16000US2712700,16000US2714158,16000US2745628,16000US2754808,16000US2743864,16000US2701486,16000US2717288,16000US2759350,16000US2755852,16000US2740166)).

Hennepin and Ramsey together hold about 60 percent of the state's U.S.-born Black residents by this measure. The margins of error are wide for smaller places; Bloomington's total Black-alone count, for example, is 10,645 give or take 1,663. In Duluth and St. Louis County, about 85 percent of Black residents were born in the United States, compared with about 54 percent in Brooklyn Park and St. Cloud, where close to half of Black residents were born abroad (same tables). In Moorhead and Clay County, more U.S.-born Black residents were born in another state than in Minnesota.

## 2. All walks of life

Nothing here describes any one person. Every number below is from a survey, with a range around it.

**The overall picture.** Compass's African American profile for 2020 to 2024 shows a median household income of $48,200, give or take $2,398. In the same profile, 36.2 percent of households earn under $35,000 and 22.2 percent earn $100,000 or more. Both ends are large, and more than one household in five is at the higher end. Owner-occupied homes are 30.1 percent. Of adults 25 and older, 89.5 percent finished high school and 21.9 percent hold a bachelor's degree or more. Among working-age adults, 67.3 percent are employed. Below poverty: 21.4 percent ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/african-american)).

**Wealth.** No Minnesota count of Black millionaires or study of wealthy Black families was found. What exists points to a deep gap in household wealth, measured for all Black Minnesotans together. The 2024 Status of Women and Girls in Minnesota report, as filed with the Minnesota House, says the median net worth of Black households in Minnesota is $0, against $211,000 for white households and $18,000 for Latino households, and calls Minnesota's racial wealth gap the third largest in the country; the passage does not state the year of its data ([Status of Women & Girls in Minnesota 2024, via Minnesota House, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.house.mn.gov/comm/docs/mHvOsaQu6UeTXRNz7uVaow.pdf)). The Minnesota Budget Project, drawing on Prosperity Now's 2020 scorecard and 2016 data, said wealth figures were then available only for white Minnesotans, and that almost three-quarters of Black households lacked enough savings to cover three months of basic needs ([Minnesota Budget Project, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://mnbudgetproject.org/blog/racial-opportunity-gaps-persist-despite-prosperity-for-some)). These are statewide medians. They do not describe any one family, and they sit beside the fifth of households earning $100,000 or more.

**Prominent names.** Public careers show the range. Alan Page, the first Black judge on the Minnesota Supreme Court ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/african-americans-minnesota)). Dave Winfield, raised in Rondo, Baseball Hall of Famer, whose family foundation's Winfield Awards have run since 1977 ([Spokesman-Recorder, June 1, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/06/01/david-winfield-statue-st-paul/)). Tracey Williams-Dillard, third-generation owner of the state's oldest Black-owned business ([WCCO](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/minnesota/news/minnesota-spokesman-recorder-90th-anniversary)). The Minnesota Black Chamber of Commerce's first Black Business Hall of Fame class in August 2026 named her with Sharon Smith-Akinsanya, Gloria Freeman, Lea Harget, Frederick Blockton, Frank Brown, Frederic Estes, Ezell Jones and Prince Wallace, with Candice Ellis and Richard Copeland honored after their deaths ([Spokesman-Recorder, Aug. 13, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/08/13/minnesota-black-business-hall-of-fame-inaugural-class/)).

**Professionals and the middle class.** Rondo had professionals and builders among its families. Marvin Anderson, a retired lawyer and longtime State Law Librarian, grew up there, and his father and partners built the Rangh Court townhomes in 1947 ([AARP](https://www.aarp.org/livable-communities/getting-around/info-2023/before-the-highway-rondo-saint-paul-minnesota-marvin-anderson.html)). Natalie Hudson served as Chief Justice from October 2023 until her retirement on Sept. 30, 2026 ([FOX 9, 2026](https://www.fox9.com/news/minnesota-supreme-court-chief-justice-natalie-hudson-retire-september)). Her father was Macalester College's first Black football coach ([WCCO, Aug. 23, 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/minnesota/news/natalie-hudson-is-named-the-first-black-chief-justice-of-minnesota-supreme-court)). Damon Jenkins leads Minnesota for First Independence Bank ([Spokesman-Recorder, June 29, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/06/29/first-independence-bank-st-paul-university-avenue/)). Architect James Garrett Jr., who describes himself as a fifth-generation St. Paulite from the Rondo community, founded the firm 4RM+ULA in 2002; in 2020 he noted that 0.6 percent of Minnesota architects are Black ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, Nov. 10, 2020, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://mspmag.com/home-and-design/change-maker/)). The Minnesota Association of Black Lawyers, founded in September 1995, says its members have served on the Minnesota Supreme Court, as chief judges, prosecutors and U.S. Attorney, and in private practice ([MABL, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.mabl.org/legacy)).

**Occupations.** Among employed Minnesotans who list Black as their only race (183,871 people, 2020 to 2024, including immigrants), 32.3 percent work in management, business, science and arts jobs, against 45.5 percent of all employed Minnesotans. Women hold slightly more of these professional jobs than men (30,634 to 28,847) ([Census Reporter, table C24010B, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=C24010B&geo_ids=04000US27); [all Minnesotans, table C24010](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=C24010&geo_ids=04000US27)).

**Working families.** Compass shows working-age women employed at 70.0 percent and men at 64.7 percent. Among households, 9.0 percent are married couples with children, 11.6 percent married couples without children, 13.6 percent single mothers, 1.4 percent single fathers and 40.8 percent people living alone ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/african-american)).

**Hardship.** More than half of households (52.2 percent) spend too much of their income on housing, including 62.6 percent of renters ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/african-american)). A May 2026 discussion put median income at about $39,000 in North Minneapolis and about $89,000 citywide, as a speaker's figures rather than a checked table ([Insight News via The Hub, May 22, 2026](https://thehub.news/fraud-scrutiny-tests-trusted-black-institutions/?amp=1)). The state council for Minnesotans of African Heritage, in a 2025 advocacy handout covering all Black Minnesotans, says more than 70 percent of Minnesota households own homes and less than 30 percent of Black Minnesotans do, with majority-Black neighborhoods undervalued by about $48,000 per home ([CMAH handout, March 2025](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/archive/minutes/senate/2025/jobs/20250324/Jobs_20250324_SF2423-CMAH-Black-Homeownership-Factsheet-handout.pdf)). The Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis, as reported by WCCO, found a 29 percent Black homeownership rate in the Twin Cities in 2024 and a Black-white gap of 46.9, the widest among comparable metros ([WCCO, May 10, 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/black-homeownership-twin-cities-gap-minneapolis-federal-reserve/)).

**Compared with African-born neighbors.** The same Compass tables show why the combined "Black" figures can mislead. Median household income: African American $48,200, Somali $49,554 (give or take $6,519), Nigerian $76,000 (give or take $22,344). Owner-occupied homes: African American 30.1 percent, Somali 15.6 percent, Nigerian 45.0 percent. Bachelor's degree or more: African American 21.9 percent, Nigerian 55.3 percent ([African American](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/african-american); [Somali](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/somali); [Nigerian](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/nigerian)). The margins are wide for the smaller groups. A difference like this describes a pattern across households, not any family, and these tables do not explain its causes.

## 3. Work and the economy

**Railroads, hotels, and building unions.** MNopedia records St. Paul porters organizing Lodge No. 5 of the United Brotherhood of Railway Porters in 1887. Frank Boyd, a Pullman porter since 1907, organized the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters' St. Paul meetings and was fired for it; a St. Paul park was named for him in 1976. Many Black workers were limited to jobs as laborers, waiters, cooks and porters, though some worked as stonecutters and bricklayers. Anthony Cassius organized the all-Black waiters' Local 614 in 1935, and a 1940 lawsuit won Black waiters a raise and back pay. Nellie Stone Johnson organized Local 665 and in 1945 won a seat on the Minneapolis Library Board, becoming the first Black person elected to a Minneapolis city office ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/african-americans-minnesota)). Cecil Newman himself worked as a bellhop and a Pullman porter before founding his papers, and led a brewery hiring boycott in 1935 ([National Endowment for the Humanities](https://www.neh.gov/blog/cecil-e-newman-newspaper-publisher-and-advocate-minnesotas-black-community)).

**Unemployment now.** State data for August 2026 show Black Minnesotans at 10.5 percent unemployment, up from 7.7 percent a year earlier, against 4.2 percent for white Minnesotans. Nationally the gap in that month was 5.6 to 3.7. The state's labor market director called the pattern "very odd" and said the department cannot yet explain it. These rates cover all Black Minnesotans, come from surveys, and can be revised. An Urban League Twin Cities leader points to hiring bias and limited professional networks; the Metropolitan Economic Development Association points to interest rates and tariffs squeezing Black-owned firms ([Spokesman-Recorder, Oct. 20, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/10/20/black-unemployment-minnesota-2026/)).

**Where people work.** No occupation table separates U.S.-born Black workers. For everyone who lists Black as their only race, the 2020 to 2024 survey shows 32.3 percent in management, business, science and arts jobs, 25.1 percent in service jobs, 21.3 percent in production, transportation and material moving, 17.8 percent in sales and office jobs and 3.3 percent in construction and maintenance. For all Minnesota workers, the service share is 14.7 percent and production and transportation 13.4 percent ([Census Reporter, table C24010B](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=C24010B&geo_ids=04000US27); [table C24010](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/acs2024_5yr?table_ids=C24010&geo_ids=04000US27)). A December 2024 column says about 1 in 10 STEM workers are Black, without naming a source, and describes hospitality as a common but lower-paid field ([Spokesman-Recorder, Dec. 12, 2024](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2024/12/12/employment-disparities-minnesota/)).

**Entrepreneurs.** The 38th Street and Fourth Avenue district had more than twenty Black-owned businesses from the 1930s to the 1970s, and only the Spokesman-Recorder remains from that era ([MNopedia, Southside](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/southside-african-american-community-minneapolis)). Today Ward 5 in North Minneapolis is described by the city as having one of the largest concentrations of Black-owned businesses ([City of Minneapolis, July 2026](https://www.minneapolismn.gov/news/2026/july/bbw-2026/)). The Northside Economic Opportunity Network, at 1007 West Broadway, reports that in 2025 it served 2,072 entrepreneurs, disbursed $2.8 million in small business loans and helped launch 137 businesses ([NEON, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.neon-mn.org/)). The Metropolitan Economic Development Association has worked with entrepreneurs of color since 1971 ([MEDA, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://meda.net/)).

## 4. Businesses and commercial life

A separate list gives each business with its current status. It names Black-owned businesses without always being able to separate U.S.-born from African-born or Caribbean owners, and the sources do not always say. What stands out:

**Business districts.** West Broadway and Plymouth Avenue in North Minneapolis; 38th Street and Fourth Avenue on the Southside; Selby Avenue and University Avenue in St. Paul's Rondo and Frogtown. The West Broadway Business and Area Coalition, led by Kristall Porter, talks publicly about displacement, insurance costs and commercial financing ([BuyBlack.org, May 27, 2026](https://www.buyblack.org/articles/west-broadway-s-future-empowering-north-minneapolis-black-community)). The Capri Theater dates to 1927 (same source).

**A list made by the community's own paper.** The Spokesman-Recorder and Sister Spokesman named 50 finalists for 2026, by nomination count, in food, hair and nails, wellness, retail and professional services, confirming each as Black-owned with a website and physical location. Winners are to be announced Nov. 7, 2026 ([Spokesman-Recorder, July 17, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/07/17/best-of-black-business-2026-finalists/)). Many finalists' own pages show them operating in 2026. Camden Social, Alfonso and Kathryn Mayfield's restaurant at 4601 Lyndale Avenue North, takes reservations ([Camden Social](https://thecamdensocial.com/); [KSTP](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/camden-social-aiming-for-change-in-north-minneapolis/)). Black Garnet Books on University Avenue hosted a University of Minnesota Black History Month event on Feb. 3, 2026 ([University of Minnesota](https://diversity.umn.edu/news-events/black-history-month-events-university-minnesota)). Strive, a downtown Minneapolis bookstore and publisher founded by Mary Taris in 2018, posts weekday hours ([Strive](https://strivepubandco.com/)), and Lutunji's Palate, a bakery-cafe and social enterprise in Elliot Park, books holiday parties ([Lutunji's Palate](https://www.lutunjispalate.com/)). Kente Circle offers family and couples therapy on East 38th Street ([Kente Circle](https://www.kentecircle.com/)), and The Curl District is a natural hair salon in St. Paul ([The Curl District](https://thecurldistrict.com/)).

**Banking.** First Independence Bank, Black-owned and founded in Detroit in 1970, became the first Black-owned full-service bank in the Twin Cities in 2022. It opened at 627 University Avenue West on June 24, 2026, and keeps a branch at 2217 East Lake Street ([Spokesman-Recorder, June 29, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/06/29/first-independence-bank-st-paul-university-avenue/)).

**What has closed.** Breaking Bread Cafe, still found on many lists of Black-owned places, is marked closed by its parent nonprofit, Appetite for Change ([Appetite for Change](https://www.afcmn.org/breaking-bread)). Readers should check any older list before relying on it.

**What is being built.** Dreamland on 38th, an $8 million Black-led building at 3800 Third Avenue South, broke ground Oct. 10, 2026, and is planned to finish in 2027 ([Spokesman-Recorder, Oct. 16, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/10/16/dreamland-on-38th-groundbreaking/)). Its name echoes Dreamland Café, which opened nearby in 1939 ([MNopedia, Southside](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/southside-african-american-community-minneapolis)). In Brooklyn Center, a Black Business Village plan on eight acres has a preliminary agreement but no guarantee of construction ([Hoodline, July 20, 2026](https://hoodline.com/2026/07/brooklyn-center-s-8-acre-lot-poised-to-become-black-business-village-hub/)).

**Support groups and events.** The Minnesota Black Chamber of Commerce, led since early 2026 by Yolanda Pierson, held its gala on Aug. 8, 2026 ([Spokesman-Recorder, Aug. 13, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/08/13/minnesota-black-business-hall-of-fame-inaugural-class/)). Minneapolis ran its sixth Black Business Week from July 27 to Aug. 2, 2026 ([City of Minneapolis](https://www.minneapolismn.gov/things-to-do/events/2026-events/july/black-business-week/)), with events run by ROHO Collective, the Northside Economic Opportunity Network and others ([Spokesman-Recorder, July 23, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/07/23/minneapolis-black-business-week-2026-guide/)). 

**Professional firms.** Published lists of Black-owned law, accounting and insurance firms are scarce, and that is a gap in the sources, not proof the firms are absent. For lawyers, the Minnesota Association of Black Lawyers offers attorney referrals and says some members make up "the largest minority-controlled law firm in the Midwest," without naming it ([MABL](https://www.mabl.org/legacy); [MABL home](https://www.mabl.org/)). Its board for 2025 to 2026 is led by Jeremiah Allison, associate counsel for the Minnesota Vikings ([MABL board](https://www.mabl.org/team)).

**Media.** Beside the Spokesman-Recorder and Insight News, BLCK Press describes its mission as reconnecting news to Black culture, won a regional Emmy in 2024 and was publishing current stories in 2026 ([BLCK Press, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.blckpress.com/who-we-are); [BLCK Press home](https://www.blckpress.com/)).

## 5. Religion and spiritual life

**The Black church, from freedom seekers onward.** Pilgrim Baptist in St. Paul began in 1863 with people who escaped slavery in Missouri, led by Robert Hickman, and was formally established in 1866. It moved to Rondo in 1928 and stands on Central Avenue today, with Rev. Dr. Charles Gill as senior pastor. Members helped found the local Urban League and NAACP chapters and Hallie Q. Brown ([Spokesman-Recorder, Feb. 22, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/02/22/pilgrim-baptist-church-minnesota-history/); [MPR News, June 23, 2023](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2023/06/23/160-years-of-sanctuary-at-pilgrim-baptist-church)).

**Other congregations.** The Minnesota Historical Society's guide names St. James A.M.E., organized in 1863; St. Peter's A.M.E.; Bethesda Baptist, the oldest Black Baptist congregation in Minneapolis; Zion Baptist; Fellowship Missionary Baptist; Mount Olivet Baptist in Frogtown; and St. Philip's Episcopal ([MNHS library guide](https://libguides.mnhs.org/aafh/church)). Sabathani Baptist formed in 1962 at 3802 Third Avenue South ([MNopedia, Southside](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/southside-african-american-community-minneapolis)). Greater Friendship Missionary Baptist hosted a health care rally on Oct. 21, 2026, with a preliminary survey by the Black Church Power Project ([Spokesman-Recorder, Oct. 26, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/10/26/black-minnesotans-health-care-rally-faith-in-minnesotav/)). Pilgrim Baptist posts its Sunday schedule at 732 Central Avenue West ([Pilgrim Baptist, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://pilgrimbaptistchurch.org/)), and Mount Olivet Baptist, whose history pages begin in 1922, posts a Sunday 10:30 a.m. service with a livestream ([Mount Olivet, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.mtolivetbaptistchurch.org/)). Greater Friendship Missionary Baptist educators founded Friendship Academy of the Arts in 2000 (see section 10). This is a partial list, and current services were not confirmed for every congregation named.

**How faith shaped place.** The City of Minneapolis notes that Black congregations often did not own their buildings and so had to move when landlords changed plans; some were lost to fire. When Jewish families left North Minneapolis around the 1950s, some Black churches moved into former synagogues, such as First Church of God in Christ ([City of Minneapolis, Aug. 19, 2025](https://www.minneapolismn.gov/media/-www-content-assets/documents/Religion-Houses-of-Worship.pdf)).

**African American Muslims.** The city's summary dates some African American conversion to Islam to the early 1900s (same source). Masjid An-Nur, at Lyndale and 18th Avenue North, grew from Nation of Islam teachings and moved toward Sunni Islam under Warith Deen Mohammed. A 2021 profile described more than 300 worshippers, a majority-African American congregation with growing Somali, Egyptian, Oromo, Bangladeshi and Turkish membership, and an Al-Maa'uun program reaching 300 to 400 families ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, Nov. 1, 2021](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/imam-makram-el-amin-community-public-safety/)). Here African American and African-born Muslims worship under one roof. Its Al-Maa'uun program invited North Minneapolis residents to a meeting at the masjid on April 1, 2026, to help shape the future North Minneapolis Resilience Hub site ([Masjid An Nur, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.masjidannur.org/)).

**Other paths.** The Cultural Wellness Center teaches ancient wisdom, genealogy and rites of passage for people of African descent ([Cultural Wellness Center](https://www.culturalwellnesscenter.org/)). No Minnesota data was found on how many Black Minnesotans belong to each tradition or to none. As national background only: Pew Research Center's 2021 survey of more than 8,600 Black adults found 66 percent Protestant, 6 percent Catholic, 3 percent other Christian, 3 percent other faiths (Islam the most common) and 21 percent unaffiliated, with about 3 in 10 Black Gen Z and Millennial adults unaffiliated compared with 11 percent of Baby Boomers ([Pew Research Center, Feb. 16, 2021, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2021/02/16/faith-among-black-americans/)). Whether Minnesota follows that pattern is not known from these sources.

## 6. Celebrations and observances

Fuller details and dates are in the cultural calendar.

**Rondo Days.** First held in July 1983, MNopedia calls it the state's largest African American festival ([MNopedia, Rondo](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/rondo-neighborhood-st-paul)). In 2026 it ran Saturday, July 18, noon to 7 p.m., at that center, with more than 18 food trucks, 30-plus vendors, a seniors brunch in the morning and a youth zone, organized by Merina Neal and Gayle Smaller ([MPR News, July 15, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2026/07/15/rondo-days-returns-to-celebrate-the-history-of-st-pauls-iconic-neighborhood)). The Summit-University Planning Council's listing gives the same noon to 7 p.m. hours at the MLK Center, 271 Mackubin, names Rondo Avenue Inc. (established 1982) as presenter and Kwamé and Bahamadia as headliners, and prints the date as "July 18, 2926," an evident typo ([Summit-University Planning Council, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.summit-university.org/blog/rondo-days-2026)). WCCO reported in 2024 that the festival is held on the third Saturday in July, with a morning parade near St. Peter Claver Church ([WCCO, July 17, 2024](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/st-paul-rondo-days-mc-lyte-rakim/)). Ramsey County listed "Kumbayah: The Juneteenth Story" for its 30th anniversary at The O'Shaughnessy ([Ramsey County, June 15, 2026](https://www.ramseycountymn.gov/news/celebrating-juneteenth-ramsey-county)). **Duluth's Day of Remembrance.** Each June, the Clayton Jackson McGhie Memorial marks the lynchings. In 2026 it was observed June 14 and 15, with a gravesite gathering at Park Hill Cemetery ([Clayton Jackson McGhie Memorial](https://claytonjacksonmcghie.org/news)).

**Others.** Duluth held an MLK gathering, march and rally on Jan. 19, 2026 ([Clayton Jackson McGhie Memorial](https://claytonjacksonmcghie.org/news)). Hallie Q. Brown marks an elders' brunch at MLK weekend and an HQB Day recalling its first program on April 1, 1929 ([Hallie Q. Brown](https://www.hallieqbrown.org/)). February has been full of local Black History Month events; the 2024 list included a Sabathani Living History Museum unveiling with Sounds of Blackness ([Spokesman-Recorder, Feb. 16, 2024](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2024/02/16/black-history-month-events-in-the-twin-cities-feb-17-22/)). ## 7. Family, home and daily life

**Households.** Average household size is 2.24, and 40.8 percent live alone ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/african-american)). No Minnesota study of extended or multigenerational Black households was found to set beside this.

**Elders and memory.** Elder-centered events recur in community reports, from Hallie Q. Brown's Legends programs ([Hallie Q. Brown](https://www.hallieqbrown.org/)) to Duluth's Elders' Luncheon ([Clayton Jackson McGhie Memorial](https://claytonjacksonmcghie.org/)). At the 2026 Juneteenth brunch, Marquitta Ransom, 81, described the dust of I-94's construction, and said she and her husband stayed in their Central Avenue home for more than 50 years ([Spokesman-Recorder, June 22, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/06/22/blm-minnesota-juneteenth-brunch-2026/)).

**Food.** Places like Wendy's House of Soul, Angela's Soul Food Kitchen and Big L's Soul Food and Steakhouse keep soul food in the neighborhood ([Spokesman-Recorder, July 17, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/07/17/best-of-black-business-2026-finalists/)). Golden Thyme's New Orleans-inspired restaurant, owned and operated by the Rondo Community Land Trust, opened on Selby in May 2025 ([Yahoo News, May 16, 2025](https://www.yahoo.com/news/golden-thyme-returns-st-pauls-160220131.html)).

**Child welfare.** State figures for 2023 show African American children at 17 percent of Minnesota's children and 27 percent of child protection cases. The state's African American Family Preservation and Child Welfare Disproportionality Act, passed in 2024, requires family preservation efforts ([DCYF](https://dcyf.mn.gov/sites/default/files/2025-12/MAAFPCWDA%20one-pager_FINAL.pdf)). See section 13.

## 8. Arts, media, sports and culture

**Press and radio.** The Spokesman and the Recorder first appeared Aug. 10, 1934 ([Spokesman-Recorder, Aug. 4, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/08/04/msr-92nd-anniversary-cecil-newman-legacy-2/)). Newman's granddaughter Tracey Williams-Dillard is CEO and publisher, and the family says it has never missed a publication day. The paper's own account and a magazine profile date the merger of the two titles and her leadership to 2000 ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, Feb. 19, 2024](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/tracey-williams-dillard-on-the-living-legacy-of-the-minnesot)). In 2026 she said corporate leaders and wealthy people are not investing in the Black press ([Spokesman-Recorder](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/08/04/msr-92nd-anniversary-cecil-newman-legacy-2/)). The city's Heritage Preservation Commission voted 8-0 on Oct. 6, 2026, to recommend the building at 3744 Fourth Avenue South for the National Register, with the state review board considering it Nov. 10 ([Spokesman-Recorder, Oct. 16, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/10/16/msr-headquarters-national-register-historic-places/)). Gordon Parks got his start as a staff photographer there, the paper says ([Spokesman-Recorder](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/08/04/msr-92nd-anniversary-cecil-newman-legacy-2/)). Insight News publishes from 1815 Bryant Avenue North ([Insight News](https://www.insightnews.com/)). KMOJ began as a five-watt North Minneapolis station in 1976 and in 2019 reported more than 50 weekly hours of public affairs ([Ampers, Aug. 22, 2019](https://ampers.org/news/the-peoples-station-kmoj-fm-broadcasts-for-the-minnesota-african-american-community-and-beyond/)). Its own site could not be reached.

**Theater and music.** Penumbra, founded in 1976 by Lou Bellamy, is credited with launching August Wilson's career ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/african-americans-minnesota)). It is at 270 North Kent Street and now pairs productions with programs in racial identity and belonging ([Penumbra](https://penumbracenter.org/)). Prince, born in Minneapolis and raised on the North Side, is credited with the Minneapolis Sound ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/african-americans-minnesota)). The Way, a youth center founded by Syl Davis in 1966, was a hotspot for that sound in the 1970s and 1980s ([MNopedia, Near North](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/near-north-african-american-community-minneapolis)). Rondo native and Emmy winner PaviElle French appeared in 2026 coverage ([Spokesman-Recorder, Rondo stories](https://spokesman-recorder.com/tag/rondo/)).

**Dance, art and gathering places.** TU Dance, founded in St. Paul in 2004 by Toni Pierce-Sands and Uri Sands, draws on modern, ballet, African-based and urban vernacular movement; a February 2026 post announced a celebration of the late Pierce-Sands's life and legacy for April 24 and 25, and the company performs (RE)SONATE at the Southern Theater Dec. 4 to 6, 2026 ([TU Dance, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.tudance.org/)). Juxtaposition Arts runs a youth art and design center, gallery and shop with paid apprenticeships at 2007 Emerson Avenue North ([Juxtaposition Arts, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://juxtapositionarts.org/)). The Capri, at 2027 West Broadway, lists gallery hours and youth programs into November 2026 ([The Capri, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://thecapri.org/)). ROHO Collective, named for the Swahili word for soul and spirit, is an artist collective, gallery and cultural center for Black and Brown artists ([ROHO Collective, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://therohocollective.com/)). Mosaic on a Stick, a St. Paul mosaic studio founded in 2004 by artist Lori Greene, who describes herself as African American, Choctaw and white, calls itself Black, Indigenous and woman-owned ([Mosaic on a Stick, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.mosaiconastick.com/about)).

**Sports.** Dave Winfield's bronze statue joined others at Toni Stone Field in 2026 ([Spokesman-Recorder, June 1, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/06/01/david-winfield-statue-st-paul/)). The same plaza already honors Toni Stone. Jimmie Slemmons created the Minnesota Negro Open golf tournament in 1939 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/african-americans-minnesota)).

**Museums and archives.** The Minnesota African American Heritage Museum and Gallery opened in 2018 at Penn and Plymouth ([MNopedia, Near North](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/near-north-african-american-community-minneapolis)). It says it preserves and celebrates the history, art and culture of Black Minnesotans, offers free admission, and shows exhibitions such as Black Liberation, on dismantling racism in Minnesota from the 1800s to the 1960s ([MAAHMG, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://maahmg.org/)). The University of Minnesota's Archie Givens, Sr. Collection holds more than 10,000 books, magazines and pamphlets and the Penumbra archives ([University of Minnesota Libraries](https://www.lib.umn.edu/collections/special/givens)). Historian Yusef Mgeni, who died April 7, 2026, contributed oral history on Rondo ([Spokesman-Recorder, April 13, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/04/13/yusef-mgeni-obituary-black-minnesota-historian/)). Scholar Mahmoud El-Kati, who came to Rondo in the late 1950s, was honored with a street co-naming at Iglehart and Milton in September 2026 ([Spokesman-Recorder, Sept. 30, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/09/30/saint-paul-restores-street-co-naming-to-honor-elder-mahmoud-el-kati/)).

## 9. Civic and political life

**Firsts and institutions.** Black men won the vote in Minnesota through an 1868 constitutional amendment, two years before the Fifteenth Amendment. NAACP branches formed in St. Paul in 1913, Minneapolis in 1914 and Duluth in 1920. MNopedia's chronology records Sharon Sayles Belton as the first Black and first woman mayor of Minneapolis (1993), Neva Walker as the first Black woman in the Legislature (2001), Keith Ellison as the state's first Black member of Congress, and Wilhelmina Wright as its first Black woman federal judge (2016) ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/african-americans-minnesota)). Melvin Carter was St. Paul's first Black mayor, elected in 2017 and 2021 ([Governing, 2025](https://www.governing.com/politics/how-st-pauls-mayor-lost-a-safe-re-election-bid)). He lost to Kaohly Her in November 2025, and she took office Jan. 2, 2026 ([MPR News, Jan. 2, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/01/02/first-woman-hmong-american-kaohly-her-to-lead-st-paul-as-new-mayor)).

**Now.** Keith Ellison is Attorney General, and spoke at the Oct. 21, 2026 rally ([Spokesman-Recorder, Oct. 26, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/10/26/black-minnesotans-health-care-rally-faith-in-minnesotav/)). Senate President Bobby Joe Champion carried the family preservation and homeownership bills ([House Session Daily, May 15, 2024](https://www.house.mn.gov/sessiondaily/Story/18392); [CMAH handout](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/archive/minutes/senate/2025/jobs/20250324/Jobs_20250324_SF2423-CMAH-Black-Homeownership-Factsheet-handout.pdf)). Natalie Hudson retired Sept. 30, 2026, as the first Black person to lead the Supreme Court ([FOX 9](https://www.fox9.com/news/minnesota-supreme-court-chief-justice-natalie-hudson-retire-september); [WCCO](https://www.cbsnews.com/amp/minnesota/news/natalie-hudson-is-named-the-first-black-chief-justice-of-minnesota-supreme-court)). No complete current list of Black elected officials was found. The sources here do not state identity for several officeholders in the news, so this page names only those a source identifies.

**Organizations.** Urban League Twin Cities (founded 1925), Hallie Q. Brown, Phyllis Wheatley and the Aurora St. Anthony Neighborhood Development Corporation formed the Legacy Stabilization Collaborative in 2026, seeking $960,000 in a first phase, as philanthropy and federal funding shift ([MPR News, July 6, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2026/07/06/twin-cities-black-nonprofits-come-together-to-form-new-collaborative); [Insight News via The Hub](https://thehub.news/fraud-scrutiny-tests-trusted-black-institutions/?amp=1)). The African American Leadership Forum leads Northside Forward, a 10-year plan for North Minneapolis written with residents, with more than 3,700 proposed housing units and a $1.5 billion goal, in response to the planned Blue Line Extension ([Gensler](https://www.gensler.com/projects/northside-forward)). Faith in Minnesota, under JaNaé Bates Imari, is pressing for health care ([Spokesman-Recorder](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/10/26/black-minnesotans-health-care-rally-faith-in-minnesotav/)).

**Debates inside the community.** Several are public. When the Council on Black Minnesotans became the Council for Minnesotans of African Heritage, its new director said the name told African immigrants the council was theirs too, and called the divide counterproductive ([Spokesman-Recorder, Jan. 13, 2016](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2016/01/13/former-council-black-minnesotans-new-name-new-director/)). Readers commenting on that report argued that descendants of American slavery have a distinct history and claim. Rise and Remember, Agape and city officials disagree over who should develop People's Way at George Floyd Square; a city survey of 796 people favored Rise and Remember's idea at 58 percent to Agape's 36 percent, and the council rejected staff's recommendation of Agape on June 11, 2026 ([Spokesman-Recorder, Oct. 8, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/10/08/peoples-way-george-floyd-square-redevelopment/)). On Black institutions after the Feeding Our Future case, Al McFarlane asks whether fear of scrutiny has dampened good work, while speakers differ on whether jobs or the cash bail system come first ([Insight News via The Hub](https://thehub.news/fraud-scrutiny-tests-trusted-black-institutions/?amp=1)).

## 10. Education

**History.** St. Paul opened a segregated School for Colored Children in 1865, and an 1869 state law banned school segregation there ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/african-americans-minnesota)). Central High on the Southside closed in 1982 ([MNopedia, Southside](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/southside-african-american-community-minneapolis)), and a community member said that closing a high school kills half a community ([Streets.mn](https://streets.mn/2025/09/29/community-and-connection-a-history-of-35th-and-36th-streets/)).

**Now.** Benjamin E. Mays school in St. Paul's Rondo announced an Afrocentric magnet program for fall 2026, replacing its International Baccalaureate program. About 69 percent of students are African American. A board member spoke of past resistance, and the superintendent acknowledged that some will not understand the need ([Spokesman-Recorder, April 4, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/04/04/st-paul-benjamin-e-mays-afrocentric-program/)). The Northside Achievement Zone, led by Sondra Samuels, reports supporting scholars "from prenatal to profession," with 30 seniors in its Class of 2026 ([Northside Achievement Zone](https://northsideachievement.org/)).

**Higher education and scholars.** The University of Minnesota has a Department of African American and African Studies, whose pages name assistant professor Rahsaan Mahadeo among others ([University of Minnesota](https://cla.umn.edu/aaas)). Elder scholar Mahmoud El-Kati taught at Macalester, which named a lectureship for him ([Spokesman-Recorder](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/09/30/saint-paul-restores-street-co-naming-to-honor-elder-mahmoud-el-kati/)). Walker West Music Academy appears in 2026 Rondo coverage ([Spokesman-Recorder, Rondo stories](https://spokesman-recorder.com/tag/rondo/)). 

**Charter schools.** Two long-running charters grew from this community. Friendship Academy of the Arts was founded in 2000 by community leaders, among them educators from Greater Friendship Missionary Baptist Church, to address the opportunity gap for African American students; it opened with 37 students, now serves kindergarten through eighth grade at two South Minneapolis sites, reports 211 students in its directory listing and was named a National Blue Ribbon School in 2016 ([Minnesota Association of Charter Schools, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://mncharterschools.org/directory/location-detail.php?ID=380)). WCCO reported the school had been ordered to close in 2009 to 2010 for academic results before turning around ([WCCO, April 17, 2020](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/coronavirus-in-minnesota-south-minneapolis-charter-school-virtually-breaks-ground-on-new-facility/)). Harvest Best Academy, at 1300 Olson Memorial Highway, traces to schools founded by Eric and Ella Mahmoud; Best Academy began in 2008 as a single-gender school for African American boys, and the merged K-8 school lists 790 students, a school day 25 percent longer than typical and a Best East program for East African families ([Minnesota Association of Charter Schools, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://mncharterschools.org/directory/location-detail.php?ID=480)). In 2015 WCCO described the Harvest network as about 1,300 mostly African American students, with graduates returning to teach ([WCCO, Sept. 24, 2015](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/former-harvest-network-students-returning-to-teach/)).

**Test results.** No current state figures separate U.S.-born from African-born Black students, and the state's own report card could not be reached. The Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis, using state files for all Black students, reported that math proficiency fell from 30 percent in 2016 to 18 percent in 2021, and reading from 34 to 31 percent, against 44 and 53 percent for all students in 2021. About 101,000 Black students made up 12 percent of public school enrollment in 2022 ([Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis, 2022, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.minneapolisfed.org/-/media/assets/articles/2022/educational-outcomes-and-minnesotas-economy-black-and-african-american-student-snapshot/educational-outcomes-black-african-american.pdf)). KSTP reported that Black students improved slightly on the 2025 tests while remaining well below white students ([KSTP, 2025](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/minnesota-student-test-scores-steady-slight-progress-in-closing-achievement-gaps/)).

**Teachers.** Black Men Teach, based in St. Paul, recruits and supports Black men as elementary teachers. It cites a 2023 study finding that 0.5 percent of Minnesota's K-12 teachers are Black men ([Black Men Teach, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.blackmenteach.org/)).

## 11. Health and wellbeing, broadly

**A U.S.-born Black focus.** The Minnesota Department of Human Services produced a report centered on U.S.-born Black Minnesotans, "Building Racial Equity into the Walls of Minnesota Medicaid." Its author wrote in 2022 that about 41.5 percent of Black Minnesotans and about 64 percent of Black children rely on Medicaid, and that among enrollees of the same average age, asthma, heart failure and depression were about three times higher for U.S.-born Black enrollees than for those born abroad ([Spokesman-Recorder, Feb. 18, 2022](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2022/02/18/report-aims-to-close-gap-in-health-disparities-for-u-s-born-blacks-minnesotans/)). The article does not explain why, and that remains an open question.

**Maternal and infant health.** The state's 2017 to 2021 report gives 40.3 maternal deaths per 100,000 live births for Black parents, 2.3 times the statewide 17.9 ([Minnesota Department of Health, Jan. 12, 2026](https://www.health.state.mn.us/news/pressrel/2026/maternal011226.html)). Hennepin County reports that in 2019 only 64 percent of U.S.-born Black pregnant people received appropriate care during pregnancy ([Hennepin County](https://www.hennepincounty.gov/government/projects-initiatives/health/maternal-health-initiative)).

**Community-rooted care.** NorthPoint Health and Wellness has served North Minneapolis since 1968, at 2220 Plymouth Avenue North, with medical, dental, behavioral and eye care and human services ([NorthPoint](https://www.northpointhealth.org/)). Roots Community Birth Center, founded by Rebecca Polston, was described in 2016 as the only African American-owned birth center in the state ([Minnesota Daily, Oct. 31, 2016](https://mndaily.com/210611/city/stbirth)) and still practices at 1901 44th Avenue North ([Roots](https://www.rootsbirthcenter.com/)). The Cultural Wellness Center, founded in 1996 with Elder Atum Azzahir as founder and director, offers doula training, rites of passage and a Community Health Hub Kitchen ([Cultural Wellness Center](https://www.culturalwellnesscenter.org/); [Spokesman-Recorder](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/10/16/dreamland-on-38th-groundbreaking/)). Sabathani's programs cover maternal health, a Healing Circle for Black Mothers, smoking cessation and more ([Sabathani](https://www.sabathani.org/)).

**Coverage.** In the Compass profile, 5.6 percent lack health insurance ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/african-american)).

## 12. Disability

**Minnesota numbers, and what they can and cannot say.** The Compass profile reports 15.8 percent with a disability (give or take 1.1), and 14.1 percent of people under 65 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/african-american)). That comes from survey answers. It does not tell us who has a diagnosis, who calls themselves disabled, or who receives services.

**Who gets which services.** A study for the Department of Human Services by the University of Minnesota and Purdue, using Medicaid records from 2016 to 2019, reports on people in the aging and disability population identified as African American or Black, a category that also includes African-born people. It counted 22,208 people under 65 and 9,689 people 65 and older, 91 percent in the Twin Cities. Under 65, 29 percent used a disability waiver (7 percent the developmental disability waiver, 22 percent another), and 31 percent used personal care assistance without a waiver, against 46 percent and 6 percent for white people. Of those 65 and older, 42 percent used community-based Elderly Waiver services, the highest with Asian and Pacific Islander people, against 17 percent of white people ([DHS study, final report](https://developtoolmn.org/dhs/assets/racial-and-ethnic-comparison-report-quantatitive_tcm1053-663962.pdf)). Why these patterns exist is a real question. It may reflect choice, family care, who offers services nearby, or who is told about them. The numbers alone do not settle it. It would be useful to ask whether families are offered residential or waiver services on equal terms, and what personal care assistance makes possible or closes off.

**Autism.** The Minnesota autism monitoring project at the University of Minnesota, covering parts of Anoka, Hennepin and Ramsey counties, reports for 2022 that 1 in 30 non-Somali Black 8-year-olds were identified with autism (33.7 per 1,000), not significantly different from 1 in 33 white children. Among 4-year-olds, it was 1 in 30 non-Somali Black children and 1 in 64 white children, a ratio of 2.1 that is significant ([8-year-olds](https://addm.umn.edu/detailed-findings/eight-year); [4-year-olds](https://addm.umn.edu/detailed-findings/four-year)). The project states that it does not examine causes ([University of Minnesota, Sept. 26, 2025](https://addm.umn.edu/detailed-findings/press-briefing-2025-09-26)). "Non-Somali Black" still combines U.S.-born children with other African-origin children. Whether a higher identification at age 4 reflects earlier evaluation, different access or something else is not established here.

**School labels.** A 2020 report on special education equity, held by the Legislative Reference Library and using 2013 to 2016 data, says nearly 52 percent of Black students in special education were labeled emotionally and behaviorally disordered, and that Black students accounted for 46.5 percent of 187 identified disproportionality cases. Its two figures for the Black share of special education, 17 percent and 12 percent, appear in the same passage and do not match ([Special Education Equity Communities of Practice, via Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2020/Other/200711.pdf)). This data is old and covers all Black students. It raises a question this community has long asked: who decides that a Black child's behavior is a disability rather than a response to a school? A label can open services and also narrow a child's path, and which it does here remains open.

**Perceptions of treatment.** A 2023 survey for the Minnesota Governor's Council on Developmental Disabilities reports that its sample of people of color with disabilities (185 people, with 38 percent identifying as Black, African American and another 17 percent as Black, East African) was more likely than white disabled respondents to report negative experiences in public settings and in financial dealings, and less likely to feel welcome in businesses. White disabled respondents, in the same survey, were the most likely to report unfair treatment overall. Results were not reported for Black respondents alone ([GCDD, Sept. 2023](https://www.mn.gov/web/prod/static/mnddc/live/extra/customer-research/2023-GCDD-Intersectionality-Study-Research-Report.pdf)).

**What is produced by history.** This page does not attribute impairment to a people. Still, evidence here invites questions: what role did housing, freeways, job access and care access play in the disabling conditions recorded in Medicaid data in section 11? Sources here document the disparities and do not prove the causes.

**People and organizations.** No Minnesota organization was found that says it is led by and for Black disabled people. In Deaf life, Gov. Tim Walz's April 2024 Deaf History Month proclamation names the St. Paul/Minneapolis Black Deaf Advocates among organizations serving the Deaf community, and honors Blanche Wilkins Williams (1876 to 1936) as the first African American woman to graduate from the Minnesota State Academy for the Deaf ([Office of the Governor, April 3, 2024, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://mn.gov/governor/assets/04.01.24%20Deaf%20History%20Month_SIgned_tcm1055-620392.pdf)). No current page of the chapter's own was found. The Sabathani, NorthPoint and Hallie Q. Brown programs are general services. The child welfare law requires disability data to be reported ([DCYF](https://dcyf.mn.gov/sites/default/files/2025-12/MAAFPCWDA%20one-pager_FINAL.pdf)), and that data may become useful.

## 13. What is changing now

**Jobs.** Black unemployment reached 10.5 percent in August 2026, which the state cannot yet explain ([Spokesman-Recorder, Oct. 20, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/10/20/black-unemployment-minnesota-2026/)).

**Enforcement spillover.** Minneapolis funded part of Black Business Week through a resiliency fund for small businesses affected by Operation Metro Surge ([City of Minneapolis, July 2026](https://www.minneapolismn.gov/news/2026/july/bbw-2026/)). Northside Achievement Zone leaders wrote about fear among families near schools and bus routes ([Northside Achievement Zone](https://northsideachievement.org/)). Those reports do not separate U.S.-born families from immigrant families.

**George Floyd Square.** Phase 1 street work was ending, with streets expected to reopen by Oct. 9, 2026, and the city plans to recommend a developer by year's end, as noted above ([Spokesman-Recorder, Oct. 8, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/10/08/peoples-way-george-floyd-square-redevelopment/)).

**Rondo and I-94.** The Legislature gave ReConnect Rondo $6.2 million in 2021, with $2 million more federally. A 2025 House bill would have barred state spending on the land bridge; supporters cited $250 million in cost and opponents more than $400 million, and it was laid over ([House Session Daily, March 3, 2025](https://www.house.mn.gov/sessiondaily/Story/18544)). No 2026 report on its current status was found.

**Child welfare.** Lawmakers passed $15 million for the Act in May 2026, as counties questioned their readiness for the January 2027 statewide start ([Sahan Journal, May 20, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/?p=83857); [DCYF](https://dcyf.mn.gov/sites/default/files/2025-12/MAAFPCWDA%20one-pager_FINAL.pdf)). On Dec. 3, 2025, Hennepin County District Judge Matthew Frank ruled the law's phase-in unconstitutional. Hennepin and Ramsey counties had begun applying it to 30 percent of eligible cases, and he wrote that the phase-in "discriminates on the basis of race and the randomness of geography and timing." Unless lawmakers wrote a new phase-in, the law would take effect for all eligible children on Jan. 1, 2027. Its author, Rep. Esther Agbaje, said there were plans to update it; Ramsey County said it was not changing course at that time ([The Imprint via Sahan Journal, Dec. 12, 2025, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/news-partners/rollout-of-landmark-minnesota-child-welfare-reform-ruled-unconstitutional/)). University of Minnesota law professor Jill Hasday told the Star Tribune she found the opinion legally sound ([University of Minnesota Law School, Dec. 13, 2025](https://law.umn.edu/news/2025-12-13-prof-jill-hasday-quoted-star-tribune-about-ruling-against-minnesota-child)). Whether the ruling was appealed was not found.

**Justice.** Families of people killed by police rallied at the Hennepin County Government Center in October 2026, and advocates issued a report card on Minneapolis police accountability ([Spokesman-Recorder, local news, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/category/news-2/local/)). The race for Hennepin County Attorney between Rep. Cedrick Frazier and Anders Folk was to be decided on Nov. 3, 2026 ([Spokesman-Recorder, Oct. 19, 2026](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/10/19/hennepin-county-attorney-debate-frazier-folk/)).

**Generations.** Elders are passing. Yusef Mgeni died in April 2026, and KMOJ voice Chaz Millionaire died in 2026 ([Spokesman-Recorder, local news, page 2](https://spokesman-recorder.com/category/news-2/local/page/2/)). New institutions, from Dreamland on 38th to First Independence on University Avenue, are taking shape on the same streets.

## 14. Strengths and contributions

One of Minnesota's oldest Black churches began with people who freed themselves and came north by steamboat ([Spokesman-Recorder](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2026/02/22/pilgrim-baptist-church-minnesota-history/)). Black Minnesotans won the vote here early, won equal accommodations law in 1885 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/african-americans-minnesota)), and pressed for the anti-lynching law of 1921 ([MNopedia, Duluth](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/duluth-lynchings)). Institutions founded under segregation are still working: Pilgrim Baptist, Hallie Q. Brown, Phyllis Wheatley, Sabathani, NorthPoint and the Spokesman-Recorder, which has run for 92 years in one family. The four groups in the Legacy collaborative count over 300 years of combined experience ([MPR News](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2026/07/06/twin-cities-black-nonprofits-come-together-to-form-new-collaborative)). The state and the world know Prince, August Wilson's St. Paul years, Alan Page, Dave Winfield and Toni Stone. Rondo's organizers created a plaza and a festival so that a neighborhood the freeway cut in half would not be forgotten. That work of remembering runs from Rondo to Duluth.

## What could not be established

- **U.S.-born Black people as a clean group.** No source separates descendants of American slavery from all other U.S.-born Black people. - **Wealth.** No Minnesota count of high-wealth Black families was found. The only Minnesota net worth figure found for Black households ($0) does not state its year or method in the passage available.
- **Faith shares.** No Minnesota figures for Black membership by denomination, or for Black Minnesotans with no religion; only national survey data was found.
- **Local observances.** No current Minnesota source for a Watch Night service, Malcolm X's birthday, April 4 or August 28 events.
- **Disability organizations.** No Minnesota organization led by and for Black disabled people was found. The St. Paul/Minneapolis Black Deaf Advocates is named in a 2024 state proclamation, but no current page of its own was found. The Minnesota Reformer's report on Minneapolis schools and Black students with disabilities could not be reached.
- **Schools.** No current state test, graduation or special education figures for Black students could be reached from the state's own report card; the test figures here run only through 2021, plus a 2025 summary without numbers. None separate U.S.-born from African-born students.
- **Professional firms.** Few Black-owned law, accounting or insurance firms are named in reachable sources, and no current membership directory for the Minnesota Black Chamber of Commerce was available. Many businesses on the business list rest only on the Spokesman-Recorder's July 2026 finalist list, because their own pages could not be found or reached.
