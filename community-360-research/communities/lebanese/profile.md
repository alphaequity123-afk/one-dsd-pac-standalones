# Lebanese in Minnesota

## 1. People and place

**How many.** Minnesota Compass, which reports Census Bureau survey data on people who name Lebanese ancestry, gives two counts. Its single-year figure for 2024 is 10,066 people, give or take 3,576. Its five-year sample for 2020 to 2024, which it uses for every other number on the profile, covers 7,232 people, give or take 1,225. Compass explains that in 2024 the Census Bureau began collecting ancestry in a way that reports it more accurately for people who are also counted as Black or White, and it calls the 2024 figure the Bureau's most accurate estimate of the community's true size ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/lebanese)). The margins are wide because the group is small and the survey is a sample. A national coalition led by the Arab American Institute Foundation argues that the ancestry question undercounts Arab Americans for several reasons, including how the question is placed, how much later generations marry outside the group, and distrust of government surveys among recent immigrants ([Yalla Count MENA In!](https://yallacountmenain.org/states/minnesota-3nmg2)). The same page, using 2013 to 2017 data, says Lebanese and Somali roots make up the largest parts of the state's Arab American population.

**Where.** In the 2020 to 2024 data, 76.7 percent live in the Twin Cities region and 23.3 percent in Greater Minnesota, each give or take 5.6 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/lebanese)). Three places have the longest Lebanese history:

- **Northeast Minneapolis.** Early settlers gathered around Main and Marshall streets. The Northeaster counts 30 newcomers living above Michael Henne's store at 123 Main Street in 1895 and 104 "Syrian" families in Northeast by 1918. The pastor of St. Maron told the paper that most who settled there were Maronite Catholics from the Batroun district ([Northeaster, Oct. 21, 2021](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/)). St. Maron Maronite Catholic Church still stands on University Avenue NE.
- **St. Paul's West Side.** University of Minnesota researchers write that Syrian and Lebanese newcomers of the late 1800s were as poor as their Jewish neighbors and found cheap housing on the river flats ([Houses of Worship, University of Minnesota](https://housesofworship.umn.edu/research-items/west-side-flats-st-paul)). MNopedia describes the West Side Flats as a poor, often flooded immigrant neighborhood that the city cleared between 1961 and 1963 for the Riverview Industrial Park ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/west-side-flats-st-paul)). Both West Side parishes later moved south, to West St. Paul and Mendota Heights (section 5).
- **Mankato.** The Blue Earth County Historical Society dates the first Lebanese arrivals to about 1890, from Zahle and Ferzol in the Bekaa Valley. Its 1910 city directory count lists ten Lebanese families, first on North Front Street and later in the Tinkomville area around James Avenue ([Blue Earth County Historical Society, 2021](https://blueearthcountyhistory.com/2021/08/04/lebanese-immigration-to-mankato/)). A 1958 study of how Lebanese families integrated in Mankato is held at Minnesota State University, Mankato ([MSU Mankato archives](https://archivesspace.lib.mnsu.edu/public/repositories/4/resources/618)).

Brooklyn Park, Fridley, Rosemount, St. Louis Park and Mendota Heights also come up in the sources below as homes and business addresses of Lebanese Minnesotans, but no source gives counts for them.

**How people came.** Sources describe waves, not a single arrival.

- *Late 1800s to about 1920.* People left Mount Lebanon, then part of the Ottoman Empire. Many were recorded as "Syrian" at the time, a point the University of Minnesota's archives guide makes explicitly ([UMN Libraries](https://libguides.umn.edu/ASCsyrians)). The Blue Earth County Historical Society names the Great Famine of Mount Lebanon, wartime politics, locusts and border wars among the push factors of 1915 to 1918 ([Blue Earth County Historical Society](https://blueearthcountyhistory.com/2021/08/04/lebanese-immigration-to-mankato/)). Theater director Kathryn Haddad told MPR News that the famine "killed more people in Lebanon than anywhere else" and drove migration in waves ([MPR News, Nov. 19, 2018](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2018/11/19/theater-director-sets-out-to-gather-tell-stories-of-the-labanese-who-came-to-minnesota)).
- *Before and after World War II.* The Northeaster describes a second wave in the 1930s, including Emily Awaijane, who arrived in 1936 and later founded Emily's Lebanese Deli ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/)). Mankato's mayor was born in the Bekaa Valley ([Blue Earth County Historical Society](https://blueearthcountyhistory.com/2021/08/04/lebanese-immigration-to-mankato/)); she says Mankato is where her family "built our future" ([Mankato Free Press, Aug. 7, 2026](https://www.mankatofreepress.com/news/local_news/mankato-mayor-candidates-address-questions-ahead-of-primary-election/article_7d757752-b589-4311-892b-646cd708193a.html)).
- *Civil war years, 1975 to about 1991.* The Northeaster calls this one of the largest waves ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/)). St. Maron's longtime pastor, Sharbel Maroun, was born in 1962 and came to the United States with his family in 1983, after the war, according to the Vatican's biography ([Holy See Press Office, Aug. 28, 2026](https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/en/bollettino/pubblico/2026/08/28/260828c.html)). In 2024 he told WCCO that more than half of his parishioners came from Lebanon to escape political unrest and economic crisis ([CBS Minnesota, Sept. 23, 2024](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/minneapolis-church-lebanese-festival/)). A St. Maron parishioner told The Catholic Spirit he fought to defend his hometown of Ijdabra as a teenager and moved to the United States in 1980 ([The Catholic Spirit, March 23, 2017](https://www.thecatholicspirit.com/news/local-news/st-maron-parish-raises-money-cross-lebanon/)).
- *Recent years.* In the 2020 to 2024 data, 9.2 percent of Lebanese Minnesotans were born abroad, give or take 3.9 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/lebanese)). That is a small share, which fits a community that has been here for five or more generations, but it is not zero.

**Generations and languages.** Among Lebanese Minnesotans 5 and older, 86.1 percent speak only English at home and 13.9 percent speak another language, give or take 6.2 points. Compass suppresses the share who speak English less than "very well" because the sample is too small ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/lebanese)). The survey does not say which other language. Arabic, French and English all appear in the sources: Holy Family's pastor lists Arabic, French, English and Italian ([Holy Family Maronite Church](https://www.holyfamilymaronitechurch.org/pastor.html)), and the Maronite liturgy uses Syriac, which the parish calls "the sister of the Aramaic language" ([Holy Family Maronite Church, history](https://www.holyfamilymaronitechurch.org/history.html)). Age is spread fairly evenly: 24.2 percent are 17 or younger and 17.0 percent are 65 or older. Women are 57.0 percent of the count ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/lebanese)).

## 2. All walks of life

All figures below are from the Compass 2020 to 2024 profile in 2024 dollars, unless noted. The margins are large, so read them as rough ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/lebanese)).

- **Well-off households.** About 35.9 percent of households take in $100,000 or more, give or take 8.8 points. Owner-occupied homes are 68.6 percent of households.
- **Professionals, owners and public figures.** Half of adults 25 and older, 50.9 percent, hold a bachelor's degree or more, and 16.9 percent hold a graduate or professional degree. Public careers in the sources include Mankato's mayor, a restaurateur born in the Bekaa Valley who has run her family's restaurant and catering business since 1984 ([KEYC, May 22, 2026](https://www.keyc.com/2026/05/22/candidates-step-forward-mankatos-2026-mayoral-election/)); the Mocol family, whose corner grocery on North Broad Street in Mankato marked 100 years in 2017 and whose Herb Mocol was mayor ([Mankato Free Press, 2017](https://www.mankatofreepress.com/news/at-100-mocols-corner-grocery-still-thriving/article_00f2dc06-47c2-11e7-a0e6-f3425abf920b.html)); the Jacob family of Northeast, whose brothers ran Jacob's 101 and other bars and restaurants ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/)); and an engineering firm owner among St. Maron's parishioners ([The Catholic Spirit, 2017](https://www.thecatholicspirit.com/news/local-news/st-maron-parish-raises-money-cross-lebanon/)). George Latimer, St. Paul's longest-serving mayor (1976 to 1990), who died in 2024, described his mother in a 1997 interview as "Catholic Lebanese growing up in a merchant family" ([MPR News archive, April 7, 1997](https://archive.mpr.org/stories/1997/04/07/voices-of-minnesota-george-latimer-part-1); [KSTP, 2024](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/top-news/george-latimer-visionary-st-paul-mayor-who-reshaped-citys-core-dies-at-89/)).
- **Middle-income and working families.** Median household income is $72,873, give or take $21,005. Among adults 18 to 64, 82.5 percent are employed: 88.3 percent of men and 78.6 percent of women. Households are small, averaging 2.12 people, and 42.8 percent of households are one person living alone.
- **People who are struggling.** About 28.1 percent of households take in less than $35,000, give or take 9.9 points. Of all people, 8.8 percent are below the poverty line, give or take 5.4, and another 12.3 percent are between the line and twice the line. Among adults 18 to 64, 13.1 percent are in poverty, give or take 8.3. Housing costs are a strain: 46.8 percent of households spend too much of their income on housing, including 40.6 percent of owners and 60.5 percent of renters. The poverty figure for 18- to 24-year-olds, 50.2 percent, has a margin of 24.8 points and may reflect students living on their own; Compass does not say.

**Hardship in history.** The sources also record hard years. The Northeaster recounts a family "on the bread line during the Depression," children sent to a children's home after a mother's death, and peddling trips by women who sold fabric door to door as far as Hibbing, sometimes for a month ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/)). In Mankato's 1952 flood, his son recalled, "the poor and the frail" called Herb Mocol because he had trucks and would help ([Mankato Free Press, Sept. 29, 2005](https://www.mankatofreepress.com/news/local_news/mankato-politician-mocol-dies/article_008fce39-aac5-5bfd-ab9e-730f2fe629e4.html)).

## 3. Work and the economy

**Peddling, groceries and trucking.** Early work followed a pattern seen in both cities. In Northeast Minneapolis, Michael Henne supplied goods on credit to peddlers, most often women, while men worked on the railroads, in factories and at the B.F. Nelson paper mill. A brother of one peddler began selling ice from a horse and wagon, the start of Jacobs Trucking ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/)). In Mankato, the Mocol family's grandfather arrived "with nothing but a skill at being a trader," sold goods from a wagon across the Upper Midwest and opened the North Broad Street store in 1917. The family also ran a wholesale business supplying many of Mankato's 48 corner stores in the 1940s ([Mankato Free Press, 2017](https://www.mankatofreepress.com/news/at-100-mocols-corner-grocery-still-thriving/article_00f2dc06-47c2-11e7-a0e6-f3425abf920b.html)). The county historical society names other Lebanese and Syrian grocery families in Mankato, among them Shama, Isen, Shalhub and Farho ([Blue Earth County Historical Society, June 30, 2021](https://blueearthcountyhistory.com/2021/06/30/mocol-family-grocery-store/)). Naiff Abdo's store on James Avenue served the neighborhood for almost 70 years before closing in 1980 ([Blue Earth County Historical Society](https://blueearthcountyhistory.com/2021/08/04/lebanese-immigration-to-mankato/)).

**Public jobs.** The Northeaster notes that one early immigrant worked 23 years for the Minneapolis Water Works and that "many worked for the City of Minneapolis" in his family, including a son who retired from the police department ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/)).

**Food and hospitality.** Restaurants, delis and catering run through the record from Emily's Lebanese Deli in 1973 to Beirut Lebanese Street Food in 2025 (section 4). In Mankato, the Massad family opened Meray's in 1984 and now runs Massad's Grille, Najwa's Catering and Olives ([Massad's Grille](https://massadsgrille.com/)).

**Today.** Compass shows high employment among working-age adults (82.5 percent) and high education levels (section 2). It does not break out industries or occupations for Lebanese Minnesotans, and no Minnesota source was found that does. No Minnesota source documents a Lebanese labor or union history.

## 4. Businesses and commercial life

Lebanese Minnesota does not have a commercial district of its own today. Its businesses are spread out, and many are food businesses. The full list, with the evidence for each, is in businesses.json. Highlights:

- **Emily's Lebanese Deli,** 641 University Ave. NE, Minneapolis. Opened in 1973 by Emily Awaijane near St. Maron. Her son told the Northeaster the family expected Lebanese customers, but most were Polish and Ukrainian neighbors ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/)). A 2025 ranking of Minneapolis Middle Eastern restaurants still lists it at that address ([Stacker, Aug. 22, 2025](https://stacker.com/stories/minnesota/minneapolis/highest-rated-middle-eastern-restaurants-minneapolis-diners)).
- **Zakia Deli,** 2412 Kennedy St. NE, Minneapolis. Opened in 2007 by David Maalouf and named for his mother, whose recipes anchor a Lebanese, Greek and American menu. Racket reported that Zakia and Assaad Maalouf began raising their seven children in Lebanon ([Racket, March 19, 2025](https://racketmn.com/best-budget-bites-9-49-grilled-chicken-sandwich-from-zakia-deli)).
- **Beirut Lebanese Street Food & Catering,** 14873 Robert Trail S., Rosemount. Its site says that "after 41 years in West Saint Paul," John and Madelaine Khoury opened this fast-casual spot and catering business ([Beirut Lebanese](https://beirutlebmn.com/)). A February 2025 roundup reported the opening by the second-generation owners of West St. Paul's former Beirut Restaurant ([Minnesota restaurant openings and closings, February 2025, via Yahoo News](https://www.yahoo.com/news/minnesota-restaurant-openings-closings-february-174006345.html)). The restaurant is a corporate sponsor of the St. George festival, along with a business listed as Byblos Lebanese Grill ([St. George festival sponsors](https://www.mideastfest.com/our-sponsors/)).
- **The Massad businesses in Mankato:** Massad's Grille in River Hills Mall, home of the family's chicken shawarma sandwich and a bottled sauce, plus Najwa's Catering ([Massad's Grille](https://massadsgrille.com/); [Najwa's Catering](https://www.najwascatering.com/)).
- **Sweet Lou's Craft Sausage and Butchery,** 6318 Minnetonka Blvd., St. Louis Park. Owner Louisa Farhat, whose extended family lives in Lebanon, told MPR News in 2024 that she raised money for the Lebanese Red Cross and had once made the Lebanese sausage na'anik for a fundraiser ([MPR News, Oct. 30, 2024](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2024/10/30/minneapolis-chef-butcher-raises-money-for-lebanese-red-cross); [Sweet Lou's](https://www.sweetlousmeats.com/)).
- **Mocol's Supermarket,** North Broad Street, Mankato. Described in 2017 as the city's last neighborhood grocery, run by four Mocol brothers ([Mankato Free Press, 2017](https://www.mankatofreepress.com/news/at-100-mocols-corner-grocery-still-thriving/article_00f2dc06-47c2-11e7-a0e6-f3425abf920b.html)). No current listing was found.
- **Parish halls as businesses.** St. Maron's Cedars Hall rents a 5,600-square-foot hall for weddings and events and uses Northeast caterers ([Northeaster, Dec. 2, 2022](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/got-an-event-northeast-has-a-place-for-it/)).

Grocery shopping for Lebanese staples is not tied to Lebanese-owned stores in the sources. The Northeaster lists Holy Land in Northeast Minneapolis as a Middle Eastern grocery serving Syrian, Egyptian, Palestinian, Lebanese, Afghan and Iranian customers, among others ([Northeaster, June 3, 2021](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/ethnic-grocers-evoke-feelings-of-home/)).

No Lebanese chamber of commerce, Lebanese professional association, law or accounting firm identified as Lebanese, Lebanese-language newspaper or radio program in Minnesota was found. The Northeaster, a neighborhood paper, is the outlet that has covered Lebanese Northeast most closely.

## 5. Religion and spiritual life

**Christian parishes with Lebanese roots.** Three Twin Cities churches were founded by Lebanese and Syrian immigrants and remain active:

- **St. Maron Maronite Catholic Church,** 600 University Ave. NE, Minneapolis (some listings give 602). The first Maronite Mass in Minneapolis was said on February 9, 1903, the feast of St. Maron, at 311 Main Street. The parish bought an abandoned church at 625 Main Street in 1919, bought the Everett school at Sixth and University by 1939, and built its present church within ten years ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/)). It belongs to the Maronite Eparchy of Our Lady of Lebanon, within the territory of the Archdiocese of Saint Paul and Minneapolis ([GCatholic](https://gcatholic.org/churches/usa-08/17077.htm)). The Catholic Spirit reported in 2017 that 70 percent of the parish has Lebanese ancestry ([The Catholic Spirit, 2017](https://www.thecatholicspirit.com/news/local-news/st-maron-parish-raises-money-cross-lebanon/)). In 2021 its pastor described it as a "mosaic" that also includes Melkite and other Christians from Syria, Jordan, Iran and the Holy Land ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/)), and The Catholic Spirit reported 10 to 20 Syrian households there in 2013 ([The Catholic Spirit, 2013](https://www.thecatholicspirit.com/news/local-news/local-syrians-find-support-st-maron/)). The parish website showed only a "New Site" placeholder on Oct. 31, 2026 ([stmaron.com](https://www.stmaron.com/)).
- **Holy Family Maronite Catholic Church,** 1960 Lexington Ave. S., Mendota Heights. Early Maronites in St. Paul worshiped at St. Michael's on the West Side for about 15 years, then bought a Protestant church in 1918. They dedicated a new church at 203 E. Robie St. in 1951, built of brick trimmed with Mankato stone, and moved to Mendota Heights in July 2009 ([Holy Family, history](https://www.holyfamilymaronitechurch.org/history.html)). It holds Mass Saturday at 4 p.m. and Sunday at 10 a.m., streams weekend liturgies on Facebook, and runs faith formation and a youth camp ([Holy Family](https://www.holyfamilymaronitechurch.org/)). Its pastor since February 2016, Father Emmanuel Nakhle-Ghorr, was born in Baabda, Lebanon ([Holy Family, pastor](https://www.holyfamilymaronitechurch.org/pastor.html)).
- **St. George Antiochian Orthodox Church,** 1250 Oakdale Ave., West St. Paul. Organized in 1913 on St. Paul's West Side as St. George Syrian Orthodox Church and dedicated in 1915. Services were first held in a home, and the parish says it was visited by Bishop Raphael Hawaweeny, now a saint. It bought land in West St. Paul in 1968, held its first service there in February 1974, and merged with St. Mary's Antiochian parish in 1998. It describes its members as descendants of the original Middle Eastern families, immigrant families from Lebanon, Syria, Jordan and Palestine, other Orthodox Christians and many converts ([St. George, Who We Are](https://www.mideastfest.com/who-we-are/)).

**Leadership news.** On Aug. 28, 2026, the Vatican announced that Pope Leo XIV had approved the election of Chorbishop Sharbel Maroun as Maronite archbishop of Damascus, Syria ([Holy See Press Office](https://press.vatican.va/content/salastampa/en/bollettino/pubblico/2026/08/28/260828c.html)). Archbishop Bernard Hebda wrote that Maroun had served St. Maron for more than 35 years and would be "deeply missed" in Northeast Minneapolis ([Archdiocese of Saint Paul and Minneapolis, Aug. 28, 2026](https://www.archspm.org/statement-regarding-bishop-elect-sharbel-maroun/)). The Maronite eparchy's own site also posted a statement ([Eparchy of Our Lady of Lebanon](https://www.eparchy.org/)). Who will lead St. Maron next was not found.

**Practice.** Maroun explained on a 2021 radio show that Maronite Lent starts on a Monday, "Ash Monday," and that Good Friday is called "Great Friday" ([The Catholic Spirit, March 18, 2021](https://www.thecatholicspirit.com/practicing-catholic/chorbishop-maroun-describes-maronite-churchs-lenten-easter-traditions/)). In 2015 relics of St. Sharbel, a Lebanese hermit, were displayed at both Maronite parishes ([The Catholic Spirit, Oct. 19, 2015](https://www.thecatholicspirit.com/news/local-news/relics-of-st-sharbel-at-maronite-churches-nov-4-7/)). St. Maron takes part in an annual Northeast Eucharistic procession in June, where in 2017 one prayer was read in Aramaic ([Northeaster, July 2, 2017](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/for-14-years-ne-churches-revive-traditional-procession/)). In Mankato, the Mocol family belonged to Saints Peter and Paul Catholic Church, a Latin-rite parish ([Blue Earth County Historical Society, 2021](https://blueearthcountyhistory.com/2021/06/30/mocol-family-grocery-store/)).

**Muslim, Druze and nonreligious Lebanese.** Lebanon's population includes Muslims and Druze, and the Northeaster notes that before the 1940s Lebanese "identified by their religious affiliation, Christian or Muslim" ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/)). In 2006 MPR News described a Brooklyn Park man as "a Christian, as are many Lebanese-Americans in Minnesota" ([MPR News, July 20, 2006](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2006/07/20/isrealreax)). No Minnesota mosque, Islamic center or Druze organization described as Lebanese was found, and no source estimates how many Lebanese Minnesotans are Muslim, Druze, or of no religion. The Census Bureau does not ask about religion. This is a gap in the record, not evidence that such families are absent.

## 6. Celebrations and observances

- **St. Maron Lebanese Festival.** Racket reports it began in 1990 to strengthen community bonds and change how people saw Lebanon at a time of war ([Racket, Sept. 15, 2022](https://racketmn.com/st-marons-lebanese-festival-northeast-minneapolis)). CBS called the September 2024 festival the 33rd ([CBS Minnesota, 2024](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/minneapolis-church-lebanese-festival/)). In 2025 it ran Sept. 13 and 14 with the Cedars Dabke Dance Group ([Northeaster listing](https://www.mynortheaster.com/event/st-maron-lebanese-festival-5/2025-09-13/)). In 2026 it ran Sept. 18 to 20, adding Friday bingo, with dabke by youth and adult groups and tours of what the parish calls the largest church mosaic in the Twin Cities ([Northeaster listing](https://www.mynortheaster.com/event/st-maron-lebanese-festival-6/2026-09-19/)). Volunteers told Racket that older women once cooked in the basement before the parish had a commercial kitchen in 1999, and that the work is now passing to a third generation that includes Syrian, Jordanian, Turkish, Iraqi and Palestinian volunteers ([Racket](https://racketmn.com/st-marons-lebanese-festival-northeast-minneapolis)).
- **St. George Middle Eastern Festival** in West St. Paul, the 16th annual, July 18 and 19, 2026 ([St. George Middle Eastern Festival](https://www.mideastfest.com/)). The 2026 program listed the St. George Dabke Dance Group, "Lebanese Singer John Khoury & Band," Lebanese wines, church tours, a Saturday Vespers service, a marketplace and camel rides ([festival highlights](https://www.mideastfest.com/festival-highlights/)). The menu describes kafta made with "special spices from Zahle, Lebanon," and manoushee baked in a traditional oven ([festival menu](https://www.mideastfest.com/our-food/)).
- **Holy Family Fall Festival** in Mendota Heights, Saturday, Sept. 12, 2026, with tabouli parsley and bread on the donation list ([Holy Family](https://www.holyfamilymaronitechurch.org/)).
- **Feast of St. Maron,** Feb. 9. A public holiday in Lebanon ([CalendarLabs](https://www.calendarlabs.com/holidays/lebanon/2026)) and the day of the first Maronite Mass in Minneapolis in 1903 ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/)). No 2026 Minnesota listing was found.
- **Lent and Easter.** Maronites begin Lent on Ash Monday ([The Catholic Spirit, 2021](https://www.thecatholicspirit.com/practicing-catholic/chorbishop-maroun-describes-maronite-churchs-lenten-easter-traditions/)). Lebanon's public calendar includes both Western and Orthodox Easter ([CalendarLabs](https://www.calendarlabs.com/holidays/lebanon/2026)); in Minnesota, the two Maronite parishes are Catholic and St. George is Orthodox. No 2026 Minnesota Easter listing was found for any of them.
- **Christmas,** Dec. 25.
- **Lebanese Independence Day,** Nov. 22, marking independence in 1943 ([CalendarLabs](https://www.calendarlabs.com/holidays/lebanon/2026)). No Minnesota celebration was found.
- **Beirut port explosion,** Aug. 4, 2020. Both Maronite parishes held Masses, adoration and a day of prayer and fasting that week ([The Catholic Spirit, Aug. 5, 2020](https://www.thecatholicspirit.com/news/local-news/mass-at-mendota-heights-parish-to-be-dedicated-to-victims-of-explosion-in-beirut/)). No yearly Minnesota remembrance was found.
- **Muslim holy days.** Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha, the Islamic New Year and Ashura are public holidays in Lebanon ([CalendarLabs](https://www.calendarlabs.com/holidays/lebanon/2026)), and Ramadan is the month of fasting that precedes Eid al-Fitr. No Minnesota celebration specific to Lebanese Muslims was found.

## 7. Family, home and daily life

**Households.** The average Lebanese household in Minnesota has 2.12 people. About 42.8 percent are one person living alone, 20.6 percent are married couples without children under 18, and 18.5 percent are married couples with children ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/lebanese)). These are small, older-skewing households compared with many immigrant communities. In 2020 to 2024, 92.6 percent lived in the same home as a year earlier.

**Family and migration.** The pastor of St. Maron told the Northeaster that during the famine years families sent children west so that "the family name would continue on," and that in the first 20 years "friends and cousins were following each other into Northeast" ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/)). Families from later waves still have relatives in Lebanon: a parishioner in 2017 had family in Ijdabra ([The Catholic Spirit, 2017](https://www.thecatholicspirit.com/news/local-news/st-maron-parish-raises-money-cross-lebanon/)), and Louisa Farhat said in 2024 that her relatives were "used to having to evacuate to the mountains" ([MPR News, 2024](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2024/10/30/minneapolis-chef-butcher-raises-money-for-lebanese-red-cross)).

**Names.** Ellis Island changed Paul Serafin's surname to Jacobs, the Northeaster reports, and Yousef Mahkoul became Joseph Mocol in Mankato ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/); [Blue Earth County Historical Society](https://blueearthcountyhistory.com/2021/08/04/lebanese-immigration-to-mankato/)). Changed or anglicized names are part of many family histories.

**Food.** Food is the thread in almost every source. Festival menus list manakish, lahmajeen, kibbeh, kafta, falafel, grape leaves, tabbouleh, hummus, baklava and Lebanese coffee ([Racket, 2022](https://racketmn.com/st-marons-lebanese-festival-northeast-minneapolis); [Northeaster listing, 2026](https://www.mynortheaster.com/event/st-maron-lebanese-festival-6/2026-09-19/)). In 1964, Helen George was photographed making Syrian flatbread for St. Paul's Festival of Nations ([MPR News, 2018](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2018/11/19/theater-director-sets-out-to-gather-tell-stories-of-the-labanese-who-came-to-minnesota)). St. George's bakers say their pastry traditions came from "the small Christian villages of Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, and Palestine," passed from mother to daughter; mamoul cookies are shaped to recall the sponge offered to Christ on the cross, and the parish has baked its round farani bread from the same recipe for over 100 years ([St. George festival pastries](https://www.mideastfest.com/sweet-treats/)). Holy Family hosts family-sponsored breakfasts, and its Ladies Society, Men's Club and "Merri-Nites" group meet monthly ([Holy Family](https://www.holyfamilymaronitechurch.org/); [Holy Family calendar](https://www.holyfamilymaronitechurch.org/calendar.html)).

**Childhood and youth.** Holy Family's youth camp for ages 9 to 17 ran Aug. 12 to 14, 2026, and included canoeing at Lebanon Hills ([Holy Family](https://www.holyfamilymaronitechurch.org/)). St. Maron's youth group dances dabke at the festival ([Racket](https://racketmn.com/st-marons-lebanese-festival-northeast-minneapolis)).

**Aging.** Seventeen percent are 65 or older ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/lebanese)). No Minnesota source describes elder care practices among Lebanese families.

## 8. Arts, media, sports and culture

- **New Arab American Theater Works,** led by Kathryn Haddad, makes theater and music by and about Southwest Asian, North African and Muslim Americans, and runs what it calls Minnesota's only Arabic percussion ensemble ([New Arab American Theater Works](https://www.newarabamericantheaterworks.org/)). In 2018 Haddad began gathering stories of Lebanese who came to Minnesota a century ago for a play, *The Hour of Separation*, drawing on records at the University of Minnesota ([MPR News, 2018](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2018/11/19/theater-director-sets-out-to-gather-tell-stories-of-the-labanese-who-came-to-minnesota)).
- **Mizna,** co-founded in 1999 by Haddad and Saleh Abudayyeh, publishes a literary journal and runs the Twin Cities Arab Film Festival, begun in 2003 ([Mizna](https://mizna.org/about/)). It serves the wider Arab and SWANA community, not Lebanese people alone.
- **Music and dabke.** The St. George Dabke Dance Group and a Lebanese singer and band performed at the 2026 St. George festival ([festival highlights](https://www.mideastfest.com/festival-highlights/)). The Cedars Dabke Dance Group performed at the 2025 St. Maron festival ([Northeaster listing](https://www.mynortheaster.com/event/st-maron-lebanese-festival-5/2025-09-13/)). One parishioner told the Northeaster the culture is "always about the food, and dancing the dabke" ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/)).
- **Archives.** The University of Minnesota's Immigration History Research Center Archives holds what it described in 2014 as one of the nation's most extensive Arab American collections, including a 1936 Festival of Nations map with a Syrian booth ([UMN Libraries, April 2014](https://libnews.umn.edu/2014/04/arab-american-print-material-on-display-at-the-immigration-history-research-center-archives/)). A national Lebanese diaspora archive at North Carolina State holds a 1950 letter from Sen. Hubert Humphrey about a Minnesota immigration case ([Nassour Archive of Lebanese Diaspora](https://lebanesestudies.omeka.chass.ncsu.edu/items/browse?search=Minnesota)). A 1978 book, *Cedars by the Mississippi: The Lebanese-Americans in the Twin Cities* by Viviane Doche, is listed among the Northeaster's sources ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/)).
- **Media.** No Lebanese Minnesota newspaper, radio show or website was found. The Northeaster and Sahan Journal have covered the community.
- **Sports.** No Minnesota athlete, team or league identified as Lebanese was found.

## 9. Civic and political life

**Mankato's Lebanese mayors.** Herb Mocol, son of a Lebanese immigrant grocer, led a successful petition drive in the late 1960s to create city wards because he thought too many council members came from the wealthier west end. He served four years on the council and was elected mayor in 1974, holding the job 12 years, according to his 2005 obituary in the Mankato Free Press, which also says he was proudest of winning federal money for flood control and was "an enthusiastic promoter of the Lebanese culture" ([Mankato Free Press, 2005](https://www.mankatofreepress.com/news/local_news/mankato-politician-mocol-dies/article_008fce39-aac5-5bfd-ab9e-730f2fe629e4.html)). The Blue Earth County Historical Society gives his years as 1975 to 1986 ([Blue Earth County Historical Society](https://blueearthcountyhistory.com/2021/08/04/lebanese-immigration-to-mankato/)). The current mayor, born in Lebanon, has held the office since her election in 2018 and beat Toby Leonard in 2018 and 2022 ([Mankato Free Press, June 8, 2026](https://www.mankatofreepress.com/news/local_news/race-to-watch-hamann-hopes-to-bring-conversation-to-city-council/article_167d04f5-3f33-4b85-a921-8e6e72a43b16.html)). She is seeking a third term in November 2026. In the August primary she received 2,378 votes (41.79 percent) to Julia Hamann's 2,267 (39.83 percent) ([Radio Mankato, Aug. 12, 2026](https://radiomankato.com/?p=437082)). The Free Press reported in May that she had "faced some backlash over her response to ICE's presence" in Mankato ([Mankato Free Press via CNHI, May 16, 2026](https://www.cnhi.com/rss_feed/massad-announces-bid-for-third-term/)). Asked by the Free Press about the city's response to immigration agents and about license-plate cameras, the mayor gave a single answer, saying the matters were before the city and that "my record, my public comments and my actions as mayor" show her approach ([Mankato Free Press, Aug. 7, 2026](https://www.mankatofreepress.com/news/local_news/mankato-mayor-candidates-address-questions-ahead-of-primary-election/article_7d757752-b589-4311-892b-646cd708193a.html)). Hamann, a newcomer, told the paper it is important for "community voices to feel heard" ([Mankato Free Press, June 8, 2026](https://www.mankatofreepress.com/news/local_news/race-to-watch-hamann-hopes-to-bring-conversation-to-city-council/article_167d04f5-3f33-4b85-a921-8e6e72a43b16.html)).

**Organizing through parishes.** In 2013 St. Maron, Holy Family and St. George held a joint bake sale for people displaced in Syria ([The Catholic Spirit, 2013](https://www.thecatholicspirit.com/news/local-news/local-syrians-find-support-st-maron/)). In 2017 St. Maron parishioners raised money for a 180-foot cross in Ijdabra, Lebanon; Maroun framed it as keeping Christianity present in the Middle East ([The Catholic Spirit, 2017](https://www.thecatholicspirit.com/news/local-news/st-maron-parish-raises-money-cross-lebanon/)). In 2022 the St. Maron festival pledged 10 percent of its net proceeds to Lebanese charities ([Racket, 2022](https://racketmn.com/st-marons-lebanese-festival-northeast-minneapolis)).

**Views on war differ.** During the 2006 war, a Brooklyn Park man told MPR that Israel "went too far" while also blaming Hezbollah for starting the conflict, and Maroun said, "We don't condone what Hezbollah is doing," while worrying "about every innocent civilian" ([MPR News, 2006](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2006/07/20/isrealreax)). In 2024 Louisa Farhat called the war "a humanitarian issue," not "an Arab issue" or "a Lebanese issue" ([MPR News, 2024](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2024/10/30/minneapolis-chef-butcher-raises-money-for-lebanese-red-cross)). These are individuals' views and do not stand for the community.

**Census advocacy.** Yalla Count MENA In!, a project of the Arab American Institute Foundation, lists Minnesota partners and argues for a Middle Eastern and North African category so that groups such as Lebanese Americans are counted ([Yalla Count MENA In!](https://yallacountmenain.org/states/minnesota-3nmg2)).

No current Lebanese American member of the Minnesota Legislature was identified.

## 10. Education

**Attainment.** Among Lebanese Minnesotans 25 and older, 97.8 percent finished high school, 50.9 percent hold a bachelor's degree or more and 16.9 percent hold a graduate or professional degree. Another 32.2 percent have some college or an associate's degree ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/lebanese)).

**Learning in parishes.** Holy Family runs weekly faith formation and First Communion preparation ([Holy Family](https://www.holyfamilymaronitechurch.org/)). St. George's 2001 expansion was built to hold "a fast growing church school program" ([St. George](https://www.mideastfest.com/who-we-are/)). No current Arabic or Syriac language class for children at the parishes was found.

**College.** The University of Minnesota's Arab Student Association, started in 2020 or 2021, welcomes Arab and non-Arab students; its listed leaders are mostly from Egypt, and it is not specific to Lebanese students ([Arab Student Association](https://asaatumn.weebly.com/about.html)). No Lebanese student association at a Minnesota college was found.

**Scholarship.** The Immigration History Research Center Archives and its research guide on Syrian and early Lebanese Americans are the main academic resource ([UMN Libraries](https://libguides.umn.edu/ASCsyrians)). A Macalester College senior paper on Lebanese placemaking in the Twin Cities (Anna Nassiff, 2014) is listed in the Northeaster's sources but could not be opened.

**School data.** The Minnesota Student Survey asks students whether they are Middle Eastern or North African, with Lebanese as one follow-up choice, according to the Health Department's table listings. Those tables could not be opened (see "What could not be established").

## 11. Health and wellbeing, broadly

**Coverage.** Compass suppresses the uninsured rate for Lebanese Minnesotans because the sample is small ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/lebanese)).

**Stress from abroad.** Sources from several crises show how events in Lebanon reach Minnesota families. In 2006 Maroun said many in his congregation were praying for relatives ([MPR News, 2006](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2006/07/20/isrealreax)). After the 2020 Beirut explosion he said "every one of his parishioners has been impacted in some way," as The Catholic Spirit paraphrased him ([The Catholic Spirit, 2020](https://www.thecatholicspirit.com/news/local-news/mass-at-mendota-heights-parish-to-be-dedicated-to-victims-of-explosion-in-beirut/)). In 2024 Farhat described "crippling helplessness" and finding strength in community ([MPR News, 2024](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2024/10/30/minneapolis-chef-butcher-raises-money-for-lebanese-red-cross)). The same pattern holds for St. Maron's Syrian members: in 2013 a Syrian-born Fridley pediatrician in the parish said of his family in Aleppo, "I am worried sick about them" ([The Catholic Spirit, 2013](https://www.thecatholicspirit.com/news/local-news/local-syrians-find-support-st-maron/)).

**Community as care.** Festival organizers describe the event as a place for reunions; Maroun told Racket that "the hugs that go on between family members and friends" happen there ([Racket](https://racketmn.com/st-marons-lebanese-festival-northeast-minneapolis)). Holy Family parishioners serve lunch monthly at the Opportunity Center in Minneapolis ([Holy Family, outreach](https://www.holyfamilymaronitechurch.org/outreach.html)).

No clinic, health program or provider group specific to Lebanese Minnesotans was found, and no Minnesota health study reports Lebanese results separately.

## 12. Disability

**The one Minnesota number.** Compass reports that 13.0 percent of Lebanese Minnesotans have a disability, give or take 4.3 points, and 10.5 percent of those under 65, give or take 4.6 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/lebanese)). Statewide, Compass says "at least 12%" of Minnesotans have a disability, and notes that the survey's six questions about hearing, vision, thinking, walking, self-care and living independently do not capture everyone the Americans with Disabilities Act protects ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/disability)). With margins this wide, the Lebanese figure cannot be said to differ from the state's. The Lebanese group is also older than many cultural communities (17 percent 65 or older), and disability rises with age, from under 1 percent of young children to 59 percent of people 85 and older statewide ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/disability)).

**What else was looked for.** No Minnesota study, Health Department report, Human Services report, University of Minnesota study or community survey was found that reports disability, special education or autism figures for Lebanese Minnesotans, or for Middle Eastern and North African Minnesotans as a group. The Minnesota Student Survey's Middle Eastern or North African tables could not be opened. No Minnesota organization serving Lebanese or Arab American families with disabilities was found.

**Questions the record raises.** These are questions, not findings:

- *Who is counted?* Most data now in use have no Middle Eastern or North African category; Yalla Count MENA In! is campaigning for one in the 2030 Census ([Yalla Count MENA In!](https://yallacountmenain.org/states/minnesota-3nmg2)). A disability report broken out only by race will not show Lebanese Minnesotans unless it also asks about ancestry or Middle Eastern and North African identity.
- *Language.* Most Lebanese Minnesotans speak only English at home, but 13.9 percent speak another language and the share with limited English is suppressed. Whether a family wants Arabic, French or English, and which Arabic, is a question for that family.
- *Where people turn.* The sources show parishes as centers of mutual help (sections 5 and 9). Whether families use them for disability support, and whether parishes are set up for that, is not documented.
- *What produced an impairment.* Some Lebanese Minnesotans lived through war; the sources name a teenage fighter in the civil war and families who left during bombing. No source connects that history to disability rates among Lebanese Minnesotans, and none should be assumed.

Material about disability in Lebanon itself describes another country's systems and conditions and should not be read as describing Lebanese Minnesotans.

## 13. What is changing now

**War in Lebanon, again, in 2026.** After U.S.-Israeli strikes on Iran, Hezbollah fired rockets and drones toward Israel on March 2, 2026, and Israel announced a ground operation on March 16, according to a federal notice ([Federal Register notice 2026-10704, May 29, 2026](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-10704.pdf)). Al Jazeera reported on April 8 that Israel said it had launched 100 strikes across Lebanon in ten minutes ([Al Jazeera, April 8, 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/video/newsfeed/2026/4/8/israel-launches-100-air-strikes-in-10-minutes-across-lebanon)). No 2026 Minnesota news story about Lebanese Minnesotans' response was found; earlier coverage from 2024 and 2006 is in sections 9 and 11.

**Immigration status, at group level.** Lebanon has been designated for Temporary Protected Status. Because the government missed its deadline to decide, the designation was automatically extended through Nov. 27, 2026, and the notice said the Secretary would review conditions at least 60 days before that date ([Federal Register notice](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-10704.pdf)). The Associated Press reported that about 11,000 Lebanese nationals nationwide hold this status ([AP via WTOP, May 2026](https://wtop.com/national/2026/05/trump-administration-grants-rare-tps-reprieve-extending-protections-for-11000-lebanese/)). No Minnesota count was found, and no decision after May was found. Separately, federal immigration agents' presence in Mankato early in 2026 became an issue in the city's mayoral race ([Mankato Free Press, Aug. 7, 2026](https://www.mankatofreepress.com/news/local_news/mankato-mayor-candidates-address-questions-ahead-of-primary-election/article_7d757752-b589-4311-892b-646cd708193a.html)).

**A pastor leaves.** St. Maron's pastor of more than 35 years has been chosen as archbishop of Damascus (section 5). The parish's website was being rebuilt in fall 2026.

**Festivals continue.** All three parish festivals were held in 2026, and St. Maron's added a third day.

**Businesses change hands.** Beirut Restaurant closed its West St. Paul location and its second-generation owners opened a fast-casual spot in Rosemount in 2025 ([Yahoo News, 2025](https://www.yahoo.com/news/minnesota-restaurant-openings-closings-february-174006345.html)). Long-running places such as Jacob's 101 (closed 2005) and Abdo's Store (closed 1980) are history.

**Counting.** After the Census Bureau changed how it collects ancestry in 2024, the single-year Lebanese count was 10,066, which Compass calls the most accurate estimate of the community's size ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/lebanese)). Advocates continue to push for a Middle Eastern and North African category in data ([Yalla Count MENA In!](https://yallacountmenain.org/states/minnesota-3nmg2)).

## 14. Strengths and contributions

- **Institutions that last.** Three parishes founded between 1903 and 1918 are still active, and the oldest Lebanese deli in Minneapolis has served from the same address since 1973 (sections 4 and 5).
- **Welcoming others.** St. Maron and St. George describe congregations that now include Christians from Syria, Jordan, Palestine, Iraq and elsewhere, plus converts ([Northeaster](https://www.mynortheaster.com/news/lebanese-came-to-northeast-in-waves/); [St. George](https://www.mideastfest.com/who-we-are/); [Racket](https://racketmn.com/st-marons-lebanese-festival-northeast-minneapolis)).
- **Sharing culture.** Three yearly festivals bring Lebanese food, dabke and church tours to the public, run largely by volunteers across generations.
- **Public service.** Two Lebanese American mayors in Mankato, and a longtime St. Paul mayor whose mother was Lebanese.
- **Giving.** Fundraising for the Lebanese Red Cross, for displaced Syrians and for Lebanese charities; monthly meals for homeless neighbors (sections 9 and 11).
- **Small business.** From peddlers and corner groceries to delis, catering, a butcher shop and trucking, the community has a long record of family enterprise.
- **Holding complexity.** Sources show Lebanese Minnesotans speaking with care about wars that divide others, worrying about "every innocent civilian," as Maroun put it in 2006.
