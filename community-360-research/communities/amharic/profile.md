# Amharic-speaking Minnesotans

This page is about a language community: people in Minnesota who speak Amharic at home, at church, at work or with their elders. Many are Amhara, but not all. Amharic is also spoken by Ethiopians of other backgrounds and by some Eritreans. An attorney in Minneapolis who once led the Supreme Court of Ethiopia's Somali Region lists English, Somali and Amharic among his languages ([AMA Law Group](https://www.amalawgroup.com/)). In Worthington, a congregation of Ethiopians and Eritreans who between them speak many languages holds its services in Amharic because nearly all members share it ([The Globe, Oct. 28, 2020](https://www.dglobe.com/lifestyle/ethiopian-orthodox-church-almost-ready-to-build)). Speaking Amharic says something about a person's history, but not their ethnicity, faith or politics, so it is better to ask.

The Ethiopian and Oromo pages cover the wider Ethiopian community and Oromo Minnesotans. This page tries not to repeat them. It looks at what the language itself opens and closes here: interpreters, schools, churches, media, voting and health information, and the people who carry Amharic across generations.

## 1. People and place

**How many speak Amharic.** No current count exists. The last time the Census Bureau published Amharic on its own for Minnesota was for 2009 to 2013. It estimated 9,490 Minnesotans age 5 and older who spoke Amharic at home, give or take 1,009. That put Minnesota seventh among the states, behind California (27,440), Virginia, Maryland, Washington, Texas and Georgia ([U.S. Census Bureau, Detailed Languages Spoken at Home, states, 2009-2013, released October 2015](https://www.census.gov/data/tables/2013/demo/2009-2013-lang-tables.html)). Since then, the bureau's standard tables fold Amharic into one category, "Amharic, Somali, or other Afro-Asiatic languages," which also takes in Somali, a far larger language in Minnesota ([Census Bureau variable list, table B16001, 2019-2023](https://api.census.gov/data/2023/acs/acs5/variables.json)). So a current Amharic-only number for Minnesota cannot be read from the published tables. Any figure you see for "Amharic" in recent years either comes from another source or is that larger group.

That 2009-2013 count is more than a decade old and was taken before many later arrivals. For scale, Minnesota Compass now estimates 45,159 people in the Ethiopian cultural community for 2020 to 2024 (give or take 5,404), and 53,754 for 2024 alone (give or take 12,826). That is a count of ancestry and birthplace, not language. It also includes Oromo Minnesotans whose answers the Census Bureau recodes to "Ethiopian," and it leaves out people born in Ethiopia who report Somali ancestry ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian); [Compass methodology](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/methodology)). Among that umbrella group age 5 and older, 76.2 percent speak a language other than English at home, give or take 4.2 points, and Compass does not say which language.

**Children, the firmest current count.** Schools report each student's primary home language. In the 2024-25 school year, 2,379 Minnesota students had Amharic as their primary home language, and 948 of them were English learners, up from 836 English learners in 2020. Amharic ranked ninth among home languages of English learners, behind Spanish, Somali, Hmong, Karen, Arabic, Oromo, Russian and Vietnamese ([Minnesota Department of Education, English Learners in Minnesota Report 2024-25, via Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/other/261381.pdf)). For comparison, the same table shows 3,297 students with Oromo as their primary home language. A home language is not an identity: a child in an Amharic-speaking family who mostly speaks English at home would not be counted.

**Where.** In 2009 to 2013 the Census Bureau placed the most Amharic speakers in Ramsey County (3,610, give or take 732), then Hennepin (3,060, give or take 546) and Dakota (1,005, give or take 352) ([Census Bureau, county tables, 2009-2013](https://www.census.gov/data/tables/2013/demo/2009-2013-lang-tables.html)). The school data from 2024-25 tell a different story for children who are still learning English: Dakota County schools reported the most Amharic-speaking English learners (320), ahead of Ramsey (247), Hennepin (147), Washington (82), Anoka (77), Olmsted (19) and Scott (15) ([MDE, 2024-25](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/other/261381.pdf)). The two sources measure different people at different times, so they cannot prove a move to the suburbs. They do suggest that a good share of Amharic-speaking family life now happens in Dakota County suburbs, which is worth knowing when planning outreach. The Rosemount-Apple Valley-Eagan school district (District 196) lists Amharic first, alphabetically, among the languages its students can test in for a bilingual seal ([District 196](https://www.district196.org/fs/pages/359)).

Outside the metro, the evidence is small and scattered. In Worthington, the Orthodox congregation reported 50 to 100 people at a typical Sunday service and about 400 at special events in 2020, drawn from southwest Minnesota, northwest Iowa and southeast South Dakota ([The Globe, 2020](https://www.dglobe.com/lifestyle/ethiopian-orthodox-church-almost-ready-to-build)). In Rochester, an Eagan man opened the city's first Ethiopian store in the summer of 2024 after talking with members of St. George Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, because people were driving to the Twin Cities two to four times a month for injera ([Post Bulletin, Aug. 2024](https://www.postbulletin.com/business/rochester-welcomes-its-first-ethiopian-store)).

**How people came.** The Ethiopian page tells the history of arrival in waves after 1974. Two Minnesota stories show how far back Amharic speakers go here. In 1976 the scholar Getatchew Haile, whose family had been forced into exile, joined the Hill Museum & Manuscript Library at Saint John's University in Collegeville. He stayed four decades as curator of its Ethiopia Study Center and published in both English and Amharic; he died in 2021, and his funeral was held at Saint John's Abbey Church ([HMML](https://hmml.org/stories/getatchew-haile-ethiopic-scholar/); [HMML media release, 2021](https://hmml.org/stories/prof-getatchew-haile-fba-1931-2021/)). In 1999, MPR News visited Enuye Faneta's Merkato grocery on Cedar Avenue in Minneapolis. She had joined her husband in the United States in 1987, five years after he came as a refugee, and had been a language and math teacher in Ethiopia. Newcomers came to her first, she said, because "we speak the same language," and she pointed them to the Social Security office, the welfare office and the Ethiopian community office ([MPR News, Nov. 3, 1999](https://archive.mpr.org/stories/1999/11/03/enuyes-merkato-ethiopian-grocery-store)). That role, the Amharic speaker who already knows the system, still matters.

**Who speaks Amharic, and why that varies.** The 1999 MPR story called Amharic "the language of the Amhara people," one of more than 80 ethnic groups in Ethiopia ([MPR News, 1999](https://archive.mpr.org/stories/1999/11/03/enuyes-merkato-ethiopian-grocery-store)). A University of Minnesota instructor of Afan Oromo, Bekele Dako, has noted that Afan Oromo could not be taught in schools in Ethiopia until 1991 ([University of Minnesota, 2026](https://brief.umn.edu/feature/reviving-once-endangered-language)). That history may help explain why some Oromo, Tigrayan and other Ethiopian Minnesotans speak Amharic, and why some may not want to be addressed in it. It is not safe to assume that an Amharic speaker is Amhara, or that an Ethiopian person wants Amharic.

## 2. All walks of life

No income, education or housing figures exist for Amharic speakers as such. The best available picture is Compass's Ethiopian umbrella for 2020 to 2024, in 2024 dollars, which includes Oromo and other Ethiopian Minnesotans and carries wide margins ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). The Ethiopian page sets those numbers out in full. In short:

- **Households doing well.** About 23.2 percent of households take in $100,000 or more, give or take 6.0 points. Median household income is $63,667, give or take $6,426.
- **Middle-income and working families.** About 40.5 percent of households earn $50,000 to $99,999. About 35.6 percent of households own their homes.
- **Families with less.** About 18.2 percent of households take in under $35,000, and another 34.5 percent of people live between the poverty line and twice that line.
- **People in poverty.** About 10.9 percent of people, give or take 3.5 points. Among people 65 and older the estimate is 34.2 percent, but the margin of 15.9 points makes that figure very uncertain.

**English and the language divide.** Language also divides the community. In 2009 to 2013, about 4,395 of Minnesota's 9,490 Amharic speakers, a little under half, told the Census Bureau they spoke English less than "very well" ([Census Bureau, 2009-2013](https://www.census.gov/data/tables/2013/demo/2009-2013-lang-tables.html)). The rest said they spoke English "very well." For the Ethiopian umbrella today, Compass puts the share who speak English less than "very well" at 30.2 percent, give or take 3.3 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). In practice that means the same community holds professionals who work all day in English and elders who need every letter, form and appointment in Amharic.

**People in public view.** A few examples show the range, and none of them stands for everyone:

- **Scholarship.** The late Getatchew Haile, a MacArthur Fellow, catalogued thousands of Ethiopian manuscripts from Collegeville and published books in English and Amharic ([HMML](https://hmml.org/stories/getatchew-haile-ethiopic-scholar/)). The current page covers his life and that of his daughter, Rebecca Haile.
- **Law.** Abdinasir Abdulahi, principal attorney of AMA Law Group on East Franklin Avenue, is licensed in Minnesota and lists English, Somali and Amharic as his languages; his firm says its staff speak Amharic and Somali ([AMA Law Group](https://www.amalawgroup.com/)).
- **Small business.** Belai Mergia and Rahel Tassew turned a coffee shop on Minnehaha Avenue into Selam, a family restaurant whose name means "peace" in Amharic ([From MPLS With Love, updated October 2026](https://mplswithlove.com/guides/best-ethiopian-east-african-food)). Kebede Abay of Eagan opened Adey Abeba in Rochester ([Post Bulletin, 2024](https://www.postbulletin.com/business/rochester-welcomes-its-first-ethiopian-store)).
- **Media.** Henok Degfu built ZeHabesha, an Ethiopian news outlet with Amharic content, from Minnesota (section 8).

**Hardship.** Before federal pandemic-era funds were cut in March 2025, workers at Niyyah Recovery Initiative in Minnesota spent hours each day at soup kitchens, mosques and on the streets of East African neighborhoods looking for people using drugs, speaking Somali, Amharic and Swahili among other languages. One worker described meeting a man who had lost his belongings and returned to using drugs. After the cuts, the program said it might not be able to continue that outreach ([KFF Health News via CBS News, April 22, 2025](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/trump-funding-cuts-addiction-recovery/)). Niyyah's own website showed an "under construction" notice when checked, so its current work could not be confirmed.

## 3. Work and the economy

There is no Minnesota data on what Amharic speakers do for a living. A few kinds of work are tied directly to the language.

**Interpreting.** Interpreting is paid work that depends on the language, and Minnesota sets few rules for it. The Minnesota Department of Health keeps a voluntary roster of spoken-language health care interpreters. Listing requires no proof of skill, the department says it is not authorized to verify what interpreters report, and some insurers require roster listing in order to bill ([MDH](https://www.health.state.mn.us/facilities/providers/interpreter/index.html)). In a 2015 report to the Legislature, MDH said the roster grew from about 100 interpreters in January 2010 to about 3,600 by December 2014, across 225 languages. An Amharic and Oromo interpreter quoted in that report objected to enforcement against interpreters and said pay in medical interpreting was low ([MDH, Interpreting in Health Care Settings, 2015, via Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2015/mandated/150235.pdf)). How many Amharic interpreters are on the roster today could not be established; MDH's roster pages give no count by language.

Court interpreting is set by the Minnesota Judicial Branch. Its roster and certification pages would not open, so whether any court-certified Amharic interpreter works in Minnesota could not be confirmed.

**Teaching.** A 2023 Minnesota law created a free pathway for heritage language teachers to earn licenses. Sahan Journal reported in January 2025 that the first cohort of 47 teachers included Amharic and Tigrinya among its languages ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 6, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/education/minnesota-teachers-heritage-language-licenses-hmong-karen-somali/)). The report does not say how many were Amharic teachers.

**Food, retail and services.** The grocery, restaurant and coffee trades are where Amharic is most heard in daily commerce (section 4). In 1999, the Merkato grocer's main business was teff flour and injera, sometimes more than 1,000 pieces on a weekend ([MPR News, 1999](https://archive.mpr.org/stories/1999/11/03/enuyes-merkato-ethiopian-grocery-store)).

**Many jobs at once.** The path of one Amharic-language publisher shows how work often stacks up. Sahan Journal reported in 2020 that after arriving in Minnesota, Henok Degfu worked at a Minneapolis bakery and then held nearly every housekeeping job at two hotels, up to director, while producing his English and Amharic newspaper at night and on weekends. He left hotel work at the end of 2017, when his outlet's advertising could support six full-time employees. The same story quoted Seble Asnake, owner of an adult foster care business in Columbia Heights, who had lived in Minnesota 34 years ([Sahan Journal, July 14, 2020](https://sahanjournal.com/profiles/ethiopian-journalist-zehabesha/)). Care work and small business both run through these accounts, but no Minnesota data show how common they are.

**Language as a workplace issue.** A Minnesota-specific study of Amharic speakers at work, or of unions with Amharic-speaking members, did not turn up.

## 4. Businesses and commercial life

The full list is in `businesses.json`. The Ethiopian page surveys restaurants and groceries in depth; this section focuses on places where Amharic is part of the business.

**Groceries and injera.** Shabelle, on East Franklin Avenue in Seward, is part grocery and part restaurant, selling teff flour, spices, green coffee beans, jebena coffee pots and injera ([From MPLS With Love, updated October 2026](https://mplswithlove.com/guides/best-ethiopian-east-african-food)). Katar River on Minnehaha Avenue bakes injera that many regulars buy to take home ([same guide](https://mplswithlove.com/guides/best-ethiopian-east-african-food)). In Rochester, Adey Abeba, whose sign includes its name in Amharic script, sells injera, the clay griddle to make it, coffee pots, mesob baskets, traditional clothing and cosmetics; its owner said Rochester families had been buying two or three bags of injera at a time in the Twin Cities ([Post Bulletin, 2024](https://www.postbulletin.com/business/rochester-welcomes-its-first-ethiopian-store)). A 2025 or 2026 sign of operation for Adey Abeba could not be found.

**Restaurants.** Selam and Meseret on Minnehaha Avenue, The Red Sea and Dilla's in Cedar-Riverside, and Bolé in St. Paul all appear in a guide updated in October 2026 ([From MPLS With Love](https://mplswithlove.com/guides/best-ethiopian-east-african-food)). Bolé's website lists its Pascal Street location in St. Paul and a Maple Grove location ([Bolé](https://boleethiopiancuisine.com/)). The guide notes that Ghebre's, an Eritrean restaurant in St. Paul, has closed.

**Professional services.** AMA Law Group, 1113 E. Franklin Ave., Minneapolis, says its staff speak Amharic and Somali and that it handles immigration, family, criminal, personal injury and Social Security disability cases ([AMA Law Group](https://www.amalawgroup.com/)). Its most recent dated blog post found was from 2024, so it is listed as unconfirmed. Ethiopian-owned tax, insurance, travel and accounting businesses that serve clients in Amharic surely exist, but none could be confirmed by name. A chamber of commerce for Ethiopian or Amharic-speaking businesses in Minnesota did not turn up.

**Media as business.** ZeHabesha is the best-known Ethiopian media business founded in Minnesota (section 8). A diaspora listing site, Mefthe, advertises Minnesota events in English and Amharic (section 6).

**What the directories miss.** Meet Minneapolis's directory of BIPOC and multicultural media, updated Oct. 15, 2026, lists Mshale, several Somali outlets and KFAI's Eritrean Community Radio under African immigrant media, but no Ethiopian or Amharic-language outlet ([Meet Minneapolis](https://www.minneapolis.org/meetings-events/destination-services/planning-services/bipoc-media/)). An organization that relies on that list to reach "East African" Minnesotans would miss Amharic speakers entirely.

## 5. Religion and spiritual life

Amharic is a language of worship for many Ethiopian Christians in Minnesota, and the churches are among the strongest Amharic-language institutions in the state. Ethiopian Minnesotans also include Catholic, Evangelical and Muslim communities, and some people of no faith; how many of them worship or gather in Amharic is not documented.

**Orthodox Tewahedo.** In the Ethiopian Orthodox church, the ancient language Ge'ez is used in the liturgy, alongside Amharic.

- **Debre Selam Medhanealem**, which opened a new church at 2601 30th Ave. S. in Seward in April 2026, holds Sunday services from 7 to 11 a.m. in Ge'ez and in Amharic, with some portions translated into English ([Longfellow Nokomis Messenger, April 27, 2026](https://www.longfellownokomismessenger.com/stories/debre-selam-medhanealem-ethiopian-orthodox-tewahedo-churchfinds-new-home-in-seward,161305)). Board Chair Debalke Gebeyehu called the church "a living bridge" for immigrants.
- **Worthington.** The congregation there, first established in 2010, has used the names Menbere Tsehay Sent Mary and Tsadakane Mariam in local coverage. In 2020 its project coordinator, Abebe Alubel, said members came from Ethiopia and Eritrea and spoke many languages, but nearly all spoke Amharic, so services were in Amharic. In 2022 the city council approved a permit for the church to move into The Globe's former office at 300 11th St. ([The Globe, 2020](https://www.dglobe.com/lifestyle/ethiopian-orthodox-church-almost-ready-to-build); [The Globe, March 29, 2022](https://www.dglobe.com/council-approves-new-home-for-ethiopian-church-oxford-street-reconstruction)). Whether the move was completed could not be confirmed.
- **Rochester.** St. George Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church was where a new store owner went to share his plans in 2024 ([Post Bulletin, 2024](https://www.postbulletin.com/business/rochester-welcomes-its-first-ethiopian-store)).
- The Ethiopian page describes Kidist Selassie Cathedral in Minneapolis and Debre Bisrat St. Gabriel and St. Arsema, whose new Rosemount building burned in July 2026.

**Evangelical.** The Ethiopian Evangelical Church in Minnesota, at 7360 33rd St. N. in Oakdale, lists a Sunday worship service in Amharic, a Wednesday service in Amharic, a Saturday young adult service in Amharic and a Sunday youth service in English ([EECMN](https://eecmn.org/)). That pattern, Amharic for adults and English for youth, shows in one schedule how a congregation is handling the generational shift. The Evangelical Mekane Yesus Lutheran Church Fellowship in Minnesota, 1566 W. Thomas Ave., St. Paul, reported average weekly attendance of 64 for 2025; its listing does not name a worship language ([Lutheran Church-Missouri Synod locator](https://locator.lcms.org/church/C/1001496)). Other Amharic-speaking Protestant congregations surely exist, but no other could be confirmed.

**Language classes in congregations.** Endalkachew Chala, a scholar and community member, wrote in June 2026 that there are roughly eight active Ethiopian and Eritrean Orthodox congregations in the metro, alongside Catholic, Evangelical and Muslim communities, and that each runs Amharic and Tigrinya classes for second-generation children ([MinnPost, June 2026](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)). That is his description, and individual schedules were not checked.

**Where language divides worship.** In 2005, MPR News reported that two Minneapolis churches held services in Oromo, and that at one of them, Bethany Lutheran on Franklin Avenue, there were "unlikely" to be Ethiopians other than Oromo at the service. The reporter described a "loose self-segregation" rooted in the strife back home ([MPR News, May 19, 2005](https://archive.mpr.org/stories/2005/05/19/ethiopians-diverse-group)). That was 21 years ago and may have changed. It is a reminder that an Amharic-language service is a home for some Ethiopian Christians and not for others.

**Muslim Amharic speakers.** No Minnesota source described a mosque or Muslim association that worships or teaches in Amharic. The Ethiopian and Oromo pages discuss Ethiopian Muslim life more broadly.

**Fasting.** Grumdahl reported in 2024 that observant Tewahedo Christians over age 7 fast about 180 days a year, giving up meat and dairy ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, 2024](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/the-new-ethiopian-scene/)). That affects meals at events, medicine taken "with food," and morning appointments.

## 6. Celebrations and observances

These are the observances most tied to Amharic-speaking church and community life. Dates and Minnesota events are in `calendar-entries.json`.

- **Enkutatash (Ethiopian New Year).** Falls on Sept. 11, 2026 and Sept. 12, 2027 ([Office Holidays, 2026](https://www.officeholidays.com/countries/ethiopia/2026); [2027](https://www.officeholidays.com/countries/ethiopia/2027)). The Ethiopian Community in Minnesota invited the public to a celebration at Highland Park Pavilion in St. Paul on Sunday, Sept. 13, 2026, from 2 to 7 p.m., with food, music, dance and traditional dress; the listing wrote the holiday's name in Amharic script ([Mefthe](https://mefthe.com/events/celebrate-ethiopian-new-year-in-minnesota/)). In 2022, the fourth annual Ethiopian Fest of Minnesota was held at Eden Prairie High School's stadium on Sept. 4, with concerts in West St. Paul, a fashion show and soccer; Henok Degfu said, "the Ethiopian New Year is coming" ([Eden Prairie Local News, Sept. 7, 2022](https://www.eplocalnews.org/2022/09/07/photo-gallery-ethiopian-fest-of-minnesota/)). A 2025 or 2026 Ethiopian Fest could not be confirmed.
- **Meskel (Finding of the True Cross).** Sept. 27, 2026 ([Office Holidays, 2026](https://www.officeholidays.com/countries/ethiopia/2026)). The Ethiopian page describes a Rochester bonfire celebration in 2022. No 2026 Minnesota Meskel event was found.
- **Genna (Christmas).** Jan. 7 in 2026 and 2027 ([Office Holidays](https://www.officeholidays.com/countries/ethiopia/2027)).
- **Timket (Epiphany).** Jan. 19 in 2026 and 2027 ([Office Holidays](https://www.officeholidays.com/countries/ethiopia/2027)). Chala wrote that on Timket in 2026, during the federal immigration enforcement operation, many families watched for agents on WhatsApp instead of walking to church ([MinnPost, June 2026](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)).
- **Fasika (Easter).** April 12, 2026 and May 2, 2027, after a long fast ([Office Holidays, 2026](https://www.officeholidays.com/countries/ethiopia/2026); [2027](https://www.officeholidays.com/countries/ethiopia/2027)). No Minnesota event details were found.
- **Adwa Victory Day,** March 2, marks Ethiopia's 1896 victory over Italy ([Office Holidays](https://www.officeholidays.com/countries/ethiopia/2026)). No Minnesota event was found.
- **Ethiopian Sports Federation in North America tournament.** June 27 to July 4, 2026, in the Twin Cities. Chala described live music every afternoon "in Amharic, Oromo and Tigrigna" ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)), and the governor declared July 3 Ethiopian Day ([MPR News, June 30, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/06/30/ethiopian-soccer-tournament-draws-thousands-despite-immigration-fears)).
- **Annual Arts Night.** A listing for Oct. 31, 2026 at the University of Minnesota's Coffman Memorial Union Theater, hosted by Yafet Art Center, promised performances, spoken word, a theatrical production and food, with $20 admission ([Mefthe](https://mefthe.com/events/celebrate-culture-art-annual-arts-night-at-the-university-of-minnesota/?mefthe_occurrence=2026-10-31)). Nothing else about the organizer could be confirmed.

Muslim Amharic speakers mark Ramadan, Eid al-Fitr, Eid al-Adha and Mawlid; nothing specific to Amharic-speaking Muslims in Minnesota was found.

## 7. Family, home and daily life

**Households.** Compass counts about 12,436 Ethiopian-community households averaging 3.30 people. One-third are married couples with children, and 26.1 percent are people living alone ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)).

**Language across generations.** The community is young: Compass counts 43.8 percent of the Ethiopian umbrella as 17 or younger ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)), and those children are going through English-language schools. In one Oakdale church, adults worship in Amharic while young people have their own English service ([EECMN](https://eecmn.org/)). In 2005 an Oromo community member, Robson Itawa, put the worry plainly: the language will not die, he said, but for the second generation it will be mixed with English ([MPR News, 2005](https://archive.mpr.org/stories/2005/05/19/ethiopians-diverse-group)). His words were about Oromo, but Amharic-speaking parents face the same question. Weekend church classes, heritage language teachers and the state's bilingual seals (section 10) are the ways families and schools are answering it.

**Interpreting at home.** Whether children in Amharic-speaking homes end up interpreting for their parents, and how often, has not been studied in Minnesota as far as could be found. The current page's warning against using children as interpreters is sound practice in any language.

**Food.** Injera is daily bread. "Everybody at home has injera," Kebede Abay told the Post Bulletin, comparing it to bread for Americans ([Post Bulletin, 2024](https://www.postbulletin.com/business/rochester-welcomes-its-first-ethiopian-store)). Coffee ceremonies, with beans roasted and brewed in a clay jebena, are part of hospitality at home and at restaurants such as Selam ([From MPLS With Love](https://mplswithlove.com/guides/best-ethiopian-east-african-food)).

**Dress, marriage and aging.** Little was found that is specific to Amharic-speaking Minnesotans. Event invitations ask people to wear traditional dress ([Mefthe](https://mefthe.com/events/celebrate-ethiopian-new-year-in-minnesota/)). Only 3.5 percent of the Ethiopian umbrella is 65 or older ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)), so the first large generation of Amharic-speaking elders is still ahead. The language needs of that generation in nursing homes, home care and memory care are a question to plan for, not yet a documented fact.

## 8. Arts, media, sports and culture

**News in Amharic.** ZeHabesha began in 2008 as a 24-page monthly newspaper in English and Amharic, read at first by Ethiopians in Minnesota, Chicago, Atlanta and South Dakota and paid for by ads from Ethiopian-owned businesses in Minnesota. "When they saw the newspaper, they remember Ethiopia," Henok Degfu said of his first readers; by 2020 it reached millions through YouTube and Facebook from a studio off Snelling Avenue in St. Paul ([Sahan Journal, July 14, 2020](https://sahanjournal.com/profiles/ethiopian-journalist-zehabesha/)). Its site had posts dated Oct. 30, 2026 when checked ([ZeHabesha](https://zehabesha.com/)). The current page describes the Ethiopian Airlines lawsuit. Beyond ZeHabesha, much Amharic news reaching Minnesota comes from outlets outside the state, which the current page lists.

**Radio.** KFAI's "Voices of Ethiopia," hosted by Ahmed Wassie, is described as a locally produced program of news, music and culture from East Africa; the most recent playlist found was from April 17, 2022 ([Spinitron, KFAI](https://spinitron.com/KFAI/pl/15852332/Voices-of-Ethiopia)). In 2005, MPR's report described him as "ethnically Amharic, not Oromo," a man who had lived in the United States since 1973 and who sometimes tried to bring sides together on the show. He described Oromo and Amharic speakers in Minnesota as giving each other "cold shoulders," though not fighting ([MPR News, 2005](https://archive.mpr.org/stories/2005/05/19/ethiopians-diverse-group)). KFAI's own site would not load, so the show's current schedule could not be confirmed.

**Music.** Concerts by Ethiopian singers are part of holiday gatherings. The 2022 Ethiopian Fest opened and closed with concerts in West St. Paul featuring Abdu Kiar, Fikeraddis Nekatibeb, Wendi Mak and Abby Lakew ([Eden Prairie Local News, 2022](https://www.eplocalnews.org/2022/09/07/photo-gallery-ethiopian-fest-of-minnesota/)). The Ethiopian page describes Minnesota-based Ethiopian musicians.

**Literature and scholarship.** Getatchew Haile published Ge'ez and Amharic texts and kept writing books in English and Amharic into retirement ([HMML](https://hmml.org/stories/getatchew-haile-ethiopic-scholar/)). The Hill Museum & Manuscript Library in Collegeville holds the Ethiopian Manuscript Microfilm Library, which HMML calls the most important archive of manuscript material in Ethiopian studies. Minnesota is, in that sense, one of the world's centers for the study of Ethiopia's written heritage.

**Sports.** The 2026 Ethiopian soccer tournament brought 32 teams to the Twin Cities. Player Nebiu Kinfu said, "there's no difference, there's no separation, we're just one" ([MPR News, June 30, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/06/30/ethiopian-soccer-tournament-draws-thousands-despite-immigration-fears)).

## 9. Civic and political life

**Voting in Amharic.** Minnesota's Secretary of State offers voter guides in the state's top languages, which a state elections official listed in 2025 as including Amharic ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 22, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/minnesota-non-english-languages-census-data/)). A state law sets stronger rules for "language minority districts," census tracts where at least 3 percent of residents speak English less than "very well." There, sample ballots and instructions are translated into the most common non-English language, and where the share reaches 20 percent, ten or more voters can ask for an interpreter at the polls ([Secretary of State, Voter Language Access, 2026](https://www.sos.mn.gov/media/eztgjxiz/2026voterlanguageaccess.pdf)). In the state's spreadsheet of designated tracts, as downloaded on Oct. 31, 2026, only one of 291 lists Amharic, and it lists it jointly: Census Tract 1034 in Hennepin County, marked "Amharic/Oromo, Somali," covering two Minneapolis Ward 5 precincts ([Secretary of State, language minority districts spreadsheet](https://www.sos.mn.gov/media/5634/language-minority-districts-tracts-precincts-languages.xlsx)). Statewide, every polling place posts voting instructions in only three languages: Spanish, Hmong and Somali ([Secretary of State, 2026](https://www.sos.mn.gov/media/eztgjxiz/2026voterlanguageaccess.pdf)). Why Amharic appears in so few tracts is not explained in the state's materials. One open question is whether Amharic speakers are spread too thinly across many tracts to reach the 3 percent line in any one of them.

**Large gatherings and old divides.** In July 2018, thousands came to Target Center in Minneapolis to hear Ethiopia's new prime minister, Abiy Ahmed. MPR reported that supporters waved Ethiopian and U.S. flags, and that he spoke of reviewing ethnic federalism, the system that divides districts by ethnic group ([MPR News, July 30, 2018](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2018/07/30/photos-ethiopian-prime-minister-visits-minnesota)). The Ethiopian page describes how the 2020-2022 Tigray war divided Minnesotans.

**Amhara Minnesotans and homeland politics.** Since July 2023, fighting has continued in Ethiopia's Amhara region between the federal army and Fano militias. Al Jazeera reported in March 2025 that the fighting followed what it called Amhara people's sense of betrayal over the 2022 Tigray peace deal, and that the army and Fano spokespeople gave sharply different casualty counts ([Al Jazeera, March 22, 2025](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2025/3/22/ethiopias-army-says-more-than-300-fano-fighters-killed-in-renewed-fighting)). In October 2026, fighting resumed in Tigray, with Ethiopia and Eritrea trading accusations ([Al Jazeera, Oct. 15, 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/10/15/ethiopian-forces-expand-control-in-tigray-as-militia-claims-eritrean-losses)). No Minnesota reporting on how Amhara Minnesotans are organizing around the Amhara conflict, or on an Amhara community organization in Minnesota, was found. That is a gap in the available record, not a sign that people here are silent. No source found here measures what Amharic-speaking Minnesotans think about these conflicts, and it is not safe to guess a person's views from their language.

**Relations among language groups.** The 2005 MPR report remains the clearest Minnesota account. An Amhara radio host described "cold shoulders" between speakers, and a University of Minnesota Morris sociologist, Solomon Gashaw, said immigrant life can temper differences because people end up working side by side ([MPR News, 2005](https://archive.mpr.org/stories/2005/05/19/ethiopians-diverse-group)). In 1999, the Merkato grocer said she treated Ethiopian, Eritrean and Oromo customers alike and welcomed concert flyers from Amhara and Eritrean bands on the same wall ([MPR News, 1999](https://archive.mpr.org/stories/1999/11/03/enuyes-merkato-ethiopian-grocery-store)). In 2026, a player at the soccer tournament said "we're just one" ([MPR News, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/06/30/ethiopian-soccer-tournament-draws-thousands-despite-immigration-fears)). All three are individual voices, a generation apart.

**Organizations.** The Ethiopian Community in Minnesota, 616 Rice St. N. in St. Paul, keeps office hours on Monday mornings and Friday afternoons ([ECM](https://ethiopiancommunitymn.org/contact/)) and organized the 2026 New Year celebration ([Mefthe](https://mefthe.com/events/celebrate-ethiopian-new-year-in-minnesota/)). No organization that describes itself as serving Amharic speakers as a language community, or Amhara Minnesotans specifically, could be confirmed.

## 10. Education

**In K-12 schools.** Amharic is the primary home language of 2,379 Minnesota students, 948 of whom were English learners in 2024-25 ([MDE](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/other/261381.pdf)).

**Bilingual seals.** Minnesota's Seal of Biliteracy program lists Amharic as an eligible language ([District 196](https://www.district196.org/fs/pages/359)), and the state's testing vendor offers Amharic among its bilingual seal tests and practice tests ([Extempore](https://extemporeapp.com/supporting-minnesotas-bilingual-seal)). A student who reads and writes Amharic can earn recognition for a language learned at home and at church. How many Amharic seals Minnesota students earn each year could not be found.

**Heritage language teachers.** Amharic was among the languages in the first cohort of Minnesota's heritage language teacher licensing pathway, created by a 2023 law ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 6, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/education/minnesota-teachers-heritage-language-licenses-hmong-karen-somali/)).

**East African Magnet School.** When St. Paul Public Schools opened it in 2023, CBS Minnesota reported that it focused on the languages of Somali, Amharic, Oromo, Tigrinya, Arabic and Swahili ([CBS Minnesota, Sept. 2023](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/first-school-year-underway-at-st-pauls-new-east-african-magnet-school/)). The school's website, checked in October 2026, describes a focus on the cultures and languages of East Africa, and its news items show Somali, Arabic and Karen teachers earning licenses and Somali books being translated; no Amharic-specific class or material was found on the school's pages ([East African Magnet School](https://eastafricanmagnet.spps.org/); [SPPS news](https://eastafricanmagnet.spps.org/news-details/~board/brag-board/post/spps-world-language-teachers-graduate-with-masters-degree-and-teaching-license)). That does not mean none exists.

**Higher education.** The University of Minnesota's language placement list includes Somali and Swahili but not Amharic ([UMN Language Testing](https://languagetesting.umn.edu/course-placement-languages)). Since 2025 the university has offered Afan Oromo, which it calls the only Oromo curriculum at a top-tier research university in the country ([University of Minnesota](https://brief.umn.edu/feature/reviving-once-endangered-language)). No Amharic course at a Minnesota college or university was found.

**Congregations as schools.** For some children, church is where they learn to read and write the Amharic script. Chala describes weekend Amharic and Tigrinya classes in the metro's Orthodox congregations ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)).

## 11. Health and wellbeing, broadly

**Health information in Amharic.** The Minnesota Department of Health's Amharic page offers materials on COVID-19, flu, food safety, depression and anxiety during and after pregnancy, a postpartum well-being plan, and a fact sheet urging people to avoid skin-lightening products, many with videos ([MDH](https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/translation/amharic.html)). Much of the page concerns COVID-19. Amharic is one of 27 entries on MDH's translation menu, which counts American Sign Language as one.

**Interpreters and trust.** When MDH met with adults in English classes for its 2015 report, Amharic speakers were among them. Participants said there were generally enough interpreters but not enough experienced ones, and that clinics sometimes failed to book one. One said, "There are lots of interpreters, but few who are experienced" ([MDH, 2015](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2015/mandated/150235.pdf)). The report does not separate what Amharic speakers said from others.

**Coverage.** About 7.3 percent of the Ethiopian umbrella has no health insurance, give or take 2.5 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)).

**Recovery and mental health.** Niyyah Recovery Initiative's street outreach in Amharic and other languages was cut back in 2025 (section 2). No Minnesota mental health provider that advertises Amharic-language counseling could be confirmed.

**Faith and healing.** The current page summarizes a 2024 study from Addis Ababa on how holy water priests and a mental health clinic work alongside each other. No Minnesota study of faith-based healing among Amharic speakers was found.

## 12. Disability

**What Minnesota numbers say.** Compass reports that 5.5 percent of the Ethiopian umbrella has a disability, give or take 1.5 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). No disability figure exists for Amharic speakers as such.

**Materials in Amharic.** What exists is thin:

- The Multicultural Autism Action Network in Minnesota has an Amharic video page with one video, on elopement, when an autistic child wanders or runs from a safe place ([MAAN](https://www.maanmn.org/ahmaric-language-videos)). Its site carries a 2024 copyright.
- PACER Center, a Minnesota nonprofit for families of children with disabilities, received a grant in 2023 to make short special education videos in eight languages. A case study about that project lists Amharic among Minnesota's most spoken languages but does not say which eight languages PACER used ([Synthesia case study](https://www.synthesia.io/case-studies/pacer-center)). Whether PACER offers Amharic materials could not be confirmed.
- MDH's Amharic page has postpartum mental health materials but none labeled as disability services ([MDH](https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/translation/amharic.html)).

**Questions for the work, not conclusions.**

- If a parent reads Amharic but not English, does the school's special education notice reach them? The state counts 948 Amharic-speaking English learners ([MDE](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/other/261381.pdf)) but not how many have parents who need Amharic.
- Who interprets at an IEP meeting or a MnCHOICES assessment? A roster listing does not show skill ([MDH](https://www.health.state.mn.us/facilities/providers/interpreter/index.html)). How would a family or a worker know the interpreter knows the words for evaluation, waiver or guardianship in Amharic?
- What word does a family use? No source was found on Amharic disability terms, respectful or stigmatizing, was found. Ask the person, and use a trained interpreter.
- Is the person Amharic-speaking, or Amharic-able? An Oromo or Tigrayan parent may speak Amharic but understand complex news far better in Afan Oromo or Tigrinya.
- What produced an impairment, if one exists? No Minnesota source addresses this for Amharic speakers. It should stay an open question, never assumed from culture or origin.

**People.** No publicly self-identified Amharic-speaking disability advocate in Minnesota was found. Abdinasir Abdulahi's firm lists Social Security disability among its practice areas and serves clients in Amharic ([AMA Law Group](https://www.amalawgroup.com/)); that is the only Amharic-language disability-related service found in the state.

## 13. What is changing now

- **Immigration enforcement and visas (2025-2026).** Chala wrote that Operation Metro Surge, the federal enforcement effort concentrated in Minneapolis and St. Paul from December 2025 through February 2026, publicly targeted Somali Minnesotans but chilled every East African community, and that partial or total visa bans on 39 African countries, including Ethiopia and Eritrea, were keeping families apart ([MinnPost, June 2026](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)). The soccer federation nearly moved its tournament because members were afraid ([MPR News, June 30, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/06/30/ethiopian-soccer-tournament-draws-thousands-despite-immigration-fears)). The Ethiopian page covers the end of Temporary Protected Status for Ethiopia.
- **War again, October 2026.** Fighting in Tigray and continuing conflict in the Amhara region (section 9) reach Amharic-speaking families here through relatives, news and social media. No Minnesota reporting on that reaction was found.
- **New church buildings.** Debre Selam Medhanealem opened its Seward church in April 2026; Debre Bisrat's new Rosemount church burned in July (Ethiopian page).
- **Language policy.** Minnesota's Enterprise Translations Office, created in 2023 to standardize translation across about 25 state agencies, had two staff each for Spanish, Hmong and Somali as of September 2025, and its budget to reimburse outside translation had been cut from $250,000 to $100,000 a year ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 22, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/minnesota-non-english-languages-census-data/)). The report names no Amharic translators on the office's staff.
- **Generations.** Heritage language licenses, bilingual seals and English youth services all point to a community deciding how to keep Amharic while its children grow up in English.

## 14. Strengths and contributions

- **A shared language that bridges.** In Worthington, Amharic lets Ethiopians and Eritreans who speak many languages worship together ([The Globe, 2020](https://www.dglobe.com/lifestyle/ethiopian-orthodox-church-almost-ready-to-build)).
- **Built by the community.** Congregations run language schools, youth services and mutual aid, mostly with volunteer labor, as Chala describes ([MinnPost](https://www.minnpost.com/community-voices/2026/06/celebrate-ethiopians-and-resilience-at-twin-cities-soccer-tourney-ethiopian-sport-federation/)).
- **Scholarship of world importance.** Getatchew Haile's work in Collegeville made Minnesota a center of Ethiopian manuscript studies ([HMML](https://hmml.org/stories/getatchew-haile-ethiopic-scholar/)).
- **People who guide newcomers.** From the 1999 Cedar Avenue grocer to today's interpreters and heritage language teachers, Amharic speakers who know Minnesota's systems help others find their way.
- **Multilingual Minnesotans.** Some Amharic speakers work across several languages, like the Minneapolis attorney who practices in English, Somali and Amharic ([AMA Law Group](https://www.amalawgroup.com/)), a skill that schools, clinics and courts need.
