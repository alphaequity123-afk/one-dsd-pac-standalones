# Oromo in Minnesota
Checked: October 31, 2026

## 1. People and place

**How many: nobody knows, and the reasons matter.** No official count of Oromo Minnesotans could be found. MNopedia's history, written by Teferi Fufa, one of the first Oromo people to settle here, says plainly that "there is no census that gives us an accurate figure," and offers "tens of thousands" as a fair guess ([MNopedia, first published Feb. 16, 2021, last modified Aug. 12, 2026, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)). Here is what each source actually says, oldest first. None of these measures the same thing.

| Year | Figure | Who says so | What it counts |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2001 | More than 12,000 | [Minnesota Daily, Aug. 1, 2001](https://mndaily.com/?p=251870) | Oromo refugees; the article names no source for the number |
| 2004 | About 15,000 | [Post Bulletin column, May 19, 2004](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/col-why-oromo-citizens-like-minnisootaa) | Oromo refugees settled over the prior decade; no source named |

**The best-sourced range.** Only two figures in this table come with a stated method: the state demographer's roughly 8,500 and the Oromo Cultural Institute's roughly 40,000, both reported in 2016 ([Wilder Research](https://www.wilder.org/wp-content/pdf-file/OromoCommunityAssessment_7-16.pdf); [Minnesota Daily](https://mndaily.com/239129/uncategorized/oromo-people-seek-more-recognition/)). Every later 40,000 found here either names no source or traces back to the institute or to MNopedia, and none rests on newer data. So the honest answer for 2026 is a range of roughly 8,500 to 40,000 as of 2016, with MNopedia's "tens of thousands" as the most careful current wording. The firmest current number is a floor, not a total: the 3,297 schoolchildren described below.

The nearest thing to a current public measure covers a wider group. Minnesota Compass reports 53,754 people of Ethiopian background for 2024, give or take 12,826, and 45,159 for 2020 to 2024, give or take 5,404. It has no Oromo category and does not mention Oromo people. Compass says the Census Bureau began in 2024 to collect data in ways that report Black and white respondents' ancestry more accurately, and it treats the 2024 total as its best estimate of size ([Minnesota Compass, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)).

Schools offer the firmest count, though of children only. In 2024-25 Minnesota schools reported 3,297 students whose primary home language is "Oromo, Afan Oromo, Oromiffa," of whom 1,568 were English learners, up from 1,194 English learners in 2020. Among the 12 languages in that table, Oromo was sixth, ahead of Vietnamese (3,071) and Amharic (2,379) ([Minnesota Department of Education, English Learners in Minnesota Report 2024-25, via Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/other/261381.pdf)). A home language is not an ethnicity. A child in an Oromo family who mostly speaks English at home would not be counted here.

**Why the count is so hard.** Sources name several reasons:

- *No box to check.* Girma Hassen of the Oromo Cultural Institute said in 2016 that the census should let people identify as Oromo rather than only as Ethiopian ([Minnesota Daily, June 1, 2016](https://mndaily.com/239129/uncategorized/oromo-people-seek-more-recognition/)).
- *Ratios stand in for counts.* State demographer Susan Brower explained in that article that the state began from about 18,000 Minnesotans identifying as Ethiopian and applied ratios to reach its Oromo figure. She said the gap could also reflect disinterest in or distrust of the census, and that the census often undercounts minorities ([Minnesota Daily](https://mndaily.com/239129/uncategorized/oromo-people-seek-more-recognition/)).
- *Broad labels.* Wilder Research wrote that the "East African" label makes Oromo people harder to see, and community leaders told its researchers that this adds to a lack of knowledge about them ([Wilder Research, July 2016](https://www.wilder.org/wp-content/pdf-file/OromoCommunityAssessment_7-16.pdf)). Nurse and organizer Aisha Oromia Ali told Sahan Journal that people "either lump you as a Somali or as an Ethiopian" ([Sahan Journal, July 17, 2020](https://sahanjournal.com/culture-community/hachalu-hundessa-oromo-protest-ethiopia-minnesota)).
- *Getting in the door.* When volunteers surveyed Cedar-Riverside in 2016, the institute's president reported that some residents refused and some landlords blocked access to buildings ([Minnesota Daily](https://mndaily.com/239129/uncategorized/oromo-people-seek-more-recognition/)).
- *Language tables.* The Census Bureau codes more than 1,300 languages and folds less common ones into broader groups, Sahan Journal reported in 2025 ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 22, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/minnesota-non-english-languages-census-data/)). The Bureau's most detailed published language table for Minnesota, for 2009 to 2013, shows Oromo only inside "Cushite," a group that also holds Somali: 38,135 speakers age 5 and older, give or take 2,032 ([U.S. Census Bureau, Table 24, released October 2015](https://www.census.gov/data/tables/2013/demo/2009-2013-lang-tables.html)). The Bureau does give Oromo its own code (4830) in its public-use survey microdata ([Census Bureau API, ACS 2019-2023 language codes](https://api.census.gov/data/2023/acs/acs5/pums/variables/LANP.json)), so a count could in principle be drawn from those records, but no published table giving one was found.
- *Race and ancestry tables.* The 2020 Census detailed tables list "Ethiopian" and "Somali" as groups but have no Oromo group ([Census Bureau API, 2020 Detailed DHC-A group list](https://api.census.gov/data/2020/dec/ddhca/variables/POPGROUP.json)).

It is also fair to ask what each number leaves out. Someone who answers "Ethiopian," or a child born in a Kenyan camp, may be counted somewhere else entirely. No source found measures how large that group is.

**Where.** The Twin Cities are known among Oromo people as "Little Oromia" ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)). Compass puts 89.7 percent of the wider Ethiopian group in the Twin Cities and 10.3 percent in Greater Minnesota, give or take 4 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). The most useful map of Oromo families specifically comes from English learners in 2024-25: 492 in Ramsey County, 449 in Anoka, 312 in Hennepin, 143 in Dakota, 94 in Washington, 10 in Stearns, and fewer than 10 each in Olmsted and Scott ([Minnesota Department of Education](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/other/261381.pdf)). That shows only children still learning English, but it suggests a community reaching well past the old neighborhoods into the northern suburbs.

Named places match that picture: St. Paul's Oromo Community of Minnesota center at 465 North Mackubin Street ([Oromo Community of Minnesota](https://oromocommunitymn.org/)); Cedar-Riverside in Minneapolis, where 4th Street South between Cedar and 15th avenues also carries the honorary name Oromo Street ([The Southerner, Dec. 9, 2015](https://shsoutherner.net/news/2015/12/09/cedar-riverside-streets-add-commemorative-names-to-celebrate-neighborhoods-culture)); a school in Columbia Heights ([Bultum Academy](https://www.bultumacademy.org/theoromolanguage)); a mosque serving Fridley and nearby cities ([Bilal Oromo Dawa Center](https://bilalodc.wixsite.com/bilalodcenter)); and a church in Robbinsdale ([Oromo Evangelical Church of Minnesota](https://oecmn.org/about-us)).

**How people came.** MNopedia dates the start to 1972. Tasissa Moti arrived around late August with his American wife, Geri, a former missionary nurse, and Teferi Fufa came in early September to attend college. Lutheran missionary ties drew these early arrivals. After Ethiopia's 1974 military coup, three more students arrived. Refugees then fled to Somalia, Djibouti and Sudan, and in 1981 Oromo people in the Twin Cities founded the Oromo Relief Association of North America to find them sponsors. Lutheran Social Service, Lutheran World Relief and the International Institute of Minnesota helped with resettlement, and by the mid-1980s more than 200 Oromo people lived here. A second, much larger wave followed 1991 and 1992. MNopedia says the Oromo Liberation Front was forced out of Ethiopia's new government in 1992 and persecution followed; many went first to Kenya ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)). One family's path, reported in 2024: eight years in a Kenyan camp before reaching the U.S. in 1999 ([Sahan Journal, April 22, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/changing-minnesota/welcome-corps-minnesota-refugee-sponsor-groups/)).

**Generations and languages.** The wider Ethiopian group is young: 43.8 percent are 17 or younger and 3.5 percent are 65 or older, and 59.8 percent are foreign-born ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). Afaan Oromo was barred from Ethiopia's schools until 1991 ([University of Minnesota, Feb. 17, 2026](https://brief.umn.edu/feature/reviving-once-endangered-language)). Student organizer Najat Hamza told Sahan Journal in 2020 that written Oromo is recent, so the older generation passed culture along orally, while younger people were schooled both at home and in the diaspora ([Sahan Journal](https://sahanjournal.com/culture-community/hachalu-hundessa-oromo-protest-ethiopia-minnesota)). In 2005 a community member already worried that the second generation's Oromo would blend with English ([MPR News, May 18, 2005](https://news.minnesota.publicradio.org/features/2005/04/27_hughesa_oromo/)). By 2026 the University of Minnesota was describing many Twin Cities children as not learning the language ([University of Minnesota](https://brief.umn.edu/feature/reviving-once-endangered-language)).

## 2. All walks of life

No Minnesota table of income, wealth or home ownership for Oromo people could be found. The figures below describe everyone Compass counts as Ethiopian for 2020 to 2024, in 2024 dollars, and that group includes people of other Ethiopian backgrounds ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). They give a sense of range, not an Oromo portrait.

- **Wealth and success.** Tashitaa Tufaa came as a refugee in 1992, worked as a dishwasher, security guard and factory worker, and now owns Metropolitan Transportation Network, which Carnegie Corporation called a multimillion-dollar company when it honored him as a Great Immigrant in 2022 ([Carnegie Corporation](https://carnegie.org/great-immigrants/tashitaa-tufaa/)). Kraus-Anderson says he grew up on a subsistence farm in Ethiopia's Oromo region ([Kraus-Anderson, undated, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://www.krausanderson.com/blog/metropolitan-transportation-network/)). Sahan Journal calls him an Oromo philanthropist, and described Abdul Dire as a Minnesota tech leader and philanthropist ([Sahan Journal, Feb. 3, 2021](https://sahanjournal.com/oromo-in-minnesota/oromia-11-tv-network-minnesota/); [Sahan Journal, Aug. 20, 2020](https://sahanjournal.com/arts/bale-oromo-witness-ethiopia/)). In the wider Ethiopian group, 23.2 percent of households take in $100,000 or more, give or take 6 points.
- **Professionals and business owners.** Among adults 25 and older in the wider group, 24.1 percent hold a bachelor's degree or more and 8.1 percent a graduate degree. Oromo people in the public record include University of Northwestern professor and novelist Teferi Tafa ([Ampers, Oct. 16, 2025](https://ampers.org/celebrating-irreechaa-in-minnesota/)), and a registered nurse active in 2020 organizing ([Sahan Journal](https://sahanjournal.com/culture-community/hachalu-hundessa-oromo-protest-ethiopia-minnesota)). The software engineer behind a bakery cart with an Oromo name shows the same mix of careers ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 17, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/minnesota-mobile-cafes-women-entrepreneurs-oromo-somali/)). The Oromo Culture Institute's chair described the community to lawmakers as including business owners, organization leaders, nurses and physicians ([letter to a Minnesota legislative committee, March 24, 2022](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/archive/minutes/senate/2022/Jobs/20220330/Jobs_20220330_SF4091-Handout-OromoCultureInsitituteofMinnesota.pdf)).
- **Middle-income and working families.** Median household income in the wider group is $63,667, give or take $6,426, and average household size is 3.30. Employment among adults 18 to 64 is 78.9 percent. Owner-occupied homes are 35.6 percent of households, give or take 6.7 points, so most households rent. Another 34.5 percent of people live above the poverty line but below twice that line.
- **People in poverty and hardship.** In the wider group, 10.9 percent live below the poverty line, give or take 3.5 points, including 10.4 percent of children. Among renters, 41.9 percent spend too much of their income on housing, and 20.5 percent of adults 25 and older did not finish high school. The one Oromo-specific measure is small and older. In 2016, 86 Oromo residents of Cedar-Riverside answered a survey given door to door in Riverside Plaza and at churches and a mosque; most were 35 or older and almost all were born abroad. Of them, 56 percent disagreed that their money covered food, housing, transportation and other basic needs ([Wilder Research](https://www.wilder.org/wp-content/pdf-file/OromoCommunityAssessment_7-16.pdf)). That describes one neighborhood ten years ago, not the state.

Mpls.St.Paul Magazine reported in 2021 that more than 80 percent of Ethiopians in the state were employed, many in professional jobs in health, education, finance and government ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/looking-back-at-minnesotas-refugee-history/)).

## 3. Work and the economy

MNopedia calls the Oromo contribution to Minnesota's economy "unquantified but significant," naming shops, restaurants, law firms, clinics, transportation companies, taxi driving and wage jobs, plus the spending that weddings and gatherings bring ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)). No occupation or industry table specific to Oromo people could be found, and Compass reports none for the wider Ethiopian group.

One company stands out in the record. Metropolitan Transportation Network, based in Fridley, reports service to Minneapolis, Hopkins, Edina, Brooklyn Center and North St. Paul districts ([MTN, published Oct. 22, 2026](https://metrotn.com)). Kraus-Anderson's undated profile gives about 300 buses, more than 300 employees and about 15,000 daily passengers ([Kraus-Anderson](https://www.krausanderson.com/blog/metropolitan-transportation-network/)). Red Wing's school board approved a three-year contract with the company on March 24, 2026 ([Republican Eagle, March 27, 2026](https://www.republicaneagle.com/news/red-wing-school-board-signs-transportation-contract-with-mtn-heres-why/article_05c3795f-a24b-4ddd-ad79-db2138b280d2.html)).

Sahan Journal described Tufaa in 2022 as "a well-known transportation business owner in the Twin Cities Oromo community" and reported that he served as a fundraising mentor to congressional candidate Amane Badhasso ([Sahan Journal, July 27, 2022](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/amane-badhasso-betty-mccollum-congress-fourth-district-primary-minnesota/)).

Younger entrepreneurs often start small, with family recipes and borrowed space. Red Wolf Chai began in June 2021 at the Fulton and Nokomis farmers markets, run by brothers Azhar Abdusebur and Mayzer Muhammad with friend Mowafag Mohamed; the chai and chapati recipes come from the brothers' mother, who is Oromo and moved to Minnesota from Ethiopia in the 1990s ([Sahan Journal, July 8, 2021](https://sahanjournal.com/food/red-wolf-chai-minneapolis/)). Its own site sells chai online ([Red Wolf Chai, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://redwolfchai.com/)), and Meet Minneapolis names coffee shops in both cities that serve it ([Meet Minneapolis, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.minneapolis.org/food-drink/international/east-african/)). Sahan Journal also profiled Chaltu Hassan's Mi'aawaa Bakery and Coffee Cart in a story on East African women's mobile cafes ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 17, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/minnesota-mobile-cafes-women-entrepreneurs-oromo-somali/)). Oromo Diaspora Media reported in March 2025 that a large market called Oromo Mall had opened in St. Paul (see section 4) ([Oromo Diaspora Media, March 30, 2025](https://oromodiasporamedia.org/oromo-mall-minisootaa-keessatti-hojii-jalqabe/)).

Language shapes work, too. In the 2016 Cedar-Riverside survey, 34 percent of the 86 respondents said they had trouble communicating at their job ([Wilder Research](https://www.wilder.org/wp-content/pdf-file/OromoCommunityAssessment_7-16.pdf)). Anole Sisters, a group for Oromo and other East African women founded in 2016 by Rufo Jiru, says 86 of its members have completed a six-week women's health certificate through the International Institute of Minnesota since 2022, and that others use the institute's nursing assistant program and English classes; it is building job-search training for girls and young women ages 15 to 25 ([Mary's Pence, undated, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://maryspence.org/stories/amplifying-the-voices-of-east-african-women/)). The Oromo Community of Minnesota runs job counseling, applications, placement and follow-up ([Oromo Community of Minnesota](https://oromocommunitymn.org/)).

The Oromo Culture Institute told lawmakers in 2022 that it was working with a state assistant commissioner on health care workforce shortages ([letter, March 24, 2022](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/archive/minutes/senate/2022/Jobs/20220330/Jobs_20220330_SF4091-Handout-OromoCultureInsitituteofMinnesota.pdf)). A 2013 University of Minnesota dissertation on how cultural capital shapes Oromo working life is held in the university's library, but its text could not be opened. No source was found on union membership, labor organizing, or the industries where Oromo Minnesotans work in the largest numbers.

## 4. Businesses and commercial life

This list is short, and that reflects how little could be confirmed rather than how much exists. MNopedia speaks of shops, restaurants, law firms and clinics without naming them. No Oromo-owned law firm, tax or accounting office, travel agency, salon or clinic could be named from a current public source; Oromo Diaspora Media's business section carries only a few Minnesota business notices ([Oromo Diaspora Media, Business section, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://oromodiasporamedia.org/category/daldala/)). Ethiopian-labeled restaurants should not be assumed to be Oromo-owned: a 2025 Mpls.St.Paul Magazine guide names many Ethiopian places, and none is identified as Oromo ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, updated Feb. 25, 2025](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/black-owned-businesses-in-the-twin-cities/)).

- **Metropolitan Transportation Network**, Fridley: school and adult transportation. Its site was published October 22, 2026 ([MTN](https://metrotn.com)).
- **Mi'aawaa Bakery and Coffee Cart**, mobile, with an owner from Brooklyn Center: lattes, cookies and Eid boxes. The name means "sweet" in Oromo ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 17, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/minnesota-mobile-cafes-women-entrepreneurs-oromo-somali/)). Mpls.St.Paul Magazine also listed it among 2025's best new food spots ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/best-new-restaurants-twin-cities-2025/)).
- **Arya Café**, at Nokomis Avenue and 46th Street, Minneapolis: opened August 2025 with what the magazine calls "Oromian-inflected flavors," such as a clove-and-date matcha. The article does not name the owner ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/eat-and-drink/best-new-restaurants-twin-cities-2025/)).
- **Mediterranean Deli**, Cedar Avenue: Meet Minneapolis calls it "Oromo-owned" and a longtime staple serving Ethiopian and Middle Eastern food ([Meet Minneapolis, undated](https://www.minneapolis.org/cultural-districts/districts/cedar-avenue-south/)). It could not be confirmed open; the West Bank Business Association's retail list does not include it ([West Bank Business Association, copyright 2025](https://www.thewestbank.org/retail)), and the website at mediterraneandeli.com belongs to an unrelated North Carolina restaurant.
- **Sainaa Café**, 317 West Lake Street, Uptown: a family-run Ethiopian coffee shop named for an Oromo word meaning "come inside" and "memories," new in 2022 ([Meet Minneapolis, Sept. 27, 2022](https://www.minneapolis.org/media/news-releases/whats-new-in-minneapolis/)). Its own site lists a menu with prices, Tuesday-to-Saturday hours and online ordering ([Sainaa Cafe, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.sainaacafe.com/)).
- **Red Wolf Chai**, Minneapolis: chai and chapatis from the founders' Oromo mother's recipes, started at farmers markets in 2021 and named for the Ethiopian wolf of Oromia's Bale Highlands ([Sahan Journal, July 8, 2021](https://sahanjournal.com/food/red-wolf-chai-minneapolis/); [Red Wolf Chai](https://redwolfchai.com/)). Meet Minneapolis features it to "highlight the rich Oromo culture in the Twin Cities" ([Meet Minneapolis](https://www.minneapolis.org/food-drink/international/east-african/)).
- **Oromo Mall**, 1001 Westgate Drive, St. Paul: described by Oromo Diaspora Media in March 2025 as a large Oromo market opening for the first time in Minnesota. The post links to the Tawhid Islamic Center of Minnesota's page beside the address ([Oromo Diaspora Media, March 30, 2025](https://oromodiasporamedia.org/oromo-mall-minisootaa-keessatti-hojii-jalqabe/)). No 2026 notice was found.
- **Adama Restaurant and Awash Bakery**, 3970 Central Avenue NE, Columbia Heights: Heavy Table reported in 2017 that new owners had turned the space into an Ethiopian restaurant "with a focus on the food of the Oromo people" ([Heavy Table, November 2017](https://heavytable.com/adama-ethiopian-columbia-heights/)). A restaurant directory updated in August 2026 lists daily hours, but it is an unclaimed listing ([Franki](https://frankiapp.com/business/adama-restaurant-and-awash-bakery-770454.md)). It could not be confirmed open.
- **Northside Grocery and Deli**, 125th Avenue NE, Blaine: advertised on Oromo Diaspora Media in June 2025 as an "African Business" and neighborhood store. The notice does not say who owns it ([Oromo Diaspora Media, June 19, 2025](https://oromodiasporamedia.org/northside-grocery-and-deli-african-business-in-blaine/)).
- **Community Link Services MN**, Twin Cities: a service provider announced to Oromo and other diaspora residents in October 2026 ([Oromo Diaspora Media, Oct. 8, 2026](https://oromodiasporamedia.org/tajaajila-community-link-services-mn-awol-windissa/)).
- **Oromia Health Care LLC**, Spring Lake Park: home care listing current to August 2026. The listing does not say who owns it or which languages staff speak ([MN Senior Guide](https://mnseniorguide.com/providers/oromia-health-care-llc)). It could not be confirmed open.

Media outlets are in section 8. The Oromo Community of Minnesota rents its building for gatherings, baby showers and engagement parties ([Oromo Community of Minnesota](https://oromocommunitymn.org/)).

## 5. Religion and spiritual life

The Oromo congregations that can be documented in Minnesota are Muslim and Christian, and people of different faiths share one community. An older Oromo faith, Waaqeffanna, is much harder to see here. MPR News reported in 2005 that about half of Oromo people, in Ethiopia and the U.S., are Muslim and the rest mainly Christian, without giving a source ([MPR News, May 18, 2005](https://news.minnesota.publicradio.org/features/2005/04/27_hughesa_oromo/)). No current Minnesota count of faith could be found: the 2016 Cedar-Riverside survey does not report respondents' faiths, and no state or university study that does so was found. The Wilder survey went door to door and also to "a number of churches and a mosque" used by Oromo residents, which shows both faiths in one small neighborhood but does not measure their shares ([Wilder Research](https://www.wilder.org/wp-content/pdf-file/OromoCommunityAssessment_7-16.pdf)). Faith also shows up as a source of strength: a University of Minnesota study built on 76 interviews with Somali and Oromo refugees in one Minnesota neighborhood named religion and religious practice first among the communities' cultural health assets ([Lightfoot et al., Journal of Health Care for the Poor and Underserved, 2016](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/cultural-health-assets-of-somali-and-oromo-refugees-and-immigrant/)).

**Muslim Oromo life.** Muslim Oromo people opened Masjid Tawfiq in Minneapolis in 2004, which MNopedia calls the first Oromo mosque in North America; it was renamed the Tawfiq Islamic Center in 2006 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)). In 2016 it was building out space at Minnehaha Avenue and 24th Street, with a prayer room for about 450, classrooms, a gym, a community room, and rooms to prepare the dead for funerals. Executive Director Oumer M. Wako said then that the center is primarily Oromo but serves Muslims of any nationality. The same article says Oromo Muslims arriving from the early 1990s founded centers in Fridley, North Minneapolis and St. Paul that work together ([Southside Pride, May 16, 2016](https://southsidepride.com/?p=3771)). The Augsburg Oromo Student Union listed an iftar volunteer night at the center for March 26, 2025 ([Augsburg University](https://calendar.augsburg.edu/event/aosu-x-tawfiq-islamic-center-masjid-volunteering/)). Bilal Oromo Dawa Center, at 7200 University Avenue NE, offers Qur'an learning and a madrasa ([Bilal Oromo Dawa Center](https://bilalodc.wixsite.com/bilalodcenter)). An Oromo Diaspora Media notice about Oromo Mall in St. Paul links to the Tawhid Islamic Center of Minnesota alongside the address 1001 Westgate Drive ([Oromo Diaspora Media, March 30, 2025](https://oromodiasporamedia.org/oromo-mall-minisootaa-keessatti-hojii-jalqabe/)); whether that center has an Oromo congregation was not established.

**Christian Oromo life.** Bethany Lutheran Church in south Minneapolis welcomed early arrivals, both Christian and Muslim ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)). Named congregations today:

- **Our Redeemer Oromo Evangelical Church**, 4000 28th Avenue South, Minneapolis: began as a 1987 Friday Bible study started by students at Luther Seminary and Augsburg College, incorporated in 1993, and joined the ELCA's Minneapolis Area Synod in 1995. It calls itself a "mother church" for diaspora Oromo churches and houses the office of the United Oromo Evangelical Churches. Sunday service is 10:30 a.m. ([OROEC, about](https://oroec.org/about); [OROEC](https://oroec.org/)).
- **Oromo Evangelical Church of Minnesota**, 4201 Regent Avenue North, Robbinsdale: an autonomous Lutheran church worshipping in Afaan Oromo, incorporated October 30, 2019, with 176 adults and 120 children registered at the start ([OECMN](https://oecmn.org/about-us)).
- **Bet'el Afaan Oromo Evangelical Church**, 2120 East 38th Street, Minneapolis: part of Converge North Central ([Bet'el](https://betelmn.org/contact-us/); [Converge North Central](https://convergenorthcentral.org/church/betel-afaan-oromo-evangelical-church/)).
- **Abenezer Oromo Evangelical Church**: named as a partner by Oromo Diaspora Media; no address could be confirmed ([Oromo Diaspora Media](https://oromodiasporamedia.org/partners/)).

**A real disagreement.** The Robbinsdale church's own history says disagreement over same-sex marriage within the ELCA spilled over into the Our Redeemer congregation, and the new church states its belief that marriage is between a man and a woman ([OECMN](https://oecmn.org/about-us)). That is one congregation's account. Our Redeemer's page lists the ELCA synod and does not discuss it ([OROEC, about](https://oroec.org/about)).

**Across faiths.** The Tawfiq center's director said in 2016 that it worked with Oromo Christians on human rights concerns in Ethiopia ([Southside Pride](https://southsidepride.com/?p=3771)).

**Waaqeffanna.** This is the older Oromo faith, centered on one creator, Waaqaa, and ways of living in truth and peace, as a Waaqeffannaa website describes it (background, not about Minnesota: [Waaqeffannaa.com](https://www.waaqeffannaa.com/waaqeffannaa/waaqeffannaa-in-brief/)). No Minnesota congregation, gathering place, leader or count could be found. Only faint hints appear. MNopedia mentions praise and thanks to "Waaqaa (God)" at Irreecha but does not name the faith ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)). A 2009 report from Lake Nokomis used the word "Waaqefataa" for followers and called it a misconception that the holiday belongs only to them ([OPride, Oct. 3, 2009](https://www.opride.com/2009/10/03/irreeechaa-in-minnesota-a-tradition-continues/)). A University of Minnesota project listed "Oromo traditionalists" among its collaborators, a term it does not define ([CURA](https://www.cura.umn.edu/project/gathering-under-odaa-tree-honoring-traditional-forms-oromo-art-governance)). No Minnesota congregation, prayer site, organization, incorporation or event listing that names Waaqeffanna could be found, and the Waaqeffannaa website cited above gives no Minnesota contact. Whether followers gather privately here, or how many there are, remains unknown, and the absence of a public listing is not evidence that no one practices. No source addressed Oromo Minnesotans who hold no faith.

## 6. Celebrations and observances

**Irreecha (also Irreechaa).** A thanksgiving at the end of the rainy season, with prayers for peace and reconciliation, led by elders or spiritual leaders at water or on high ground ([OPride, 2009](https://www.opride.com/2009/10/03/irreeechaa-in-minnesota-a-tradition-continues/)). Ethiopia banned it for over a century, MNopedia says, and violence at the October 2016 celebration killed 55 people by the government's count and about 700 by other claims ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)). Ampers reported in 2025 that novelist and professor Teferi Tafa calls it "Oromo Thanksgiving," volunteer Talile Gobena calls it a symbol of resilience, and board member Tolcha Gemechu credits young people with making it popular. People wear cultural clothing, sing, dance and share badenna, a sour flatbread ([Ampers, Oct. 16, 2025](https://ampers.org/celebrating-irreechaa-in-minnesota/)).

Two dates in the year, and more than one organizer:

- **Irreechaa Tulluu, May 2, 2026, Powderhorn Park.** A ceremony traditionally held on mountaintops, smaller than the September one, marking the end of the dry season, as posted by the Irreechaa Organization ([irreechaa.org, Aug. 9, 2026](https://irreechaa.org/irreechaa-tulluu-2026/)).
- **Irreechaa Malkaa, September 19, 2026, Powderhorn Park, 3400 15th Ave. S., 10:30 a.m. to 7 p.m.** Organizer: Oromo Irreecha Minnesota ([irreechaa.org](https://irreechaa.org/event/ireechaa-by-the-water-sept-19-2026-at-powderhorn-park-lake-in-minneapolis/)). Oromo Diaspora Media reported afterward that it was held at Powderhorn Park and thanked an organizing committee, the Minnesota Humanities Center and the City of Minneapolis ([Oromo Diaspora Media, Sept. 21, 2026](https://oromodiasporamedia.org/ayyaana-irreechaa-mn-fulbaana-19-2026/)).
- **Irreecha at Lake Nokomis, also reported for September 19, 2026.** A write-up on an Australian advocacy website says Hawaasa Oromoo Minnesota organized a celebration at Lake Nokomis that day ([Advocacy for Oromia, Sept. 20, 2026](https://advocacy4oromia.org/2026/09/20/minnesota-oromo-community-celebrates-irreecha-with-gratitude-unity-and-hope/)). This is one lone report, and it conflicts with the two sources above that place the day's event at Powderhorn Park. No second source for Lake Nokomis could be found. MNopedia names Lake Nokomis as the usual site, so this may be a second gathering or a mistaken place. It remains unsettled.

The 2009 report describes a celebration on October 3 at Lake Nokomis ([OPride](https://www.opride.com/2009/10/03/irreeechaa-in-minnesota-a-tradition-continues/)). Oromo Diaspora Media reported a 2025 Minnesota celebration in a post dated October 6, 2025, without giving a day or park ([Oromo Diaspora Media](https://oromodiasporamedia.org/irreechaa-oromoo-minisootaa/)). No 2027 dates could be found.

**Oromo Week and the OSFNA tournament.** The Oromo Sports Federation in North America holds a week of soccer, music, vendors and nightlife. Its 2026 tournament, marking 30 years, was listed for August 1 to 8 in St. Paul ([Oromo Community of Georgia listing](https://oromoga.org/)), with a family festival at Central High School ([Eventbrite](https://www.eventbrite.com/e/osfna-30-year-anniversary-celebration-sports-unity-family-festival-tickets-1993887127071)) and the final at Allianz Field on August 8 ([Allianz Field](https://www.allianzfield.com/events/osfna-2026)). The tournament rotates among North American cities, with Minnesota as its organizational home ([OSFNA, Jan. 1, 2026](https://osfna.org/2026/01/01/culture-and-athletic/)). MNopedia says St. Paul first hosted in 2011 and that both cities' mayors declared "Oromo Week" ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)).

**Gubaa.** Oromo Diaspora Media reports that "Ayyaana Gubaa" was celebrated September 17, 2026, in Minneapolis, organized by a group it calls Kutaa Bulchiinsa MN. The post does not say what the day marks ([Oromo Diaspora Media, Sept. 21, 2026](https://oromodiasporamedia.org/ayyaanni-gubaa-fulbaana-17-2026-minisootaatti-kabajame/)).

**Ramadan and Eid.** The Oromo Community of Minnesota listed a Ramadan iftar for March 21, 2025, at its St. Paul office ([Oromo Community of Minnesota](https://oromocommunitymn.org/events/)), and Augsburg's Oromo Student Union sought volunteers to serve iftar at Tawfiq on March 26, 2025 ([Augsburg University](https://calendar.augsburg.edu/event/aosu-x-tawfiq-islamic-center-masjid-volunteering/)). No Oromo-specific Eid prayer schedule could be found. One bakery owner began with Eid boxes for customers ([Sahan Journal](https://sahanjournal.com/business-work/minnesota-mobile-cafes-women-entrepreneurs-oromo-somali/)).

**Other gatherings.** The Oromo Studies Association, founded in 1986, held its annual conference in Minneapolis from July 31 to August 2, 2026 ([Oromo Studies Association](https://oromostudies.org/)). Oromo Minnesotans first took part in St. Paul's Festival of Nations in April 1982 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)); current participation was not confirmed. The Oromo Community of Minnesota also listed a July 4 barbecue and a Black History Month event at Rondo Community Library in 2025 ([Oromo Community of Minnesota](https://oromocommunitymn.org/events/)). A memorial for the singer Ilfinash Qannoo was held in Minnesota, Oromo Diaspora Media reported in September 2026 ([Oromo Diaspora Media](https://oromodiasporamedia.org/yaadannoo-fi-sirni-gaggeessaa-weellistuu-fi-qabsooftuu-ilfinash-qannoo-minisootaatti-taasifame/)).

No Minnesota Oromo observance could be confirmed for the Ethiopian Orthodox calendar days (Enkutatash, Genna, Timkat, Fasika) or Adwa. They may be kept by some families, but no source says so.

## 7. Family, home and daily life

Sources on daily Oromo family life here are few. The wider Ethiopian group's average household has 3.30 people, and 43.8 percent of people are children ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). In the 2016 Cedar-Riverside survey of 86 people described in section 2, about six in ten agreed their housing was big enough for their family ([Wilder Research](https://www.wilder.org/wp-content/pdf-file/OromoCommunityAssessment_7-16.pdf)).

**Young people and elders** were a particular concern in that survey. About three-quarters disagreed that Oromo youth were doing well, and of those, 73 percent said young people were not connected with Oromo culture and language. And 79 percent disagreed that elders were doing well, most often naming the need for language help and a place to gather. Several said Oromo people are often mistaken for Somali, with language services offered in Somali but not Oromo ([Wilder Research](https://www.wilder.org/wp-content/pdf-file/OromoCommunityAssessment_7-16.pdf)). The Oromo Community of Minnesota's seniors program now offers socializing, interpretation, rides, home visits and help with clinic appointments ([Oromo Community of Minnesota](https://oromocommunitymn.org/)).

**Food and gathering.** Ampers describes badenna at Irreecha ([Ampers](https://ampers.org/celebrating-irreechaa-in-minnesota/)). The coffee ceremony appears at community events such as the 2025 Under the Odaa Tree show ([Sahan Journal](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/twin-cities-things-to-do-oromo-nigerian-hmong-art/)), and a University of Minnesota project links it with poetry and basket weaving ([CURA](https://www.cura.umn.edu/project/gathering-under-odaa-tree-honoring-traditional-forms-oromo-art-governance)). The Odaa, the sycamore on the Oromo flag, stands for a place where people decide matters, settle disputes and hear elders ([Sahan Journal](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/twin-cities-things-to-do-oromo-nigerian-hmong-art/)).

**Growing up.** One University of Minnesota student told Power 104.7 she grew up without Oromo community around her and began learning about her culture in high school ([Power 104.7 FM, Feb. 24, 2023](https://www.power1047.fm/post/oromo-minnesotans-embrace-oromummaa-in-quest-to-connect-to-their-culture)). The 2020 killing of the singer Hachalu Hundessa, also spelled Haacaaluu Hundeessaa, pushed her to study her heritage, and the piece describes Oromummaa, the idea that all Oromo people are connected by shared identity ([Power 104.7 FM](https://www.power1047.fm/post/oromo-minnesotans-embrace-oromummaa-in-quest-to-connect-to-their-culture)). One student's story is not a generation's, and no source measures how common that path is.

**Women's networks.** Anole Sisters, founded in 2016 by Rufo Jiru, builds community and support for Oromo women, and East African women more broadly, offering interpretation and help reaching immigration assistance, health care, housing, food and support for families raising children with special needs. At a gathering hosted by The Woman's Club of Minneapolis, members cooked Oromo dishes, "brewed coffee in the authentic Oromo manner" and danced, the group's funder Mary's Pence wrote ([Mary's Pence, undated, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://maryspence.org/stories/amplifying-the-voices-of-east-african-women/)).

**Home kitchens.** Red Wolf Chai's founder told Sahan Journal that after school his Oromo mother made dabo shahee, bread dipped in chai, and the children ate it together on the living room floor ([Sahan Journal, July 8, 2021](https://sahanjournal.com/food/red-wolf-chai-minneapolis/)). One family's memory, but a rare published glimpse of an Oromo Minnesota kitchen.

**One family's road here.** Amane Badhasso told Sahan Journal in 2022 that at about age 5 she, her 13 siblings and their father went to a refugee camp in Kenya, then lived for about eight years in city neighborhoods she called more dangerous than the camp, before reaching Minnesota at 13 ([Sahan Journal, July 27, 2022](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/amane-badhasso-betty-mccollum-congress-fourth-district-primary-minnesota/)). Another family's long stay in a Kenyan camp appears in section 1; no source measures how common such paths are.

**What families asked for.** Asked in 2016 for the one thing that would most improve their family's life, the most common answer among 84 Cedar-Riverside respondents was a community center (31 percent), followed by language services (20 percent), education (15 percent) and safe spaces (13 percent) ([Wilder Research](https://www.wilder.org/wp-content/pdf-file/OromoCommunityAssessment_7-16.pdf)).

Sources on marriage customs, dress, childhood practices and aging at home among Oromo Minnesotans were not found, beyond the church teaching noted in section 5.

## 8. Arts, media, sports and culture

**Visual art.** Under the Odaa Tree, curated by Lokho Kotile and Naima Badri, showed six emerging Oromo artists for one night at the Oromo Community of Minnesota on August 9, 2025, with music and dance from the Oromo Youth Association of Minnesota. Badri said Minnesota has few spaces for Oromo art ([Sahan Journal](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/twin-cities-things-to-do-oromo-nigerian-hmong-art/)). *Mara: 40 Years of Oromo Knowledge Making* ran August 1 to 16, 2026, at Soomaal House of Art in Minneapolis, with Kotile, Murata Wolldeyohannes and Agartuu Inor, organized around the Oromo idea of time and including Oromo women's ritual knowledge ([mplsart.com](https://www.mplsart.com/events/mara-40-years-of-oromo-knowledge-making); [Power 104.7 FM, Aug. 13](https://www.power1047.fm/post/what-is-time-a-minneapolis-exhibit-explores-the-oromo-understanding-of-it)).

**Music and spoken art.** A University of Minnesota project named Addisu Furgaasaa and Adinan Mohammed as masters of traditional oral art, alongside poet Fayise Abrahim ([CURA](https://www.cura.umn.edu/project/gathering-under-odaa-tree-honoring-traditional-forms-oromo-art-governance)). After singer Hachalu Hundessa was killed in Ethiopia in 2020, about 200 people from the community protested at the State Capitol ([Sahan Journal, June 30, 2020](https://sahanjournal.com/arts-culture/minnesota-oromo-community-demands-justice-after-musician-and-activist-hachalu-hundessa-killed-in-ethiopia/)).

**Writing.** *Oromo Witness*, by Minnesota's Abdul Dire, tells the story of Hangasu Waqo Lugo and the Bale uprising ([Sahan Journal, Aug. 20, 2020](https://sahanjournal.com/arts/bale-oromo-witness-ethiopia/)).

**Media.** KFAI carries *Voice of Oromiyaa* on Sundays from 5 to 6 p.m. ([KFAI schedule](https://www.kfai.org/schedule/)). Oromo Diaspora Media publishes mainly in Afaan Oromoo, with a heavy Minnesota focus and a radio stream, and lists the Minnesota Department of Human Services, the Minnesota Department of Health and Hennepin County among its partners ([Oromo Diaspora Media](https://oromodiasporamedia.org/); [partners](https://oromodiasporamedia.org/partners/)). Oromia Media Network is registered at 1437 Marshall Avenue in St. Paul, with $213,558 in 2024 revenue and one employee that year ([Cause IQ](https://www.causeiq.com/organizations/oromia-media-network,465137810/)). Oromia 11 launched from downtown Minneapolis studios in 2021; its current status was not confirmed ([Sahan Journal, Feb. 3, 2021](https://sahanjournal.com/oromo-in-minnesota/oromia-11-tv-network-minnesota/)). Ampers ran a 90-second history of Little Oromia in 2025 ([Ampers, Sept. 23, 2025](https://ampers.org/little-oromia/)).

**Sports.** The Oromo Sports Federation in North America began in 1996 in Toronto with six clubs, among them one from Minnesota ([OSFNA](https://osfna.org/2026/01/01/culture-and-athletic/)). Its 2026 final was scheduled for Allianz Field ([Allianz Field](https://www.allianzfield.com/events/osfna-2026)). The Ethiopian Sports Federation's tournament in Eagan, June 27 to July 4, 2026, is a separate event; MPR's report does not mention Oromo people ([MPR News, June 25, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/episode/2026/06/25/ethiopian-soccer-tournament-twin-cities)).

## 9. Civic and political life

**Organizations.** The Oromo Community of Minnesota began as a mutual aid group, was formally established in 1985, gained nonprofit status in 2000, bought its St. Paul center in 2008 and paid off the mortgage on August 31, 2013 ([CareerForce](https://careerforce.mn.gov/so/group/173/location/158)). Members paying at least $5 a month can vote for its board ([About](https://oromocommunitymn.org/about-us/)). Its current board page lists Leyla Abawari as president and Tuse Gemechu as vice president, and shows the women's affairs and elders' affairs seats vacant ([Team](https://oromocommunitymn.org/team/)). Its 2024 filing shows revenue of $241,434 against expenses of $282,326, a third straight year of loss ([ProPublica](https://projects.propublica.org/nonprofits/organizations/411727260)). Anole Sisters, founded in 2016, works with Oromo and other East African women (section 7) ([Mary's Pence](https://maryspence.org/stories/amplifying-the-voices-of-east-african-women/)). Others include the Oromo Culture Institute of Minnesota, called the Oromo Cultural Institute in 2016 reports and chaired by Girma Hassen ([letter, 2022](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/archive/minutes/senate/2022/Jobs/20220330/Jobs_20220330_SF4091-Handout-OromoCultureInsitituteofMinnesota.pdf)) and the Oromo Resource Center in Fridley, formed in 2020 and led by Arif Bakar as of late 2025 ([Cause IQ](https://www.causeiq.com/organizations/oromo-resource-center-of-minnesota,851090106/)).

**Elected office.** No Oromo person currently holding elected office in Minnesota could be confirmed. Oballa Oballa, who won an Austin City Council seat in 2020, was described as the first Ethiopian elected public official in Minnesota ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/looking-back-at-minnesotas-refugee-history/)) and is still listed on the council ([City of Austin](https://www.ci.austin.mn.us/city-council)). Sahan Journal says his family fled Gambella and does not name an ethnic group ([Sahan Journal](https://sahanjournal.com/election-2020/minnesota-austin-election-2020-oballa-oballa-city-council)), so he should not be counted as Oromo on this evidence.

The most visible Oromo bid for office was Amane Badhasso's 2022 challenge to Rep. Betty McCollum in the DFL primary for the Fourth Congressional District. Sahan Journal described her as "an Oromo refugee from Ethiopia" who would have been the first Oromo person in Congress; she had been president of the International Oromo Youth Association, graduated from Hamline University in 2015 and worked on campaigns for Keith Ellison and others ([Sahan Journal, July 27, 2022](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/amane-badhasso-betty-mccollum-congress-fourth-district-primary-minnesota/)). The Intercept reported that she raised more than $300,000 in late 2021 partly by canvassing the Oromo diaspora ([The Intercept, Jan. 10, 2022](https://theintercept.com/2022/01/10/betty-mccollum-minnesota-primary-challenge-amane-badhasso/)). Sahan Journal also reported that DFL delegates who signed up through her campaign, mostly East African and Muslim first-time participants, received calls and texts questioning their registration ([Sahan Journal](https://sahanjournal.com/democracy-politics/amane-badhasso-betty-mccollum-congress-fourth-district-primary-minnesota/)). In the August 9, 2022 primary she received 10,557 votes, 15.17 percent, to McCollum's 83.40 percent ([Minnesota Historical Election Archive, University of Minnesota](https://mn.electionarchives.lib.umn.edu/election/232022401011204)). In the 2016 survey, 67 percent disagreed that Minnesota's political leaders were advocating well for Oromo people ([Wilder Research](https://www.wilder.org/wp-content/pdf-file/OromoCommunityAssessment_7-16.pdf)).

**Advocacy.** MNopedia records that Rep. Keith Ellison held an April 19, 2016 hearing on H.R. 128 that more than a thousand Twin Cities Oromo people attended, and that thousands protested and cheered when Ethiopian Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed visited Target Center in 2018 ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)). After Hachalu Hundessa's killing, hundreds blocked Interstate 94 in St. Paul on July 2, 2020 ([Sahan Journal, July 17, 2020](https://sahanjournal.com/culture-community/hachalu-hundessa-oromo-protest-ethiopia-minnesota)). Sens. Tina Smith and Amy Klobuchar wrote to the State Department about two detained men with Minnesota ties that year, one of them Jawar Mohammed ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 2, 2020](https://sahanjournal.com/oromo-in-minnesota/minnesota-senators-jawar-mohammed-detained-ethiopia/)). Two Oromo refugees helped form a Welcome Corps group that sponsored a Congolese newcomer, a small story of paying it forward ([Sahan Journal, April 22, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/changing-minnesota/welcome-corps-minnesota-refugee-sponsor-groups/)). The state's elections director's office lists Oromo among the languages for voter guides, Sahan Journal reported ([Sahan Journal, Sept. 22, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/minnesota-non-english-languages-census-data/)).

**Debates inside the community.** In 2020, organizer Abdulrahman Wako argued that elders hold most of the power and should step back for younger people with technology and organizing skills ([Sahan Journal](https://sahanjournal.com/culture-community/hachalu-hundessa-oromo-protest-ethiopia-minnesota)). In 2016, 11 percent of Cedar-Riverside respondents named more Oromo people in leadership as the most helpful change ([Wilder Research](https://www.wilder.org/wp-content/pdf-file/OromoCommunityAssessment_7-16.pdf)). Some divisions from Ethiopia came along, at least as of 2005. MPR reported in 2005 that Oromo and Amharic speakers here could be distant toward each other, though not in open conflict ([MPR News, May 19, 2005](https://archive.mpr.org/stories/2005/05/19/ethiopians-diverse-group)); no recent measure was found. Fufa's MNopedia account suggests the Ethiopian consulate that opened in St. Paul in 2019 may also watch Oromo people; that is his view ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)).

## 10. Education

**K-12.** Bultum Academy, at 1555 40th Avenue NE in Columbia Heights, is a charter school teaching Afaan Oromo alongside regular subjects, aiming for students fluent in both languages. It calls itself "the first charter school in the United States to teach Afaan Oromo," and elsewhere on the same page the first in the world through immersion; those are the school's own claims, and no outside source for them was found ([Bultum Academy](https://www.bultumacademy.org/theoromolanguage)). It was advertising teaching jobs in October 2026 ([mnschooljobs.org](https://mnschooljobs.org/company/bultum-academy-charter-school/)). St. Paul Public Schools' East African Magnet at 437 Edmund Avenue West lists Oromo among six languages of focus ([East African Magnet](https://eastafricanmagnet.spps.org/)). It opened in fall 2023; a January 2024 report had enrollment of 203 and a principal hoping to deepen Somali and Arabic lessons, without mentioning Oromo ones ([Sahan Journal, Jan. 23, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/education/st-paul-schools-halt-enrollment-slide-hmong-spanish-east-african-programs/)). So whether Oromo is actually taught there each day could not be confirmed.

**College.** The University of Minnesota started an Oromo language pilot in 2025, Accelerated Beginning Oromo I and II, built by teaching specialist Bekele Dako and faculty member Bula Sirika Wayessa with support from the Oromo Student Union and families. Early funding, secured in 2023, included a federal grant. The university says it is the only R1 research university offering such a curriculum ([University of Minnesota, Feb. 17, 2026](https://brief.umn.edu/feature/reviving-once-endangered-language)). Before that, Minneapolis Community and Technical College announced Afaan Oromo I and II in 2013, taught by Mathias Gudina; whether it still runs was not confirmed ([TC Daily Planet, Aug. 20, 2013](https://www.tcdailyplanet.net/minneapolis-college-first-offer-afaan-oromo-us/)). Augsburg has an Oromo Student Union ([Augsburg University](https://calendar.augsburg.edu/event/aosu-x-tawfiq-islamic-center-masjid-volunteering/)).

**Interpreting.** The need is documented better than the supply. In the 2016 survey, 93 percent had trouble communicating because of language, 65 percent every day, and 94 percent had met that barrier at a hospital or clinic ([Wilder Research](https://www.wilder.org/wp-content/pdf-file/OromoCommunityAssessment_7-16.pdf)). The institute's president said many events had no Oromo translators ([Minnesota Daily, 2016](https://mndaily.com/239129/uncategorized/oromo-people-seek-more-recognition/)). Respondents said Somali services were offered where Oromo ones were not ([Wilder Research](https://www.wilder.org/wp-content/pdf-file/OromoCommunityAssessment_7-16.pdf)). According to LinguaOne, a commercial interpreting company, Minnesota has no certified court interpreters in Oromo, so courts use interpreters from the roster whose language skill has not been formally tested ([LinguaOne, checked Oct. 30, 2026](https://www.linguaone.com/court-interpreter-services-minnesota/)). That claim rests only on this vendor's page, which is unclear about its date; the Judicial Branch's own brochure could not be opened to confirm it. The Minnesota Department of Health posts materials under "Oromo (Afan Oromoo)" on topics from COVID-19 to mental health ([MDH, updated Oct. 2, 2026](https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/translation/oromo.html)).

**Scholars.** The Oromo Studies Association, founded in 1986, brought its 2026 conference to Minneapolis ([Oromo Studies Association](https://oromostudies.org/)). Teferi Fufa wrote MNopedia's history of the community ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)). Teferi Nigussie Tafa teaches at University of Northwestern ([OLLAA](https://ollaa.org/the-board)).

## 11. Health and wellbeing, broadly

No Minnesota health statistics specific to Oromo people could be found. Compass reports 7.3 percent of the wider Ethiopian group without health insurance, give or take 2.5 points ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). In the 2016 survey, 75 percent agreed their family could get good care when needed, yet hospitals and clinics ranked second, after their own apartment buildings, among the places they most needed to communicate easily ([Wilder Research](https://www.wilder.org/wp-content/pdf-file/OromoCommunityAssessment_7-16.pdf)).

**What people lived through.** A University of Minnesota team surveyed 1,134 Somali and Oromo refugees in Minnesota, and reported torture rates from 25 to 69 percent, depending on ethnicity and gender, higher than usually reported. The people were not randomly chosen ([Jaranson et al., American Journal of Public Health, 2004](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/somali-and-oromo-refugees-correlates-of-torture-and-trauma-histor/)). A follow-up on 458 of the women found more trauma among older women with large families, and urged public health nurses to look past medicine alone ([Robertson et al., Journal of Advanced Nursing, 2006](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/somali-and-oromo-refugee-women-trauma-and-associated-factors/)). This data is more than 20 years old and describes those who arrived then, not their children and not any one person.

**Smaller, more recent studies.** Each is small and none speaks for the whole community:

- *Preventive care.* A 2018 Bethel University physician assistant thesis surveyed Oromo immigrants in Minnesota in two languages. Finding time for an annual visit was the barrier that reached statistical significance. Among those who said they never or only sometimes find time, 46 percent had had no preventive care in five years, yet 92 percent had seen a clinic or hospital for an illness ([Gernand, Kingsley and Yadetta, Bethel University, 2018](https://spark.bethel.edu/etd/229)).
- *Viral hepatitis.* A Mayo Clinic-led community study held focus groups with 63 African immigrants in Minnesota who identified as Ethiopian-Amhara, Ethiopian-Oromo, Liberian or Kenyan. Participants knew little about hepatitis screening, vaccination and treatment, and described the illness by its symptoms; the Oromo term it reports is "birdheellee." Across the communities, the authors write, many elderly participants preferred traditional healers to doctors. The study does not report results for Oromo participants separately ([Mohamed et al., Frontiers in Public Health, 2020](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2020.00025/full)).
- *Strengths.* The 2016 University of Minnesota study of Somali and Oromo refugees noted in section 5 named a strong culture of sharing, oral tradition, traditional foods and medicine and a high value placed on health as assets to build on ([Lightfoot et al., 2016](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/cultural-health-assets-of-somali-and-oromo-refugees-and-immigrant/)).

**Who provides care.** The Oromo Community of Minnesota offers health navigation, insurance enrollment and health education ([Oromo Community of Minnesota](https://oromocommunitymn.org/)). Oromo Diaspora Media ran an Afaan Oromoo interview on mental health with a health professional in September 2026 ([Oromo Diaspora Media, Sept. 3, 2026](https://oromodiasporamedia.org/af-gaaffii-luba-fiqiruu-itichaa-ogeessa-fayyaa-waliin-dhimmoota-fayyaa-irratti-taasifnedha/)). The Oromo Community of Minnesota lists a November 28, 2026 gathering in New Brighton on health care in Oromia, proposed by Lammiif Relief & Development Organization ([Community events](https://oromocommunitymn.org/community-events/)).

WellShare International community health worker Debisaa Qanaatee (also spelled Debisa) leads "Learning to Breathe," a weekly mindfulness and stress-reduction class "designed specifically for the Oromo community," at the Oromo Community of Minnesota on Mondays; a session was listed for September 21, 2026 ([WellShare International, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://wellshareinternational.org/event/l2b-oromo-class-7/)). He also spoke about childhood vaccination in an Afaan Oromoo interview ([Oromo Diaspora Media, March 30, 2025](https://oromodiasporamedia.org/talaallii-daaimmanii-irratti-ogeessa-fayyaa-hawaasacwhwellshare-international-kan-tahe-obbo-debisaa-qanaatee/)). Anole Sisters reports 86 members certified through a women's health workshop since 2022 ([Mary's Pence](https://maryspence.org/stories/amplifying-the-voices-of-east-african-women/)). The Minnesota Department of Health's Oromo materials include hepatitis B information for pregnant women, tuberculosis testing and treatment, and mental health ([MDH](https://www.health.state.mn.us/communities/translation/oromo.html)).

No Oromo-owned clinic could be named. How traditional healing is practiced among Oromo Minnesotans was not documented beyond the hepatitis study's general remark about elders and healers.

## 12. Disability

**The number.** No disability figure for Oromo Minnesotans could be found. For the wider Ethiopian group, Compass reports 5.5 percent, give or take 1.5 points, and 4.5 percent under 65 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/ethiopian)). With 43.8 percent of the group being children and only 3.5 percent 65 or older, how much does that reflect age? How well does a survey question capture disability for someone whose first language is Afaan Oromo? Neither has been answered.

**Who gets seen.** The Minnesota Governor's Council on Developmental Disabilities' 2017 survey included 52 East African respondents: 62 percent born in Somalia, 35 percent in Ethiopia and 4 percent in Kenya. It never identifies Oromo respondents ([Governor's Council, 2017](https://mn.gov/mnddc/extra/customer-research/MN-Racial-Ethnic-Communities-Survey2017.txt)). So that survey cannot speak for Oromo families as a group. Could the same be true of autism monitoring and special education data? Those reports could not be opened, but no opened source reports Oromo children separately.

**What the word opens or closes.** The Autism Society of Minnesota wrote in 2023 that "there is no word for 'autism' in the Oromo language," right after Multicultural Autism Action Network's director, Maren Christenson, said that stigma around disability shows up differently in different communities ([AuSM, June 20, 2023](https://ausm.org/news/ausm-earns-grants-to-help-somali-and-multicultural-families-understand-autism-and-access-services/)). What follows from a missing word? Does a family have to work through a clinic's label without a shared term? Are there words for the need even if not for the diagnosis? No Oromo family's own account of this was found. Who defines the condition here, a clinician, a school team, an elder, a parent? What does a diagnosis open, in services and in schooling, and what might it close, in family or community life? Those remain questions, and no Minnesota Oromo evidence answers them.

**People and organizations.** The Multicultural Autism Action Network offers family support, education and legislative advocacy, with videos in Oromo, Somali and Amharic, and ran a family camp with the Autism Society in October 2023 for Somali and Oromo families ([MAAN](https://www.maanmn.org/)). The University of Minnesota's Institute on Community Integration profiled its work training families on school plans and home and community services, including vaccination clinics for autistic children, in January 2026; that article does not mention Oromo families ([ICI, Jan. 2026](https://ici.umn.edu/news/leading-in-challenging-times)). As a 2015-16 fellow, Barite Gemada made an Oromo video on early screening, "struck by the lack of resources" in the language, and took on the Oromo delegate role in the state's Act Early network ([University of Minnesota LEND](https://lend.umn.edu/node/631)).

**What produced an impairment?** Sources do not say for Oromo Minnesotans. The 2006 paper above reports physical, social and psychological problems associated with trauma and torture among refugee women ([Robertson et al.](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/somali-and-oromo-refugee-women-trauma-and-associated-factors/)). That points to what people endured, not to anything about Oromo culture or people. How many older Oromo Minnesotans live with those effects, and whether they are recognized as disability, are open questions. Respondents in 2016 worried about elders, largely over language ([Wilder Research](https://www.wilder.org/wp-content/pdf-file/OromoCommunityAssessment_7-16.pdf)).

Anole Sisters lists support for families raising children with special needs among the help it connects Oromo women to, though its funder's account gives no numbers ([Mary's Pence](https://maryspence.org/stories/amplifying-the-voices-of-east-african-women/)). Language access runs through every finding above. In 2016, 94 percent of Cedar-Riverside respondents had met a language barrier at a hospital or clinic and 84 percent at a school ([Wilder Research](https://www.wilder.org/wp-content/pdf-file/OromoCommunityAssessment_7-16.pdf)). Does a parent who cannot read a school evaluation in Afaan Oromo get the same chance to agree to or question a disability label? No study answers that for Oromo families here.

Not found: Oromo Deaf or DeafBlind people here, Oromo use of disability waivers or personal care, and any thesis focused on disability among Oromo Minnesotans.

## 13. What is changing now

**Federal enforcement.** In early December 2025, Amano Dube of the Brian Coyle Center said agents targeting Somali people were "snatching everyone, whether they are Somali, Ethiopian, Eritrean, or Oromo" ([Sahan Journal, Dec. 3, 2025](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/ice-enforcement-somali-immigrants-minnesota-twin-cities-sweep/)). The Oromo Community of Minnesota set up a Rapid Response Team offering legal referrals, food delivery and volunteer drivers; its page gives no start date ([Oromo Community of Minnesota](https://oromocommunitymn.org/response-english)).

**Temporary Protected Status for Ethiopians.** The Department of Homeland Security announced in December 2025 that it would end the designation ([Federal Register notice](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2025-22746.pdf)). The Oromo Community of Minnesota said December 16, 2025, that it was "deeply concerned" and would offer referrals ([Oromo Community of Minnesota](https://oromocommunitymn.org/2025/12/16/oromo-community-of-minnesota-responds-to-tps-revocation-for-ethiopian-nationals/)). A court paused the end in January 2026. On August 18, 2026, a Massachusetts federal court lifted its stay, and the immigration law firm Fragomen reported that benefits and work permits ended that day, with a constitutional challenge continuing ([Fragomen, Aug. 18, 2026](https://www.fragomen.com/insights/united-states-federal-district-court-allows-termination-of-tps-for-ethiopia-to-proceed.html)). How many Oromo Minnesotans held this status is unknown.

**Refugee status reviews.** Sahan Journal reported in October 2026 on a federal effort, Operation PARRIS, to end refugee status, centered on Minnesota. One Ethiopian man, given a pseudonym, says U.S. officials cited a payment they link to the Oromo Liberation Front, which they treat as a terror group under a case-by-case category; he denies sending it. Mid-Minnesota Legal Aid's Danielle Hendrickson estimated hundreds of Minnesota refugees may be affected. The story does not discuss Oromo people as a group ([Sahan Journal, Oct. 16, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/immigration/refugee-status-termination-operation-parris-minnesota/)).

**Language and culture.** Recent steps include the East African Magnet (2023), the University of Minnesota's course (2025), exhibitions in 2025 and 2026, and a tournament final scheduled for Allianz Field. Bultum Academy's opening year could not be confirmed (sections 8 and 10).

**Where Irreecha happens.** MNopedia places it at Lake Nokomis. For 2026, the organizer's listing and Oromo Diaspora Media place it at Powderhorn Park, with city and Humanities Center support, while one lone report says Lake Nokomis (section 6).

**Finances and vacancies.** The main community organization has run deficits for three straight years and shows two open board seats (section 9). What that means for its capacity in a hard year is not reported.

## 14. Strengths and contributions

By its own account, the Oromo Community of Minnesota grew from a mutual aid group to a community center it fully owned by 2013 ([CareerForce](https://careerforce.mn.gov/so/group/173/location/158)), and in 1981 organized from the Twin Cities to find sponsors for refugees in camps ([MNopedia](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/oromos-minnesota-making-little-oromia)). A company started with one van now carries thousands of Minnesota children to school ([Carnegie Corporation](https://carnegie.org/great-immigrants/tashitaa-tufaa/)). Refugees who remember camps have raised money to welcome a stranger ([Sahan Journal, April 22, 2024](https://sahanjournal.com/changing-minnesota/welcome-corps-minnesota-refugee-sponsor-groups/)). One Islamic center has offered classes, a food shelf and funeral rooms, and its director has described working with Oromo Christians ([Southside Pride](https://southsidepride.com/?p=3771)). A language once barred from Ethiopian classrooms is now taught in a Minnesota charter school and at the University of Minnesota ([University of Minnesota](https://brief.umn.edu/feature/reviving-once-endangered-language)). Women's groups such as Anole Sisters train members in health and job skills and carry Oromo food, coffee and dance into wider Minnesota settings ([Mary's Pence](https://maryspence.org/stories/amplifying-the-voices-of-east-african-women/)), and a community health worker leads a weekly stress-relief class designed for the Oromo community ([WellShare International](https://wellshareinternational.org/event/l2b-oromo-class-7/)).
