Culture in the system we run

See how majority-culture values are built into disability policy and practice.

If something here brings up a hard memory or feeling, you can stop at any time. State employees can reach the State of Minnesota Employee Assistance Program for free, confidential support; your human resources office can connect you.

Opening

Here is a bit of Minnesotans' own humor about themselves. Howard Mohr's 1987 book How to Talk Minnesotan described a place where an offer of help is refused three times before it is accepted (Kirkus Reviews). The guest must refuse the food, and the host must insist (Duluth News Tribune via Echo Press, Sept. 6, 2022).

It is a joke, but it carries a real pattern. In many Minnesota homes, help is given more easily than it is asked for, and it often comes through institutions: the church, the cooperative, the nonprofit, the state.

Disability services are one of those institutions. We built them, and they carry our habits. That includes our ideas about what help looks like, who should decide, and what a good life is. This module looks at the system we run as a cultural product. That is not a criticism of the system. It is a way to see it more clearly, and to notice the families it fits easily and the ones it fits less well.

A history we inherited

Disability is not only a service area. It is part of Minnesota's cultural history, and that history still shapes how some people view public agencies.

Content note. This section describes abuse, neglect, forced sterilization and deaths in state institutions. Readers may pause or skip to the next section.

Minnesota built a system of state institutions for people with disabilities. They included Faribault State School and Hospital, which began in 1879, and the Colony for Epileptics, which opened at Cambridge in 1925 and closed as Cambridge State Hospital in 1997 (Minnesota Historical Society Library, Institutions for People with Disabilities, checked Oct. 31, 2026). Over time, such institutions shifted from education to lifelong custodial care. By the 1960s they were overcrowded and understaffed, and families worried about chemical and physical restraints (MNopedia, Welsch v. Likins).

The eugenics laws are a harder part of the story. A 1917 law let county probate judges commit people judged "feeble minded" without their families' consent. Being poor or behaving unusually could count as a sign. MNopedia notes that an immigrant who failed an English-language IQ test could be labeled this way. A 1925 law authorized sterilization (MNopedia, Eugenics in Minnesota).

At least 2,204 Minnesotans were sterilized between 1925 and 1945, 77 percent of them women. The true number is unknown because no central board kept records (MNopedia, Eugenics in Minnesota).

The people behind this saw themselves as reformers. MNopedia describes Charles Dight, founder of the Minnesota Eugenics Society, as "consistently interested in social reform and utopian politics" (MNopedia, Eugenics in Minnesota). The lesson for us is not that reform is dangerous. It is that confidence in our own good intentions is not a safeguard.

Families pushed back. The parents of Patricia Welsch, a resident at Cambridge, worked with the Legal Aid Society of Minneapolis and the Arc of Minnesota to bring a class action. In 1974, Judge Earl Larson ruled that residents had a constitutional right to treatment (MNopedia, Welsch v. Likins). The Arc's history credits parents with pressing for the Developmental Disabilities Waiver and helping negotiate the plan to close the state institutions and replace them with community services (The Arc Minnesota, History of The Arc in Minnesota). And around 13,000 people who died in state hospitals were buried in graves marked only with numbers, or nothing. In 1994, disability self-advocates formed Remembering With Dignity to give them named stones (MNopedia, Remembering With Dignity).

Notice the arc of this story. It moves from institutions run for people, to parents suing on their behalf, to self-advocates speaking for themselves. The central question shifted from "how do we care for people?" to "who decides?" For a culture proud of helping, that shift can be uncomfortable, because it means sharing control. It is also the shift today's policy rests on.

Values written into law

Those hard-won lessons are now written into Minnesota law. State policy says adults with disabilities, and children with support from their families, may make "informed choices" about services. The process must give information "in a manner that is culturally and linguistically appropriate" and identify the person's cultural needs (Minn. Stat. 256B.4905, checked Oct. 31, 2026).

The licensing law for home and community-based services requires person-centered planning. It asks what is important "to the person" as well as "for the person," and calls for self-determination and "the most integrated setting" (Minn. Stat. 245D.07). Minnesota's Olmstead Plan aims for disabled Minnesotans to live full, integrated lives in communities of their choosing, as the Metropolitan Center for Independent Living summarizes it (Metropolitan Center for Independent Living).

The licensing law asks planners to respect "each person's history, dignity, and cultural background" (Minn. Stat. 245D.07).

These are strong protections, won by disabled people and families. They also carry assumptions that match the majority culture: that the individual is the main decision-maker, that independence is a central goal, and that the most integrated setting is usually best. Seeing these as cultural values, and not simply as "correct," is not a criticism. The law itself names the tension when it requires cultural needs to be identified.

The same values, seen from other homes

The community profiles in this series document how some families describe disability, care and decision-making. These examples come from particular studies at particular times. They do not describe every member of any community.

In a 2014 Minnesota Department of Health study of families of children with autism, Hmong parents described stigma tied to a belief that families may be "genetically flawed or cursed." A Somali parent described a fear that people would say "it's because of the mother." A Latino parent described a fear that a child would be seen as "crazy" (Minnesota Department of Health, report to the Legislature, Feb. 15, 2014). In 2015 community conversations with 220 Somali participants, people described turning first to the mosque, the Quran or spiritual leaders (Minnesota Department of Health, Somali Mental Health Project). Some Somali parents, professionals and autistic people have since proposed maangaar, "unique mind," as a new word for autism (Sahan Journal, updated April 7, 2026).

Data on service use raises related questions. A 2021 study for DHS compared how racial and ethnic groups in Minnesota's Medicaid population used home and community-based services (Arling and colleagues, report to DHS, March 26, 2021).

Under age 65, 46 percent of white people in the study were on a disability waiver. Asian and Pacific Islander people (38 percent) and Black people (31 percent) most often used personal care assistance without a waiver (Arling and colleagues, report to DHS, March 26, 2021).

The authors say plainly that they "do not attempt to draw conclusions" about causes (Arling and colleagues, 2021). So we should not either. Research has not established why these gaps exist in Minnesota.

What these findings do not show is that any community "values independence less." They show differences in where families turn first, how disability is named, who in the family decides and which services get used. Joseph Vandello and Dov Cohen's 1999 index of state individualism even counted older people living alone and grandparents in the household as markers (Cambridge University Press supplementary material describing the index). A system designed around a person who lives on their own, makes their own choices and works in the community will fit some households more naturally than others. When a family member answers for an adult with a disability, is that a rights problem, a norm the person has chosen, or both? The honest answer is that you have to ask.

Inside the agency

Culture also shapes how we work with each other. Minnesota Management and Budget's 2025 Employee Engagement and Inclusion Survey asked state employees whether they feel comfortable sharing ideas "even when they differ from others'." 58 percent agreed and another 24 percent somewhat agreed (MMB, Statewide Employee Engagement and Inclusion Survey, High-Level Employee Statement Results, August 2025). Roughly four in ten did not fully agree. The public summary does not break results down by agency or group, so it cannot tell us whether DHS differs.

Sociologist Danielle Lavin-Loucks writes that a smile and a nod "appears like agreement" (The Society Pages, 2020).

No published study has observed how these habits play out in state agency meetings. Still, the question is worth carrying into your next one: when no one objects, has the group agreed, or have some people decided objecting is not worth the cost?

Recent oversight findings also touch DHS directly. On March 17, 2026, the Office of the Legislative Auditor found that DHS "has long had legal authority" to address alleged kickbacks in the EIDBI autism benefit, though officials had said it lacked that authority before 2025. The auditor also identified a decades-old error in the department's rules that may limit its ability to suspend payments during such investigations (Office of the Legislative Auditor, special review, March 17, 2026). Separately, DHS ended its Housing Stabilization Services program on October 31, 2025, citing widespread fraud, even though, in the department's words, it "truly filled an important gap" for many participants (DHS bulletin, Oct. 31, 2025).

These findings raise questions without settling them. How does a culture of trust in providers and partners, which serves many people well, notice when that trust is abused? And how does an agency tighten oversight without casting suspicion on whole communities?

Try it

Example for discussion. This is a composite written for teaching, not a real case.

Maria is a lead agency case manager meeting with Sam, a 24-year-old with a developmental disability, and his parents and older sister. The family says decisions about Sam are made together, and they want him to keep living at home with relatives caring for him. The planning form in front of Maria has sections on independent living goals and competitive employment.

What could Maria do next?

  1. This may help make sure Sam's voice is heard, which the law and the disability rights history both value. It may also feel to the family like distrust if it comes without explanation. Asking Sam first how he would like decisions made, and who he wants in the room, may honor his choice and the family's way of deciding together.
  2. This may cover everything the form asks, and the family will hear about real options. It may also signal that care at home is a starting point to move past, not a valid choice. The family may agree politely in the meeting without feeling heard, and the plan may not reflect what they actually want.
  3. This may take longer, and the answers may not fit the form's headings neatly. It treats care at home and shared decision-making as choices worth understanding, while still making sure Sam and his family know every option well enough for the choice to be informed. It may also build trust that carries into future meetings.

Choose any answer to see what it might lead to. You can open all three.

Reflect

For your own reflection. Nothing here is collected.

  1. How do I picture a "good outcome" for a person with a disability: living independently, working, making their own choices? Where did that picture come from, and would every family share it?
  2. When a family wants to make decisions together, or care for someone at home, how do I respond? How does the planning process respond?
  3. What do I know about the history of Minnesota's state institutions, eugenics laws and racial covenants? How might that history shape the trust a family gives me?

Take away

Practice this week

In your next planning conversation, before you turn to the form, ask the person and their family what a good life means to them, and write it down in their words.
