## Opening

Here is a piece of Minnesota humor, and Minnesotans wrote it about themselves.

In 1987, Howard Mohr, a writer for *A Prairie Home Companion*, published *How to Talk Minnesotan: A Visitor's Guide* ([MPR News, Sept. 6, 2022](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2022/09/06/author-howard-mohr-known-for-how-to-talk-minnesotan-dies)). One of its lessons is the "Minnesota Long Goodbye." The visit is over. Everyone says so. Then it goes on in the front hall, then on the steps, and it ends only with the guests in the car and the hosts bent at the driver's window ([Duluth News Tribune via Echo Press, Sept. 6, 2022](https://www.echopress.com/lifestyle/arts-and-entertainment/front-row-seat-remembering-howard-mohr-and-how-to-talk-minnesotan)).

If you smiled, you probably know the rules. Nobody taught them in a class.

This module looks at those rules with affection and with honesty. "Minnesota nice" has a warm side that is real and worth keeping. It also has a guarded side that can be hard to read if you did not grow up with it. In a state that is changing fast, and in work that serves families from many cultures, both sides matter.

## The warm side is real

Start with what is good, because it is not a myth.

Rachel Hutton, a Star Tribune culture reporter, told NPR in 2024 that Minnesotans are "literally nice." She meant kind and polite, the sort of people who will help push a stranger's car out of a snowbank ([NPR, Aug. 9, 2024](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5065632)).

There is data behind the civic side of that picture.

:::fact
In 2023, 40.3 percent of Minnesotans 16 and older said they had volunteered in the past year, third among the states, against 28.3 percent nationally ([America's Health Rankings, volunteerism, from the Census Bureau's Current Population Survey, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.americashealthrankings.org/explore/measures/volunteerism_d/MN)).
:::

Minnesotans also give and take part. Samuel Myers Jr., a University of Minnesota economist, describes a "sustained and vigorous corporate giving culture" in the state ([Humphrey School, The Minnesota Paradox, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.hhh.umn.edu/research-centers/roy-wilkins-center-human-relations-and-social-justice/minnesota-paradox)). In 2024, 76.35 percent of eligible Minnesotans voted, second only to Wisconsin. Minnesotans 18 to 29 voted at the highest rate in the nation, 62 percent ([KSTP, citing the Minnesota Secretary of State](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/local-news/minnesotans-ranked-2nd-in-voter-turnout-1st-among-young-voters/)).

Even people who are critical of Minnesota nice grant the warmth. An immigration attorney who moved here told MPR in 2012 that she had seen the state's "collective good will" toward people in need ([MPR News, March 12, 2012](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2012/03/12/outsiders1-is-minnesota-nice-to-newcomers)).

:::voice
"I know someone will write the check." An immigration attorney, on Minnesota's generosity ([MPR News, March 12, 2012](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2012/03/12/outsiders1-is-minnesota-nice-to-newcomers)).
:::

Her next point was the harder one. She was not sure how good Minnesotans were at making refugees "really feel Minnesotan" ([MPR News, March 12, 2012](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2012/03/12/outsiders1-is-minnesota-nice-to-newcomers)). Generosity and distance, side by side: that pairing runs through the rest of this module.

## Reading the code

Mohr's book is humor, but it is humor by a close observer. A Kirkus review summed up the place it describes: things "could be worse," poetry should rhyme, and an offer of help is refused three times before it is accepted ([Kirkus Reviews](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/howard-mohr/how-to-talk-minnesotan-a-visitors-guide/)).

Writing after Mohr's death in 2022, Jay Gabler recalled more of the lessons ([Duluth News Tribune via Echo Press, Sept. 6, 2022](https://www.echopress.com/lifestyle/arts-and-entertainment/front-row-seat-remembering-howard-mohr-and-how-to-talk-minnesotan)):

- "That's different" works as "a blanket reply on neutral ground."
- One lesson is called "The Power of the Negative."
- A guest must refuse food, and the host must insist.

Gabler also named the book's limits. It described how "a certain, then culturally dominant, strain" of Minnesotans talked, not all Minnesotans ([Duluth News Tribune via Echo Press, Sept. 6, 2022](https://www.echopress.com/lifestyle/arts-and-entertainment/front-row-seat-remembering-howard-mohr-and-how-to-talk-minnesotan)). That is worth keeping in mind. Many Minnesotans of every background use these habits, and many do not.

Journalists describe the same code today. Hutton told NPR that if you disliked someone's hotdish at a potluck, you might call it "different" or "interesting" while quietly scraping it into the trash. She added that calling someone "weird," in Minnesota speech, is a harsh statement. Corey Bonnema, co-author of a guide for transplants, described a relative who asks "is it hot in here" instead of asking for the air conditioning ([NPR, Aug. 9, 2024](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5065632)).

Notice what these moves have in common. The words carry less than the meaning. "Interesting" may mean "I don't like it." "Is it hot in here" means "please turn on the air." A first "no" may mean "ask me again." The listener is expected to read the setting and fill in the rest.

For someone raised in the code, this is smooth and polite. For a coworker, client or family member from somewhere else, including another part of the United States, it can be a puzzle. They may take "that's interesting" as real interest. They may take a first "no, I'm fine" at face value. Or they may sense that something is being left unsaid and not know what.

## When nice avoids conflict

The guarded side of Minnesota nice is the one Minnesotans joke about most.

Hutton described "a habit of understatement" and a tendency, "instead of being direct," to be passive-aggressive. Jerilyn Veldof, Bonnema's co-author, told NPR that passive aggressiveness is what gives the phrase "Minnesota nice" its double meaning ([NPR, Aug. 9, 2024](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5065632)).

Danielle Lavin-Loucks, a sociologist who grew up in Stillwater and now teaches at Valparaiso University, wrote about this in 2020 ([The Society Pages, July 3, 2020](https://thesocietypages.org/specials/rethinking-minnesota-nice/)).

:::voice
"Avoidance of conflict is central." Danielle Lavin-Loucks, sociologist ([The Society Pages, July 3, 2020](https://thesocietypages.org/specials/rethinking-minnesota-nice/)).
:::

In her description, the things to avoid are visible conflict, open disagreement, un-nice topics and complaints. The tools are a smile, a nod and a change of subject, often to the weather. To others, she writes, that response "appears like agreement" ([The Society Pages, July 3, 2020](https://thesocietypages.org/specials/rethinking-minnesota-nice/)).

That is one person's reflective essay, not a study. But her point matters a great deal at work. In a meeting, silence and a nod can look like a yes. In a reply to a family, a warm tone can look like an answer. Neither is always what it seems.

## Friendly, but hard to befriend

In 2012, MPR's "Outsiders" series heard from dozens of people who had moved to Minnesota. Many found the locals friendly but hard to befriend. Some said their only friends were other transplants ([MPR News, March 12, 2012](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2012/03/12/outsiders1-is-minnesota-nice-to-newcomers)).

:::voice
Minnesotans "will give you directions to anywhere except their own house." A newcomer from Fargo ([MPR News, March 12, 2012](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2012/03/12/outsiders1-is-minnesota-nice-to-newcomers)).
:::

Todd Klingel, then president of the Minneapolis Regional Chamber of Commerce and a lifelong Minnesotan, said the chamber's focus groups with newcomers told stories he found horrifying. He said feelings of isolation were even more common among professionals of color. He also offered a self-test: who have I invited into my house in the last year that I did not know five years ago? MPR noted that Seattle has a similar reputation, so this is not only a Minnesota pattern ([MPR News, March 12, 2012](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2012/03/12/outsiders1-is-minnesota-nice-to-newcomers)).

Grace Vieth, a University of Minnesota researcher, explained the pattern in 2023 as a preference for long-term friendships. Many locals are willing to be friendly but do not follow up, because their existing friends already meet their social needs ([University of Minnesota, 2023](https://cla.umn.edu/psychology/news-events/story/vieth-navigating-social-isolation-newcomer-minnesota)).

None of these sources measured friendship across the state. They describe a reputation that many newcomers recognize. No statewide study measuring Minnesotans' friendliness, indirectness or welcome to newcomers has been found.

## What research shows, and a question

Is there harder evidence? A little, and it comes with cautions.

**Personality by state.** In a 2013 study, Peter Rentfrow and colleagues analyzed samples totaling more than 1.5 million people and mapped average personality traits by state ([Rentfrow et al., 2013, American Psychological Association](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/psp-a0034434.pdf)).

:::fact
Minnesota's average agreeableness score, 61.6, ranked sixth of 49 listed, among the highest. Its openness score, 38.5, ranked 44th of 49, among the lowest ([Rentfrow et al., 2013, appendix](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/psp-a0034434.pdf)).
:::

In plain terms, the data fit the "nice" part of the reputation: agreeable and considerate, on average. They also add something the reputation does not always mention, a lower average taste for novelty and change. The authors say their labels give "only a broad sense" of the patterns, and most of the data came from people who chose to take online personality tests ([Rentfrow et al., 2013](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/psp-a0034434.pdf)).

**Rules and their enforcement.** Jesse Harrington and Michele Gelfand ranked the 50 states on "tightness," meaning many strongly enforced rules and little tolerance for deviance. Minnesota ranked 28th, near the middle ([Harrington and Gelfand, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2014, via PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4050535/)). Their index was built from laws and punishments. It may miss norms that are enforced by quiet disapproval rather than by law. That is a question, not a finding.

**High and low context, as a question.** The anthropologist Edward T. Hall described two styles of communication. In high-context communication, much of the meaning sits in the setting, the relationship and what goes unsaid. In low-context communication, meaning is put into explicit words. An open college textbook, summarizing Hall, places the United States and most of Northern Europe at the low-context end, with the principle "say what you mean" ([Kirkwood Community College open textbook, Cultural Dimensions – Hall, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://kirkwood.pressbooks.pub/emcworthy/chapter/cultural-dimensions/)). The business researcher Erin Meyer calls the United States "the lowest-context culture in the world," as a 2020 article on her work reports ([Welcome to the Jungle, June 1, 2020](https://www.welcometothejungle.com/en-GB/articles/keys-managing-multicultural-teams)).

Yet "that's different," "is it hot in here" and a "no" that means "ask me again" are high-context moves. So here is a question, not an answer: does mainstream Minnesota speech run more high-context among insiders than the national label suggests? And could that help explain why newcomers find it hard to read?

Two cautions. A 2011 review of research using Hall's idea found most country labels rest on "seemingly less-than-adequate evidence" ([Kittler, Rygl and Mackinnon, 2011, University of Stirling repository](https://www.stir.ac.uk/research/hub/publication/818365)). And no study measuring context in Minnesota speech has been found.

Meyer also places Americans in the middle on giving negative feedback. In her account, they are trained to wrap criticism in praise ([Welcome to the Jungle, summarizing Meyer](https://www.welcometothejungle.com/en-GB/articles/keys-managing-multicultural-teams)). If Minnesota leans even gentler, a supervisor's concern may be wrapped so softly that a colleague from a more direct culture does not hear it at all. A colleague from a less direct culture may still hear it as harsh. That is a hypothesis to test in your own workplace.

Use these frameworks as mirrors. Each one describes averages, not people. They are most useful for noticing your own default settings, and least useful as labels for anyone else.

## Try it

:::scenario
Example for discussion. This is a composite written for teaching, not a real case.
Dana works in a DSD program area that answers questions from families. A mother writes: "I have called three times in two weeks. Nobody has called back. My son's services stopped on the 1st. I want to know who is responsible and when this will be fixed." Dana checks and learns the services stopped because a form was not processed, and that a fix may take another week. A coworker suggests a reply that thanks her warmly, says the team "is looking into it," and wishes her a good weekend.
? How might Dana respond?
- Send the coworker's warm reply as written. || This may feel kind and safe, and it avoids naming a mistake. But the mother asked two direct questions, and this reply answers neither. To someone used to plain speech, warmth without an answer may read as a brush-off, and it may lead to a fourth call and less trust.
- Reply plainly and kindly: acknowledge the missed calls, explain that a form was not processed, give the expected timeline, and name who will follow up and when. || This may be uncomfortable, because it states a problem out loud. It also answers what she asked, in words she does not have to decode. Pairing honesty with courtesy uses the best of Minnesota nice instead of its guarded side.
- Call the mother first, ask what she most needs to know, then follow up in writing with the facts and a timeline. || This may build trust, since a call can show care that a written reply cannot. It works best if Dana still says the hard part clearly on the call and does not let a friendly conversation stand in for the answer. The written follow-up may help her keep the facts.
:::

## Reflect

:::reflect
- When I say "that's interesting" or "that's different" at work, what do I mean, and would a colleague from another background hear it that way?
- When a meeting ends with no objections, how do I know whether people agreed?
- Whose feedback do I find hardest to hear: feedback that is too blunt, or too gentle? What does that tell me about my own cultural settings?
:::

## Take away

:::takeaway
This week, say one "no" or one concern plainly and kindly. For example, in your next reply to a family or coworker, answer the direct question in the first sentence, then add the warmth.
:::
