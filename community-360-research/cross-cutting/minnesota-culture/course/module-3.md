## Opening

Picture a funeral lunch in a church basement in a small Minnesota town. Long tables, folding chairs, coffee in a big urn, bars on paper plates and at least three kinds of hotdish. Nobody sent a sign-up sheet. People just knew what to bring.

That scene has a history. MNopedia, the Minnesota Historical Society's encyclopedia, traces the earliest known hotdish recipe to a 1930 cookbook from a Lutheran church women's society in Mankato. After World War II, it says, the dish moved "out of the church rec room" into home kitchens, served at showers, funerals and rallies as a way to express care ([MNopedia, Hotdish](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/hotdish-mnopedia)).

So a casserole that many of us think of as just "food" grew out of particular churches, particular immigrants and a particular way of showing care. This module asks the same question about the habits we bring to work. They feel natural. They also came from somewhere.

## Settlers, churches and keeping your head down

A culture is partly a set of answers that earlier people worked out to the problems they faced. Mainstream Minnesota culture carries answers worked out by settlers, farmers, church members and organizers more than a century ago.

The story begins with land. MNopedia describes the state's origins as "primarily about the taking of land" from Native people, through treaties, treaty violations and the aftermath of the US-Dakota War of 1862 ([MNopedia, Land, Labor, the Market, and Politics](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/land-labor-market-and-politics)). The settlement story that follows began on land that was taken, and an honest account says so.

Germans were the state's largest single ethnic group by 1917 ([MNopedia, Anti-German Nativism, 1917–1919](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/anti-german-nativism-1917-1919)). Hundreds of thousands of Norwegians arrived between 1851 and 1920, often as whole families who meant to stay, and many settled near one another because they feared extreme isolation ([MNopedia, Norwegian Immigration to Minnesota](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/norwegian-immigration-minnesota)). More Swedes settled in Minnesota than in any other state. Their churches, mostly Lutheran, were "hubs for social as well as religious activities," places that countered isolation and offered help ([MNopedia, Swedish Immigration to Minnesota](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/swedish-immigration-minnesota)).

The church was school, social club and aid society, and later a social service agency. That is one reason so much help in Minnesota still flows through organizations. Today 22 percent of Minnesota adults belong to mainline Protestant churches and 18 percent are Catholic, though many others claim no religion ([Pew Research Center, Religious Landscape Study, Minnesota](https://www.pewresearch.org/religious-landscape-study/state/minnesota/)). The habits these churches formed may outlast their hold.

The majority culture also learned to keep its head down. During World War I, a state commission with what MNopedia calls "near-dictatorial powers" watched German Americans closely, and officials in New Ulm who questioned the draft were removed from office ([MNopedia, Anti-German Nativism](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/anti-german-nativism-1917-1919)). Swedish Lutheran churches moved to English services in the 1920s and 1930s, hastened in part by pressure to Americanize ([MNopedia, Swedish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/swedish-immigration-minnesota)). Whether some of today's caution about standing out was learned in those years is a fair question. No source in the essay answers it.

Many Minnesotans point to a Scandinavian source for that caution. In a 2009 MPR essay, playwright Syl Jones traced Minnesota nice to the "Jante law," the unwritten rules of a fictional town in a 1930s novel by Aksel Sandemose, which begin "Don't think that you are special" ([MPR News, Dec. 14, 2009](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2009/12/14/syljones)).

:::voice
Syl Jones argued that Minnesota nice is "more about keeping up appearances" than niceness ([MPR News, 2009](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2009/12/14/syljones)).
:::

That is one writer's essay, not a study. It describes a novel's town, not measured Minnesotans. It is worth knowing because so many people find it familiar.

The usual story of roots also leaves people out: the Dakota and Ojibwe people whose land this was, the Black Minnesotans of St. Paul's Rondo neighborhood and north Minneapolis, and Mexican farmworkers, about 7,000 of whom worked the sugar beet fields as early as 1928 ([MNopedia, Land, Labor, the Market, and Politics](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/land-labor-market-and-politics)). A culture's picture of itself is a selection.

## Farms, winters and cooperatives

Where did Minnesota nice come from? University of St. Thomas sociologist Lisa Waldner told MPR in 2019 that there is no definitive documentation. She offered two likely threads. Northern European settlers clustered by group, so that people lived side by side without mixing much. And on a farm you might need a neighbor's help when your plow broke at harvest, so politeness won out over hostility ([MPR News, Nov. 21, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/11/21/ask-a-sotan-revisiting-minnesota-nice)).

:::voice
Of early settlers, Lisa Waldner said people "co-existed, but you didn't intermingle" ([MPR News, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/11/21/ask-a-sotan-revisiting-minnesota-nice)).
:::

Waldner's account holds two values in tension: a strong ethic of handling your own problems, and a practical dependence on the people nearby.

Winter belongs in the story too. Writer Jay Gabler summarized humorist Howard Mohr's view that Minnesota sociability rests on a stoicism born of the need to outlast brutal winters ([Duluth News Tribune via Echo Press, Sept. 6, 2022](https://www.echopress.com/lifestyle/arts-and-entertainment/front-row-seat-remembering-howard-mohr-and-how-to-talk-minnesotan)). That is humor by a close observer, not research. University of Minnesota researcher Grace Vieth has linked newcomers' isolation partly to long winters, when people stay with the friends they already have ([University of Minnesota Department of Psychology, Sept. 2023](https://cla.umn.edu/psychology/news-events/story/vieth-navigating-social-isolation-newcomer-minnesota)).

Minnesota's habit of solving problems together has a concrete history. In 1890 the Danish American community at Clarks Grove founded one of the state's first cooperative creameries. For decades the creamery was also a community center, hosting talks, meetings and school recitals ([MNopedia, Clarks Grove Cooperative Creamery](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/clarks-grove-cooperative-creamery)).

:::fact
By 1928, nearly half of all the cooperative creameries in the United States were in Minnesota ([MNopedia, Clarks Grove Cooperative Creamery](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/clarks-grove-cooperative-creamery)).
:::

Pooling resources, sharing profits and losses, and meeting to decide are habits with deep roots here.

## Government as a shared moral project

The political scientist Daniel Elazar described three American political cultures. In the "individualistic" one, politics is a marketplace and a career. In the "traditionalistic" one, an established elite governs. In the "moralistic" one, politics is a shared search for the good society, government is "a positive instrument" for the general welfare, and public service carries moral duties. Elazar called Minnesota "the archetypical example" of the moralistic culture and traced it to Yankee settlers reinforced by Scandinavian and German immigrants ([Daniel J. Elazar, "Minnesota: The Epitome of the Moralistic Political Culture"](https://dje.jcpa.org/articles2/minn3.htm)). In his view, Minnesotans argue about how large a role government should play, not whether it should play one.

Treat this framework as a mirror, not a label. It is one scholar's classification, built from settlement history rather than surveys, and it describes a state's public life, not any person in it. Elazar himself named a tension that matters for a large agency: the moralistic culture has a "major difficulty with bureaucracy," and where merit systems exist, "they tend to be rigidly maintained" ([Elazar](https://dje.jcpa.org/articles2/minn3.htm)).

The history behind the framework is real. The Farmer-Labor Party grew from farmers' and workers' organizing against monopolies and elected hundreds of candidates from 1917 into the 1940s ([MNopedia, Minnesota Farmer-Labor Party, 1924–1944](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesota-farmer-labor-party-1924-1944)). In 1948 Hubert Humphrey urged his party to "walk forthrightly in the bright sunshine of human rights" ([MNopedia, Hubert H. Humphrey](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/humphrey-hubert-h-1911-1978)). The 1967 state civil rights law passed with support from both parties ([MNopedia, Minnesota Human Rights Act](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/minnesota-human-rights-act)). The 1971 "Minnesota Miracle," which shifted most school costs to the state, took the longest special session in state history. Before it, the richest districts had the lowest tax rates and the highest spending per pupil ([MNopedia, Minnesota Miracle](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/minnesota-miracle-legislation)).

:::fact
In 2024, 76.35 percent of eligible Minnesotans voted, second in the nation, and young Minnesotans' turnout ranked first ([KSTP, citing the Minnesota Secretary of State](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/local-news/minnesotans-ranked-2nd-in-voter-turnout-1st-among-young-voters/)).
:::

This picture is not the whole present. Hamline political scientist David Schultz argued in a 2025 column that "the myth of Minnesota exceptionalism" no longer holds, describing a swing state with sharp regional divides ([Duluth News Tribune, June 26, 2025](https://www.duluthnewstribune.com/opinion/columns/state-view-minnesota-exceptionalism-it-turns-out-was-a-myth)). Easy consensus is part of the inheritance, but not a promise.

## Values under the surface

Values are the beliefs about what is good that sit underneath behavior. Here are five that the history above helps explain. Each is a strength. Each can also hide something.

**Self-reliance, with organized help.** Farm history, and a 2013 personality study's finding of high average conscientiousness across the wider north central region, point to a culture that values steady work and handling one's own problems ([Rentfrow et al., 2013](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/psp-a0034434.pdf)). Yet cooperatives and church aid societies show a strong habit of helping. In the essay's reading, help here is often organized through institutions, and given more easily than it is asked for.

**Egalitarianism and "treat everyone the same."** Business researcher Erin Meyer places Scandinavian countries among the most egalitarian on her leading scale ([Welcome to the Jungle, summarizing Meyer](https://www.welcometothejungle.com/en-GB/articles/keys-managing-multicultural-teams)). Minnesota's civic tradition prizes fairness too. The difficulty is that fairness can mean identical treatment, or treatment that gets people to the same place. That question returns in Module 6.

**Modesty and privacy.** Jones's essay and Mohr's humor both describe a culture that discourages standing out ([MPR News, 2009](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2009/12/14/syljones); [Duluth News Tribune via Echo Press, 2022](https://www.echopress.com/lifestyle/arts-and-entertainment/front-row-seat-remembering-howard-mohr-and-how-to-talk-minnesotan)). A culture that keeps its troubles private may also find it awkward to ask others about theirs, which matters in work that depends on asking.

**Consensus and trust in process.** The bipartisan rights law and the Minnesota Miracle show a culture that trusts long process to reach fair results ([MNopedia, Minnesota Miracle](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/minnesota-miracle-legislation)). Meyer notes that consensus decisions "take much longer to reach" but are harder to change ([Welcome to the Jungle, summarizing Meyer](https://www.welcometothejungle.com/en-GB/articles/keys-managing-multicultural-teams)). Trust in institutions is a strength. It can be a blind spot with people whose own or family history gives them reason to trust less.

None of these values is "Minnesotan" in the sense of belonging to every Minnesotan, and none is measured for Minnesota alone. They are inherited defaults. Knowing their history lets you choose when to lean on them and when to set them aside.

## Try it
:::scenario
Example for discussion. This is a composite written for teaching, not a real case.
Dana joined your DSD unit three months ago after years working for a state agency in the South. After a team meeting she tells you what puzzles her: no one took credit when a project went well, a coworker turned down help twice before accepting it, and a small policy change has now been discussed in four meetings "until everyone is on board." She asks you, "Why does everyone here work this way?"
? How might you respond?
- "Honestly, that's just how professional teams work. You'll get used to it." || This may reassure Dana that nothing is wrong. It may also present local habits as the universal standard, which is the "water" this course asks us to notice, and it leaves her as the one who must adjust.
- "Some of that may be Minnesota habits. Modesty, refusing help at first and slow consensus all have local history, from church and farm life to cooperatives. How did your old team handle these things?" || This may help Dana see the habits as learned values rather than rules, and it names their roots without claiming they fit everyone. Asking about her old team treats her experience as equally valid and may show you something about your own defaults.
- "Ha, that's Minnesota nice. Don't take it personally." || This may put Dana at ease with shared humor, and it is kind. It may also bundle three different habits under one joke, so the values behind them stay out of view, and it does not invite her to say what she would find helpful.
:::

## Reflect
:::reflect
- When I think "I treat everyone the same," what is the "same" based on? Whose way of doing things is the default?
- What strengths of Minnesota culture do I bring to my work, and how could I use them more deliberately with people whose culture differs from mine?
:::

## Take away
:::takeaway
This week, name one value you hold at work, such as waiting for consensus or not asking for help, and trace it to where you learned it: a church basement, a family farm, a first supervisor. Write the pair down where you will see it.
:::
