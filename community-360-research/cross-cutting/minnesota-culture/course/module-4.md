## Opening

Here is a line that gets a knowing laugh, and it is meant as humor. A newcomer from Fargo told MPR News about being warned Minnesotans "will give you directions to anywhere except their own house" ([MPR News, March 12, 2012](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2012/03/12/outsiders1-is-minnesota-nice-to-newcomers)).

Most of us who grew up here can laugh at that, because we know the person giving those directions. They are friendly. They are helpful. They mean well. And they may not notice that the newcomer drove away still feeling like a guest.

That small joke points to something larger. A culture looks one way from the inside and another way from the outside. The people inside it often see kindness. People arriving from elsewhere, or people the culture has kept at a distance, may see something more mixed.

This module listens to those outside views. It also looks at parts of Minnesota's racial history that the majority culture has not always talked about. The goal is not to feel bad about home. It is to see home more completely.

## Voices from outside the majority

Many of the sharpest observations about Minnesota's majority culture come from people who stand partly outside it. None of them speaks for a whole community.

In a 2002 MPR documentary, Arthur McWatt, a St. Paul native and retired social studies teacher, described employers who accepted a Black applicant's application and then threw it away. He called it discrimination "done in a more subtle way than in the South." He said Black Minnesotans learned that racist white people often had smiling faces and polite voices, and developed "an antenna" for insincerity. He also said Black Minnesotans generally felt safer here than in the South ([MPR, "Up South," Feb. 12, 2002](https://archive.mpr.org/stories/2002/02/12/african-americans-jim-crow-north)).

:::voice
Ken Charles, a General Mills executive, said a job gets newcomers "the 9 to 5," and "the harder part is the 5 to 9" ([MPR News, March 12, 2012](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2012/03/12/outsiders1-is-minnesota-nice-to-newcomers)).
:::

That same 2012 MPR series reported that professionals of color who moved to the Twin Cities often said locals "accept them, but lack a genuine understanding of other cultures." The same story carried a refugee's very different view: Minnesotans welcomed him and gave him a job before he spoke English well, and "I think I can say that's nice" ([MPR News, March 12, 2012](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2012/03/12/outsiders1-is-minnesota-nice-to-newcomers)). Both experiences are real.

Some voices come from inside the majority, looking back. Danielle Lavin-Loucks, a sociologist who grew up in Stillwater in the 1970s, wrote that her family stressed that "people are people." By not talking about race, she did not really see it. She now sees that kind of colorblindness as discounting the experiences of people of color ([The Society Pages, July 3, 2020](https://thesocietypages.org/specials/rethinking-minnesota-nice/)). University of St. Thomas sociologist Lisa Waldner described a white Minnesotan who reasons that being polite to everyone means there are no issues around race. She said she does not think the intent is malicious, but called it a form of denial that can sustain institutional racism in housing and education ([MPR News, Nov. 21, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/11/21/ask-a-sotan-revisiting-minnesota-nice)).

Immigrant and refugee participants in a 2021 Twin Cities planning effort on belonging voiced frustration at "over-engagement": being asked for input again and again without seeing it in the final product ([The Journey to Belonging in the Twin Cities, City of Saint Paul, 2022](https://www.stpaul.gov/sites/default/files/2022-04/The%20Journey%20to%20Belonging%20in%20the%20Twin%20Cities%20Report.pdf)). For a public agency, that one deserves a long pause.

These voices do not say Minnesotans are unkind. Most say the opposite. What they describe is a culture in which kindness and avoidance can look the same from inside, and very different from outside.

## The Minnesota paradox

Samuel Myers Jr., director of the Roy Wilkins Center at the University of Minnesota's Humphrey School, calls it "the Minnesota Paradox." Minnesota is one of the best places to live in America. Yet by racial gaps in unemployment, income, incarceration, homeownership, test scores, school discipline and more, African Americans are worse off here than in "virtually every other state" ([Humphrey School, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.hhh.umn.edu/research-centers/roy-wilkins-center-human-relations-and-social-justice/minnesota-paradox)).

There is a nuance worth holding. A 2020 WCCO report noted that many indicators for Black Minnesotans are not worse than elsewhere. The gaps are wide partly because white Minnesotans do better than average ([CBS Minnesota, June 23, 2020](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/the-minnesota-paradox-why-the-land-of-10000-lakes-has-one-of-the-largest-racial-disparities/)).

:::voice
Samuel Myers Jr. described Minnesota's racism as the kind "where people say there is no racism" ([CBS Minnesota, June 23, 2020](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/the-minnesota-paradox-why-the-land-of-10000-lakes-has-one-of-the-largest-racial-disparities/)).
:::

Myers also names a habit worth watching for. He writes that in the 1990s, "in a characteristically Minnesotan manner," leaders blamed rising problems in the Black community on migrants from Chicago, Detroit and Gary, so the problem was "not Minnesota" ([Humphrey School](https://www.hhh.umn.edu/research-centers/roy-wilkins-center-human-relations-and-social-justice/minnesota-paradox)). He sees the same move in claims after 2020 that outside agitators drove the unrest. The pattern he describes, placing a problem outside the group so the group's self-image stays intact, is common in many dominant cultures. It is a mirror, not a verdict: you can ask whether you have ever reached for it yourself.

## A history kept out of view

Minnesota's racial history is often less visible than the South's, and that is part of the story.

**Racial covenants.** Starting in 1910, Minneapolis developers wrote racial covenants into property deeds, barring sale or rental to people of Black, Asian and other descent. By the 1930s most new residential developments in the Twin Cities carried them. The legislature banned them in 1953. MNopedia notes they sat "out of view" in county deed books, in places "imagined to be free from Jim Crow" ([MNopedia, Racial Housing Covenants in the Twin Cities](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/racial-housing-covenants-twin-cities)). The University of Minnesota's Mapping Prejudice project maps these deeds with community volunteers ([Mapping Prejudice, University of Minnesota](https://mappingprejudice.umn.edu/)). A 2019 state law lets homeowners formally discharge a covenant, though the project explains this does not erase what it calls "the receipts of structural racism" ([Mapping Prejudice, discharging racial covenants](https://mappingprejudice.umn.edu/discharging-racial-covenants)). Myers traces today's homeownership gaps partly to these policies ([Humphrey School](https://www.hhh.umn.edu/research-centers/roy-wilkins-center-human-relations-and-social-justice/minnesota-paradox)).

**Rondo and I-94.** St. Paul's Rondo neighborhood was the center of Black life in the city, with its own clubs, a credit union and a cooperative ([MNopedia, Rondo Neighborhood](https://www3.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/rondo-neighborhood-st-paul)). Interstate 94, built between 1956 and 1968, cut it in half.

:::fact
In Rondo, 600 families lost their homes to Interstate 94 ([MNopedia, Neighborhood Resistance to I-94](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/neighborhood-resistance-i-94-1953-1965)).
:::

MNopedia compares Rondo with mostly white, professional Merriam Park, where well-connected residents won several changes. In Rondo, it concludes, a "lack of political clout, along with racial and class bias, left Rondo vulnerable" ([MNopedia, Neighborhood Resistance to I-94](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/neighborhood-resistance-i-94-1953-1965)). For anyone who works in public process, the lesson is pointed. A process can be open to everyone and still respond most to people who already know how it works.

**Schools.** Minnesota's graduation rate reached a record 85 percent for the class of 2025, with gains among Black, Asian and Native American students and students in special education ([MPR News, April 3, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/04/03/minnesota-graduation-rates-hit-record-high-in-2025)). Even so, Hamline University political scientist David Schultz wrote in 2025 that outcomes for students of color "are among the nation's worst" ([Duluth News Tribune, June 26, 2025](https://www.duluthnewstribune.com/opinion/columns/state-view-minnesota-exceptionalism-it-turns-out-was-a-myth)). Scholars disagree about the causes. Myron Orfield of the University of Minnesota Law School has argued that ending desegregation efforts made gaps worse, while Myers does not see housing segregation as the main cause ([CBS Minnesota, 2020](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/the-minnesota-paradox-why-the-land-of-10000-lakes-has-one-of-the-largest-racial-disparities/)).

:::note
Content note: This passage describes the 1920 lynchings in Duluth. Readers may pause or skip to the next section.
:::

**Duluth, 1920.** On June 15, 1920, three Black circus workers, Elias Clayton, Elmer Jackson and Isaac McGhie, were lynched by a mob in Duluth after a white couple accused six Black circus workers of robbery and rape. A doctor who examined the young woman found no evidence of rape. No one was convicted of murder ([MNopedia, Duluth Lynchings](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/duluth-lynchings)). MNopedia notes that lynching "is widely believed to be something that happened only in the South." The lynchings were then "mostly forgotten or ignored" until 1991, when the men's graves received headstones. A memorial and an annual day of remembrance followed ([MNopedia, Duluth Lynchings](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/duluth-lynchings)). The long forgetting is part of the cultural story too.

## 2020: a hard look at culture

:::note
Content note: This section refers to the police killing of George Floyd in 2020 and to findings about police use of force. Readers may pause or skip to the next section.
:::

On May 25, 2020, George Floyd, a 46-year-old Black man, was killed by a Minneapolis police officer during an arrest. Myers opened his account of the Minnesota paradox by asking how this could happen in a state "known for being 'nice'" ([Humphrey School](https://www.hhh.umn.edu/research-centers/roy-wilkins-center-human-relations-and-social-justice/minnesota-paradox)).

In April 2022, the Minnesota Department of Human Rights found probable cause that the City of Minneapolis and its police department had engaged in a pattern of race discrimination. Its findings included more severe force used against Black people than white people in similar circumstances ([Minnesota Department of Human Rights, April 27, 2022](https://mn.gov/mdhr/assets/Investigation%20into%20the%20City%20of%20Minneapolis%20and%20the%20Minneapolis%20Police%20Department_tcm1061-526417.pdf)).

What matters most for this course is the report's main conclusion. The department wrote that the pattern was caused "primarily by an organizational culture," and that without real culture change, new policies and trainings "will be meaningless." It said culture is shaped by the tone leaders set, how norms are taught and reinforced, and what happens when they are broken ([MDHR, 2022](https://mn.gov/mdhr/assets/Investigation%20into%20the%20City%20of%20Minneapolis%20and%20the%20Minneapolis%20Police%20Department_tcm1061-526417.pdf)). The report concerns one police department, and nothing here suggests it describes DHS. But its idea, that culture can outlast policy, applies to any public institution, including ours.

## Repair, equity work and an honest debate

Since 2020, and before, Minnesotans have worked on this history in many ways.

**Repairing the record.** The covenant discharge law, Mapping Prejudice's volunteer mapping, the state's 2020 pardon of Max Mason, a Black man convicted in a case related to the Duluth lynchings, and the work of Duluth's memorial committee all address history directly ([Mapping Prejudice](https://mappingprejudice.umn.edu/discharging-racial-covenants); [MNopedia, Duluth Lynchings](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/duluth-lynchings)). Rondo's residents have kept their neighborhood's memory alive through Rondo Days since 1983 ([MNopedia, Rondo](https://www3.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/rondo-neighborhood-st-paul)).

**State government.** Executive Order 23-12, signed in 2023, continued the state's diversity and equity councils, noted the appointment of the state's first Chief Equity Officer, and directed agencies to carry out a long-range equity plan ([Executive Order 23-12](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/archive/execorders/23-12.pdf)). An earlier order said a person's opportunities "should not depend on their race, identity, ability," place of origin, place of worship or zip code ([Executive Order 21-13](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/archive/execorders/21-13.pdf)).

**Community-led work.** Much of the most important equity work is led by communities themselves, including newsrooms such as the Minnesota Spokesman-Recorder, which launched an essay series on the paradox in 2024 ([Minnesota Spokesman-Recorder, Nov. 14, 2024](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2024/11/14/minnesota-paradox-black-disparities/)).

**The debate.** Equity work has critics, and their views are part of Minnesota's civic life. In 2021, Catrin Wigfall, a policy fellow at the Center of the American Experiment, argued that equality means equal opportunity, while equity means guaranteeing equal outcomes, which in her view is "at its core, discrimination" ([Post Bulletin, June 8, 2021](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/local/center-for-the-american-experiment-presenters-criticize-use-of-critical-race-theory-in-minnesota-schools)). By 2025, federal executive orders had ended diversity, equity and inclusion programs across the federal government. Minnesota State Chancellor Scott Olson defended his system's equity work, saying "Minnesota needs everybody in the workforce" and describing equity in terms of first-generation and low-income students as well as students of color ([Post Bulletin, Jan. 25, 2025](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/local/dei-is-being-banished-in-government-and-business-but-not-in-higher-education-in-minnesota)).

You do not need to pick a side here. It helps to notice that people of good faith disagree about the words "equity" and "equality," and that the majority culture's habit of "treat everyone the same" sits at the center of that disagreement.

## Try it

:::scenario
Example for discussion. This is a composite written for teaching, not a real case.
Your DSD team just held a meeting with community partners about changes to how families learn about a waiver service. To you, it went smoothly: no one objected, and the group agreed to move ahead. Afterward, your colleague Dana, who is Black and has worked with several of the families involved, tells you she saw it differently. She says two partners went quiet after their first comments were passed over, and she felt her own concern was thanked and then dropped.
? What do you do next?
- Explain what you saw: no one objected, so it seemed like real agreement. || This may feel like reassurance to you, but it can tell Dana that her view counts for less than yours. Sociologist Danielle Lavin-Loucks has written that a smile and a nod can look like agreement when they are not ([The Society Pages, July 3, 2020](https://thesocietypages.org/specials/rethinking-minnesota-nice/)), so your view of the room may be the less complete one.
- Thank her, ask what she noticed, and ask whether the two of you could follow up with the partners who went quiet. || This may take more time, and it may surface concerns the group then has to address. It treats Dana's outside view as information you could not see from where you sat, and it moves toward the people whose voices were lost, not just toward the record of the meeting.
- Thank her and suggest she raise her concern with the meeting lead. || This may respect her voice, and the lead may need to hear it. It may also leave the work of speaking up with the person who already felt passed over. Rondo's history shows that processes tend to respond most to people who already know how to work them, so offering to go with her, or raising it yourself, may help.
:::

## Reflect

:::reflect
- Who have I invited into my home, or my circle at work, in the last year whom I did not know five years ago?
- What did I learn growing up about race in Minnesota, and what did I learn later? Who taught me each?
- When a problem arises in a community, do I look first for causes inside our systems, or outside them?
:::

## Take away

:::takeaway
Seek out one perspective you would not usually hear. This week, ask a colleague, partner or family member from outside your own background how a recent meeting, letter or process looked to them, then listen without explaining what you meant.
:::
