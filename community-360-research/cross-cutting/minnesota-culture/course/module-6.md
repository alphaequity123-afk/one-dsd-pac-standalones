## Opening

Few things are more Minnesotan than hotdish. It is a potluck staple, and Minnesota's members of Congress even hold a hotdish contest. In 2019, Rep. Ilhan Omar took second place with her "Little Moga-Hot-dishu." In 2021, chef Yia Vang published a Hmong hotdish recipe ([MNopedia, Hotdish](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/hotdish-mnopedia)).

There is a gentle joke in there that many Minnesotans will recognize: nothing says "you belong here" like being handed a casserole. But there is also something serious. The dish did not stay the same. New Minnesotans picked it up and changed it, and the old Minnesotans ate it.

This last module is about that kind of change. You have spent five modules looking at the water we swim in. Now the question is what to do with what you have seen: which lenses help, how to use them without turning them into labels, and which strengths of Minnesota culture are worth carrying forward on purpose.

## Six ways of meeting difference

Milton Bennett, an intercultural researcher, describes six stages in how people experience cultural difference. He groups the first three as "ethnocentric," meaning a person's own culture feels more central to reality. He groups the last three as "ethnorelative," meaning all cultures are experienced as different ways of organizing reality ([Bennett, 2017, Intercultural Development Research Institute](https://www.idrinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/DMIS-IDRI.pdf)). In plain words, drawn from his summary:

- **Denial.** Other cultures are noticed only vaguely, if at all. An organization at this stage has no way of recognizing cultural difference.
- **Defense.** Difference is seen as "us and them," often with "us" on top. Bennett also describes a reversed form, where a person idealizes another culture and criticizes their own.
- **Minimization.** Differences are played down in favor of what people share. The catch is that the "shared" things are often based on one's own worldview.
- **Acceptance.** People become curious about difference and see other cultures as just as complex as their own.
- **Adaptation.** People can shift their point of view and their behavior to communicate across cultures.
- **Integration.** People move in and out of cultural worldviews as part of who they are.

([Bennett, 2017](https://www.idrinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/DMIS-IDRI.pdf))

Acceptance has a trap of its own. Bennett warns against a stance that says "it's not bad or good, it's just different." The task, in his view, is to keep making judgments, but judgments that are not built only on your own culture's yardstick ([Bennett, 2017](https://www.idrinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/DMIS-IDRI.pdf)).

:::voice
Bennett calls the "it's just different" stance "naïve and paralytic" ([Bennett, 2017](https://www.idrinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/DMIS-IDRI.pdf)).
:::

Two things are important to say right away. First, this is a mirror, not a test. Nothing in this module measures you, and no one is being placed on a stage. Second, Bennett names the main criticism of his own model: its assumption of steady, step-by-step progress may reflect a "Western" or "male" bias ([Bennett, 2017](https://www.idrinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/DMIS-IDRI.pdf)). Many people find themselves in more than one stage depending on the day, the topic or the person across the table.

## Whose "same"?

The stage most worth a close look in a Minnesota workplace is Minimization. Bennett writes that organizations at this stage "tend to exaggerate the benefits of unbiased equal opportunity," which can mask "the continued operation of dominant culture privilege" ([Bennett, 2017](https://www.idrinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/DMIS-IDRI.pdf)).

Here is why that matters. Many of us were raised to believe that fairness means treating everyone the same. That is a real value, and a good one. But "the same" always has a starting point. If the standard form, the standard meeting and the standard plan were all designed around one kind of household, then treating everyone "the same" quietly treats that household as the norm.

The essay that goes with this course notes that fairness can be defined two ways: as identical treatment, or as treatment that gets people to the same place. People of good faith disagree about which matters more. In Minnesota's public debate, critics such as Catrin Wigfall of the Center of the American Experiment have argued that pursuing equal outcomes requires favoring one group over another ([Post Bulletin, June 8, 2021](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/local/center-for-the-american-experiment-presenters-criticize-use-of-critical-race-theory-in-minnesota-schools)). This module does not ask you to settle that argument. It asks you to notice the starting point hiding inside your own idea of "the same."

The fit with Minnesota's majority culture is worth thinking about, as a question. A culture that values treating everyone alike, likes to stress what people share, avoids open conflict and takes pride in being fair may find Minimization comfortable and natural. University of Minnesota economist Samuel Myers Jr. has described Minnesotans saying "the only race is the human race" ([CBS Minnesota, 2020](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/the-minnesota-paradox-why-the-land-of-10000-lakes-has-one-of-the-largest-racial-disparities/)). Sociologist Danielle Lavin-Loucks has written about colorblindness in Minnesota nice ([The Society Pages, 2020](https://thesocietypages.org/specials/rethinking-minnesota-nice/)). Those sound like what Bennett describes. But that is an observation about a cultural style, not a measurement. No study measuring the intercultural development of Minnesotans, of state employees or of DHS staff was found. Many individual Minnesotans will recognize themselves in other stages entirely.

## Averages, not people

This course has offered several frameworks: Hall's high and low context, Hofstede's dimensions, Meyer's scales, Rentfrow's regional personality map, the tight-and-loose ranking of states, and now Bennett's stages. Each one describes averages or ideal types. They are most useful as mirrors, for noticing your own default settings. They are least useful as labels for other people.

The researchers themselves give good reasons for care.

- Rentfrow and colleagues, whose study found Minnesota among the highest states on agreeableness, say their regional labels give "only a broad sense" of the profiles. Their data came largely from people who volunteered to take online personality tests ([Rentfrow et al., 2013, American Psychological Association](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/psp-a0034434.pdf)).
- A 2011 review of research using Hall's high and low context found that most country classifications are "flawed or, at best, very limited" ([Kittler, Rygl and Mackinnon, 2011, University of Stirling repository](https://www.stir.ac.uk/research/hub/publication/818365)).
- Some measures leave out what may matter most. A 2014 study ranked Minnesota 28th of 50 states on "tightness," near the middle, with an index built from items such as corporal punishment in schools, marijuana penalties and religiosity ([Harrington and Gelfand, 2014, via PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4050535/)). An index built mostly from laws and punishments may not capture norms enforced by quiet disapproval. Whether Minnesota works that way is a question, not a finding.
- Even the numbers can disagree. Hofstede's published data set scores the United States 91 on individualism ([Hofstede dimension data, 2015 version](https://geerthofstede.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/6-dimensions-for-website-2015-08-16.csv)), while The Culture Factor, which now publishes Hofstede-based profiles, lists 60 ([The Culture Factor, country comparison tool](https://www.theculturefactor.com/country-comparison-tool?countries=united+states)).

:::fact
Rentfrow's regional personality study drew on five samples totaling more than 1.5 million people, and still describes averages, not any one Minnesotan ([Rentfrow et al., 2013](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/psp-a0034434.pdf)).
:::

Researchers warn against moving from a group average to a single person, a mistake sometimes called a stereotype. A useful test: if a framework is helping you ask a better question about yourself or your process, it is working. If it is helping you predict what a particular family or coworker "will" do, set it down.

## Strengths worth keeping

Seeing a culture clearly includes seeing what it does well. Minnesota's majority culture brings real strengths to this work.

**Fairness and civic commitment.** Minnesotans show up. In 2023, 40.3 percent of Minnesotans 16 and older said they had volunteered in the past year, third among the states ([America's Health Rankings, volunteerism](https://www.americashealthrankings.org/explore/measures/volunteerism_d/MN)). The political scientist Daniel Elazar described a culture that sees government as a positive force and expects clean, principled public service ([Elazar](https://dje.jcpa.org/articles2/minn3.htm)). At its best, that is exactly the ethic a public agency needs.

:::fact
In 2024, 76.35 percent of eligible Minnesotans voted, second in the nation, and 62 percent of those 18 to 29 voted, first in the nation ([KSTP, citing the Minnesota Secretary of State](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/local-news/minnesotans-ranked-2nd-in-voter-turnout-1st-among-young-voters/)).
:::

**Follow-through and patience.** The cooperative creameries and the long negotiation of the 1971 Minnesota Miracle show a culture that can pool resources and keep talking until a fair deal emerges ([MNopedia, Clarks Grove](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/clarks-grove-cooperative-creamery); [MNopedia, Minnesota Miracle](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/minnesota-miracle-legislation)).

**Care.** Politeness, helpfulness and a reluctance to embarrass people are real assets in hard conversations with families, as long as they come paired with honesty. Modesty can make room for listening and for the humility this work asks of us.

**A record of rights and reckoning.** Minnesota passed civil rights protections early, its families and self-advocates helped close the state institutions, and its law now requires that informed choice be offered in culturally and linguistically appropriate ways ([MNopedia, Minnesota Human Rights Act](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/minnesota-human-rights-act); [The Arc Minnesota](https://arcminnesota.org/about/why-the-arc/history-of-the-arc-in-minnesota/); [Minn. Stat. 256B.4905](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.4905)). Projects like Remembering With Dignity's named gravestones show Minnesotans facing hard history in public ([MNopedia, Remembering With Dignity](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/remembering-dignity)).

Each strength has a shadow covered earlier in this course. Trust in process can become the belief that holding a meeting means you listened. The 2021 Twin Cities belonging effort heard frustration at "over-engagement," with people giving input again and again without seeing it reflected in the result ([The Journey to Belonging in the Twin Cities](https://www.stpaul.gov/sites/default/files/2022-04/The%20Journey%20to%20Belonging%20in%20the%20Twin%20Cities%20Report.pdf)). Follow-through is the strength that answers that shadow: going back to people and showing what changed.

:::voice
Bennett asks how often intercultural competence counts in "hiring or advancement" ([Bennett, 2017](https://www.idrinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/DMIS-IDRI.pdf)).
:::

Questions like Bennett's turn a framework into practice. They are not about who you are. They are about what you, and we, do next.

## Try it

:::scenario
Example for discussion. This is a composite written for teaching, not a real case.
Think back to the planning meeting in Module 5, now with new choices. Sam, the 24-year-old with a developmental disability, lives with his parents and older sister, and the family has said they want to make decisions together and keep his care at home. Your plan template leans toward goals like living on his own and working in the community. A colleague reminds you, kindly, "We offer every family the same options."
? Using what you have seen in this module, what do you do next?
- Agree with your colleague, walk the family through the standard options in the usual order, and note their preference for home care at the end. || This may feel fair, because every family hears the same thing. But the "same" here starts from one picture of a good life, which is what Bennett means by Minimization. The family may leave feeling their preference was tolerated rather than planned for.
- Ask Sam, and then his family, what a good life looks like to them and who usually helps decide what, then build the plan from their answers while still explaining every option in plain language. || This may take longer, and it uses Minnesota's patience and care on purpose. Asking Sam first, and making sure the options are truly understood, keeps his own voice in the plan. Recording who helps whom at home can show interdependence as a strength, not a gap.
- Decide the family's culture clearly favors home care, skip the other options, and finish the plan quickly to respect their wishes. || This may come from real respect. But it uses a group pattern to predict one family, which is the mistake this module warns about. Sam and his family may have wanted to hear the choices, and his own wishes may differ from the family's or from any average.
:::

## Take away

:::takeaway
Choose one habit to practice for the next month, such as asking every family "What does a good life look like to you?" or closing each meeting by asking who has not yet weighed in. Put a weekly note on your calendar to jot down one moment when the habit changed what you heard, and look back over those notes at the end of the month.
