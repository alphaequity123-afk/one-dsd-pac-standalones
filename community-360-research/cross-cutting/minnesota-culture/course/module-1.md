## Opening

Here is some humor, written by Minnesotans about Minnesotans. In 1987 Howard Mohr, a writer for *A Prairie Home Companion*, published *How to Talk Minnesotan: A Visitor's Guide* ([MPR News, Sept. 6, 2022](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2022/09/06/author-howard-mohr-known-for-how-to-talk-minnesotan-dies)). It taught visitors that "That's different" is "a blanket reply on neutral ground." It explained that a guest must refuse food and the host must insist. And it described the "Minnesota Long Goodbye," which ends with the guests in the car and the hosts bent at the driver's window ([Duluth News Tribune via Echo Press, Sept. 6, 2022](https://www.echopress.com/lifestyle/arts-and-entertainment/front-row-seat-remembering-howard-mohr-and-how-to-talk-minnesotan)).

If you smiled, you probably recognized something. That is the point. Habits like these feel like plain good manners to the people who grew up with them. To someone from elsewhere, they can look like a set of rules nobody wrote down. This module is about that gap: what it means that the way "we" do things is a culture, too.

## What the fish cannot see

In a 2005 commencement speech, the novelist David Foster Wallace told a short parable. Two young fish meet an older fish, who asks them how the water is. Later, one young fish turns to the other and asks, "What the hell is water?" Wallace's point was that the most obvious and important realities are often the hardest to see and talk about ([Farnam Street transcript of Wallace's Kenyon speech](https://fs.blog/david-foster-wallace-this-is-water/)).

Most training on culture asks staff to learn about other people's cultures. That matters. But the culture a person grew up in rarely feels like a culture at all. It feels like "normal," the neutral background against which everyone else looks "cultural." For many people in Minnesota's public agencies, mainstream Minnesota culture is the water.

:::voice
Milton Bennett writes that people often experience their own culture as more "central to reality" than others ([Bennett, 2017](https://www.idrinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/DMIS-IDRI.pdf)).
:::

Bennett, an intercultural researcher, adds a second point that is easy to miss. The categories people have for seeing their own culture are far more detailed than the ones they have for anyone else's ([Bennett, Developmental Model of Intercultural Sensitivity, IDRI, 2017](https://www.idrinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/DMIS-IDRI.pdf)). If you grew up here, you may be able to tell many kinds of Minnesota "no" apart. You may have far fewer ways to read a "no" from a family whose culture differs from yours.

Put plainly, the people least likely to notice how culture shapes a meeting, a form or a service plan are often the people whose culture shaped them. That is not a flaw in anyone. It is how being inside a culture works.

Seeing your own culture is not the same as criticizing it. Minnesota's majority culture has real gifts, from its cooperatives and civic habits to its volunteering and its early civil rights laws. Later modules look at those strengths with respect. It has also, by the testimony of many Minnesotans of color, immigrants and newcomers, made some things hard to say out loud. Both can be true at once.

Three cautions apply throughout this course:

- **Patterns are not people.** A pattern found across a state or a country describes an average. It cannot tell you how a particular coworker, family or client thinks.
- **"Minnesota culture" is plural.** The majority culture grew mostly from Northern European, Protestant and Catholic, farm and small-town roots. Minnesotans of every background now share in it and change it, and many live in more than one culture at once.
- **Evidence varies in strength.** Some points rest on large surveys. Others rest on interviews, essays or humor. The course tells you which is which.

Even the humor in the opening comes with a caution. Writing after Mohr's death, Jay Gabler noted that the book described how one strain of Minnesotans talked, not all Minnesotans ([Duluth News Tribune via Echo Press, Sept. 6, 2022](https://www.echopress.com/lifestyle/arts-and-entertainment/front-row-seat-remembering-howard-mohr-and-how-to-talk-minnesotan)).

:::voice
Gabler called it "a certain, then culturally dominant, strain" of Minnesotans ([Echo Press, 2022](https://www.echopress.com/lifestyle/arts-and-entertainment/front-row-seat-remembering-howard-mohr-and-how-to-talk-minnesotan)).
:::

## Who Minnesotans are now

If the majority culture is the water, it helps to know who is swimming in it. The numbers show a majority that is still large, and a state that is changing fast.

**The majority, counted more than one way.** The Census Bureau's 2024 American Community Survey counts 5,793,151 Minnesotans. Of those, 4,329,205, or 74.7 percent, identified as white alone and not Hispanic ([Census Bureau, ACS 2024, table B03002, via Census Reporter](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B03002&geo_ids=04000US27)). Counting everyone who marked white as their only race, Hispanic or not, the share is 75.9 percent. Counting everyone who marked white alone or with another race, it is 82.8 percent ([ACS 2024, tables B02001 and B02008](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B02001,B02008,B05012,B05002&geo_ids=04000US27)). The definition changes the number, so it is worth naming which one you use.

Minnesota Compass reports about 74 percent of Minnesotans as non-Hispanic white, down from 83 percent in 2010, and about 1.5 million Minnesotans of color, or 26 percent ([Minnesota Compass, by race and ethnicity](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/race-ethnicity)).

**The young are far more diverse than the old.** This may be the single most important fact for a public agency.

:::fact
People of color are 7 percent of Minnesotans 80 and older, but 42 percent of children under 5 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/race-ethnicity)).
:::

The people who lead and staff institutions tend to be older than the people those institutions will serve in twenty years.

**Immigrants, then and now.** About 9 percent of Minnesotans were born abroad in 2024, up from 5 percent in 2000. That is far below the 37 percent of the late 1800s. One in five Minnesota children is the child of an immigrant, and Mexican and Somali immigrants are the two largest immigrant groups ([Minnesota Compass, by immigrant group](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/immigration)). In other words, today's majority culture was itself built by immigrants, at a time when the share of newcomers was about four times what it is now.

**Homegrown.** Of everyone living in Minnesota in 2024, about 66 percent were born here ([ACS 2024, table B05002](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B02001,B02008,B05012,B05002&geo_ids=04000US27)). In 2012 MPR News reported that about seven in ten Minnesotans were born in the state, against roughly six in ten nationally ([MPR News, March 12, 2012](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2012/03/12/outsiders1-is-minnesota-nice-to-newcomers)). A large homegrown majority is one reason local ways of doing things can feel settled, and hard for newcomers to enter. Module 2 returns to that.

**Metro and Greater Minnesota.** About 55 percent of Minnesotans live in the Twin Cities region and about 45 percent in Greater Minnesota ([Minnesota Compass, all Minnesotans, 2020 to 2024](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/minnesota)). The majority culture shows up in both places, with different accents.

## Who works in state government

Now turn the lens on ourselves. Minnesota Management and Budget's workforce report for fiscal year 2023 (July 2022 to June 2023) counts about 37,100 executive branch positions across 87 agencies, boards and commissions. DHS is the largest agency, with about one in five executive branch employees ([MMB, State of Minnesota Workforce Planning Report FY 2023](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2024/other/240792.pdf)).

- **Race and ethnicity.** About 15.9 percent of executive branch employees identified as Black, Indigenous or People of Color (BIPOC), 78.5 percent as white, and about 5 percent did not report race. That compares with 20.4 percent BIPOC in the Minnesota labor force. Hispanic and Latino workers are the most underrepresented, at 2.9 percent of state workers against 5.4 percent of the labor force. BIPOC employees were 21 percent of new hires in FY 2023, up from 11 percent in FY 2011 ([MMB FY 2023](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2024/other/240792.pdf)).
- **Disability.** Employees with disabilities were 11.6 percent of the executive branch, about twice their 5.9 percent share of the state labor force in the report's comparison ([MMB FY 2023](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2024/other/240792.pdf)).
- **Age.** The median age of state workers was 46, and about 40 percent were 50 or older. The median age of new hires was 36 ([MMB FY 2023](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2024/other/240792.pdf)).

:::fact
The report lists 7,360 DHS employees: 20.0 percent people of color, 78.1 percent white, and 12.1 percent reporting a disability ([MMB FY 2023, appendix table](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2024/other/240792.pdf)).
:::

So DHS is somewhat more racially diverse than the executive branch as a whole, and close to the state's labor force. The report does not compare DHS staff with the people DHS serves. Set beside Compass's finding about the youngest Minnesotans, though, the age gap between state staff and the state's future population is clear.

What these numbers do not show matters just as much. The workforce report covers race, sex, disability, veteran status and age ([MMB FY 2023](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2024/other/240792.pdf)). No published source was found that says where state or DHS employees grew up, how many were born in Minnesota, what languages they speak, or what religious or cultural backgrounds they come from. Race is not the same as culture. A white employee may have grown up in a recent immigrant family. An employee of color may have grown up entirely inside Minnesota's majority culture. The numbers describe who is in the room, not how anyone in it thinks.

## A mirror, not a label

So why start a course here? Because the habits of a majority culture tend to become the default settings of its institutions. In a public agency, those settings show up in how meetings run, how feedback is given, how forms are worded, and what counts as a "good" plan. When most of the people setting the defaults share one culture, the defaults can feel neutral. They are not neutral. They are simply familiar to some people and not to others.

This course uses a few frameworks from researchers, starting with Bennett's. Each one is offered as a mirror for noticing your own default settings, not as a score or a label for you or anyone else. No study measuring the intercultural development of Minnesotans, state employees or DHS staff was found. So this course cannot tell you where Minnesotans "are." It can only invite you to look.

A good first step is small. Start noticing the moments when "normal" turns out to mean "how we do it here."

## Try it

:::scenario
Example for discussion. This is a composite written for teaching, not a real case.
At a DSD team meeting, Sam, who moved to Minnesota from another state six months ago, proposes a new way to schedule follow-up calls with families. A longtime team member, Karen, says, "Well, that's different," and the meeting moves on. After the meeting, Sam tells you he is glad the team liked his idea and plans to start next week. You have worked with Karen for years, and you suspect she has doubts she did not say out loud.
? What do you do?
- Say nothing. Karen will raise her concerns if they matter, and it is not your place to step in. || This may protect everyone from an awkward moment today. It may also leave Sam acting on a meaning he could not read, and Karen's concern may surface later as quiet resistance. Silence can feel neutral from inside the culture while it leaves a newcomer outside it.
- Tell Sam, "Around here, 'that's different' usually means 'no.' I'd drop it." || This may help Sam learn a local code, which is a real kindness. It also assumes you know what Karen meant, and it may end a useful idea before anyone has said why. One pattern does not describe one person, and the decoding is still a guess.
- Check in with Karen privately: "I wasn't sure how you meant 'that's different.' Sam heard it as a yes. Would you be willing to tell him what you're thinking?" || This may help both of them. It treats the phrase as a cultural habit rather than a fault, and it invites Karen to say her view plainly to the person who needs it. It also gives Sam real feedback instead of a code to crack.
:::

- What did "being polite" mean in the home I grew up in? What was I taught not to talk about?

## Take away

:::takeaway
This week, notice one moment at work when "normal" was really "how we do it here." It might be a phrase, a meeting habit or a line on a form. Write it down, and ask who might experience it differently.
:::
