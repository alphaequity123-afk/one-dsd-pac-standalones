# Minnesota Culture: The Water We Swim In
Checked: October 31, 2026

A few sections of this essay carry a brief content note before material that describes violence, deaths or abuse. Readers may pause there or skip ahead to the next section. The rest of the essay discusses race, politics, poverty and disability in ordinary terms, without notes.

This essay is written for people who work at the Minnesota Department of Human Services, and especially in its Disability Services Division. Most of us grew up inside Minnesota's majority culture, or have lived and worked in it long enough to absorb its habits. The aim is to look at that culture the way a cross-cultural researcher would look at any other: with real affection for what it does well, honest attention to what it tends not to see, and care not to turn a pattern into a verdict on any one person. Each idea is tied to its source. Where the research is national rather than Minnesotan, or a popular theory rather than research, the text says so.

## 1. Why look at our own culture

Most training on culture asks staff to learn about other people's cultures. That work matters, and the companion profiles in this series do it in depth. But there is a gap in that approach. The culture a person grew up in rarely feels like a culture at all. It feels like normal, the neutral background against which everyone else looks "cultural."

The novelist David Foster Wallace put this in a parable in his 2005 commencement speech at Kenyon College. Two young fish meet an older fish who asks how the water is, and one of the young fish later asks the other, "What the hell is water?" Wallace's own summary was that the most obvious, important realities are often the hardest to see and talk about ([Farnam Street transcript of Wallace's Kenyon speech, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://fs.blog/david-foster-wallace-this-is-water/)). That is the spirit of this essay. For many people in Minnesota's public agencies, mainstream Minnesota culture is the water.

Intercultural researchers have a more formal way of saying the same thing. Milton Bennett, whose model of intercultural development is described in section 14, writes that at the earliest stages people experience their own culture as more "central to reality" than others, and that the categories they have for perceiving their own culture are far more complex than those they have for anyone else's ([Bennett, Developmental Model of Intercultural Sensitivity, IDRI, 2017](https://www.idrinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/DMIS-IDRI.pdf)). The company that publishes a widely used questionnaire based on his model goes further. It describes one common way of overlooking cultural difference as coming from "limited cultural self-understanding," and says this form is more often experienced by members of a dominant group ([Intercultural Development Inventory, the Intercultural Development Continuum, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://idiinventory.com/idc)). Put plainly: the people least likely to notice how culture shapes a meeting, a form or a service plan are often the people whose culture shaped them.

Seeing one's own culture is not the same as criticizing it. Minnesota's majority culture has given the state real gifts, from its cooperatives and civic habits to its volunteering and its early civil rights laws, and those are described here with respect. It has also, by the testimony of many Minnesotans of color, immigrants and newcomers, made some things hard to say out loud. Both are true. A public servant who can see both is better equipped to serve a state that is changing fast.

Three cautions apply throughout.

- **Patterns are not people.** A pattern found across a state, a region or a country describes an average. It cannot tell anyone how a particular coworker, family or client thinks. Many of the researchers cited here say so directly.
- **"Minnesota culture" is plural.** The state's majority culture grew mostly from Northern European, Protestant and Catholic, farm and small-town roots. Minnesotans of every background now share in it and change it. Many Minnesotans also live in more than one culture at once.
- **Evidence varies in strength.** Some claims below rest on large surveys or published studies. Others rest on interviews, essays or humor. Each is labeled.

## 2. Who Minnesotans are: majority and change

**The majority, counted two ways.** The Census Bureau's 2024 American Community Survey counts 5,793,151 Minnesotans. Of those, 4,329,205 people, or 74.7 percent, identified as white alone and not Hispanic, give or take about 5,700 people ([Census Bureau, ACS 2024 one-year, table B03002, via Census Reporter, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B03002&geo_ids=04000US27)). Counting everyone who marked white as their only race, Hispanic or not, the figure is 4,398,985, or 75.9 percent. Counting everyone who marked white alone or with another race, it is 4,797,907, or 82.8 percent ([ACS 2024, tables B02001 and B02008](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B02001,B02008,B05012,B05002&geo_ids=04000US27)). Which definition is used changes the number, so it matters to name it.

Minnesota Compass, rounding and using its own survey period, reports about 4.3 million non-Hispanic white Minnesotans, 74 percent, down from 83 percent in 2010, and about 1.5 million Minnesotans of color, 26 percent ([Minnesota Compass, by race and ethnicity, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/race-ethnicity)).

**The young are far more diverse than the old.** Compass reports that people of color are 7 percent of Minnesotans 80 and older but 42 percent of children under 5 ([Minnesota Compass](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/race-ethnicity)). This single fact may matter more than any other for public agencies. The people who lead and staff institutions tend to be older than the people those institutions will serve in twenty years.

**Immigrants, then and now.** About 9 percent of Minnesotans were born abroad in 2024, up from 5 percent in 2000, but far below the 37 percent of the late 1800s. Compass also reports that one in five Minnesota children is the child of an immigrant, and that Mexican and Somali immigrants are the two largest immigrant groups ([Minnesota Compass, by immigrant group, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/immigration)). The ACS count is 523,549 foreign-born residents in 2024 ([ACS 2024, table B05012](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B02001,B02008,B05012,B05002&geo_ids=04000US27)). In other words, today's majority culture was itself built by immigrants, at a time when the share of newcomers was four times what it is now.

**Homegrown.** Of everyone living in Minnesota in 2024, 3,846,317, about 66 percent, were born in Minnesota ([ACS 2024, table B05002](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B02001,B02008,B05012,B05002&geo_ids=04000US27)). In 2012 MPR News reported that about seven in ten Minnesotans were born in the state, against roughly six in ten nationally, and quoted then recently retired state demographer Tom Gillaspy saying the homegrown share was shrinking ([MPR News, March 12, 2012](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2012/03/12/outsiders1-is-minnesota-nice-to-newcomers)). A large homegrown majority is one reason, discussed in section 4, that long-standing friendships and local ways of doing things can feel settled and hard to enter.

**Metro and Greater Minnesota.** Compass places 55.2 percent of Minnesotans in the Twin Cities region and 44.8 percent in Greater Minnesota ([Minnesota Compass, all Minnesotans, 2020 to 2024](https://www.mncompass.org/topics/demographics/cultural-communities/minnesota)). Rural Minnesota has its own profile in this series. The majority culture described here appears in both places, but with different accents. Section 7 describes the political divide between them.

**Gaps behind the averages.** In 2024 the median household income was $87,117 for all Minnesota households and $91,406 for white non-Hispanic households, but $53,653 for Black households. About 9.3 percent of all Minnesotans lived below the poverty line, compared with 7.4 percent of white non-Hispanic Minnesotans and 19.9 percent of Black Minnesotans ([ACS 2024, tables B19013 and B17001 with race iterations, via Census Reporter](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B19013,B19013H,B19013B,B17001,B17001H,B17001B&geo_ids=04000US27)). Minnesota Housing reports that in 2024, 77 percent of white households owned their homes, compared with 62 percent of Asian and Pacific Islander, 54 percent of Latino, 47 percent of Native American and 28 percent of Black households ([Minnesota Housing, homeownership rates dashboard, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://mnhousing.gov/home/policy-and-research/agency-plans/strategic-plan/address-homeownership-barriers-and-reduce-disparities/homeownership-rates-dashboard)). Section 6 returns to what these gaps mean.

## 3. Roots of mainstream Minnesota culture

A culture is partly a set of answers that earlier people worked out to the problems they faced. Minnesota's majority culture carries answers worked out by settlers, farmers, church members and organizers more than a century ago. Knowing where those answers came from helps explain why they still feel natural, and why they may not fit everyone.

**Land, first.** MNopedia, the Minnesota Historical Society's encyclopedia, describes the state's origins as "primarily about the taking of land" from Native people and its transfer to newcomers, through treaties and treaty violations, and after the US-Dakota War of 1862 ([MNopedia, Land, Labor, the Market, and Politics, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/land-labor-market-and-politics)). This essay does not discuss Dakota and Ojibwe nations as communities; that work belongs to the state's Tribal relations offices. But the settlement story that follows began on land that was taken, and an honest account of the majority culture says so.

**Scandinavian and German settlement.** Germans were the state's single largest ethnic group when the United States entered World War I in 1917 ([MNopedia, Anti-German Nativism, 1917–1919](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/anti-german-nativism-1917-1919)). Hundreds of thousands of Norwegians came between 1851 and 1920, making the Twin Cities what MNopedia calls "the unofficial capital of Norwegian America." MNopedia adds that internal religious and social conflicts shaped the group's experience as much as the climate did. Many first-generation families settled near one another because they feared extreme social isolation, and early Norwegian immigration was unusual in being made up of whole families who meant to stay ([MNopedia, Norwegian Immigration to Minnesota](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/norwegian-immigration-minnesota)). More Swedes settled in Minnesota than in any other state. MNopedia describes their churches as "hubs for social as well as religious activities," places that countered isolation and offered help, and notes that most devout Swedes attended Lutheran churches ([MNopedia, Swedish Immigration to Minnesota](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/swedish-immigration-minnesota)). The European American profile in this series describes these groups in more depth.

**Lutheran and Catholic institutions.** The church was not only a place of worship. It was school, social club, aid society and later social service agency. Lutheran Social Service and other agencies with immigrant roots remain part of the state's service system today, as the European American profile notes. Even Minnesota's signature dish has church roots: MNopedia traces the earliest known hotdish recipe to a 1930 cookbook of a Lutheran church women's society in Mankato, and says that after World War II it moved "out of the church rec room" into home kitchens, served at showers, funerals and rallies to express care ([MNopedia, Hotdish](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/hotdish-mnopedia)). Religion is changing fast now, as section 13 describes.

**Pressure to blend in.** The majority culture also learned to keep its head down. During World War I, the state's Commission of Public Safety, created in 1917 with what MNopedia calls "near-dictatorial powers," watched German Americans closely. In New Ulm, officials who questioned the draft were removed from office, and a college president was forced to resign over a speech ([MNopedia, Anti-German Nativism](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/anti-german-nativism-1917-1919)). Swedish Lutheran churches moved to English services in the 1920s and 1930s, a change MNopedia says was hastened in part by wartime pressure to Americanize ([MNopedia, Swedish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/swedish-immigration-minnesota)). It is a fair question, though no source opened here answers it, whether some of the majority culture's caution about standing out was learned in those years.

**Jante law.** Many Minnesotans point to a Scandinavian source for the culture's modesty. In a 2009 MPR essay, playwright Syl Jones traced "Minnesota nice" to the "Jante law" described by the Danish-Norwegian novelist Aksel Sandemose in the 1930s, unwritten rules of a fictional town that begin with "Don't think that you are special." Jones argued that Minnesota nice is "more about keeping up appearances" than about being nice ([MPR News, Dec. 14, 2009](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2009/12/14/syljones)). This is an essay, not a study. It is a common local explanation and worth knowing, but it describes a novel's town, not measured Minnesotans.

**Farm and small-town interdependence.** University of St. Thomas sociologist Lisa Waldner told MPR in 2019 that there is no definitive documentation of where Minnesota nice came from. She pointed to Northern European settlers who clustered by group, so that people "co-existed, but you didn't intermingle," and to rural life, where you might need a neighbor's help when your plow broke at harvest, so politeness won out over hostility ([MPR News, Nov. 21, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/11/21/ask-a-sotan-revisiting-minnesota-nice)). That explanation holds two values in tension that recur through this essay: a strong ethic of self-reliance, and a practical dependence on neighbors.

**Climate.** Winter is part of the story too. The Department of Natural Resources counts 11,842 lakes of 10 acres or more ([Minnesota DNR, lakes, rivers and wetlands facts](https://www.dnr.state.mn.us/faq/mnfacts/water.html)), and the long cold season shapes both social life and humor. The journalist Jay Gabler, writing about Howard Mohr's 1987 book *How to Talk Minnesotan*, summarized Mohr's view that Minnesota sociability rests on a stoicism born of the need to outlast brutal winters ([Duluth News Tribune via Echo Press, Sept. 6, 2022](https://www.echopress.com/lifestyle/arts-and-entertainment/front-row-seat-remembering-howard-mohr-and-how-to-talk-minnesotan)). University of Minnesota psychology researcher Grace Vieth has linked newcomers' isolation in part to long winters, when people stay with friends they already have ([University of Minnesota Department of Psychology, Sept. 2023](https://cla.umn.edu/psychology/news-events/story/vieth-navigating-social-isolation-newcomer-minnesota)).

**Cooperatives.** Minnesota's habit of solving problems together has a concrete history. In 1890 the Danish American community at Clarks Grove founded one of the state's first cooperative creameries. By 1898 there were more than 550 cooperative creameries in Minnesota, and by 1928 nearly half of all the cooperative creameries in the United States were in Minnesota, including what became Land O'Lakes ([MNopedia, Clarks Grove Cooperative Creamery](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/clarks-grove-cooperative-creamery)). MNopedia notes that for decades the creamery was also a community center for talks, meetings and school recitals. Pooling resources, sharing profits and losses, and meeting to decide are habits with deep roots here.

**Progressive and Farmer-Labor traditions.** Minnesota's Farmer-Labor Party, which MNopedia calls one of the most successful progressive third-party coalitions in American history, elected hundreds of candidates from 1917 into the 1940s, built clubs across the state, and grew from farmers' and workers' organizing against monopolies ([MNopedia, Minnesota Farmer-Labor Party, 1924–1944](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesota-farmer-labor-party-1924-1944)). Section 7 describes how political scientists have characterized the civic culture that came out of this history.

**Whose stories were left out.** The usual story of Minnesota's roots centers Scandinavian and German farmers. It tends to leave out the Dakota and Ojibwe people whose land this was; the Black Minnesotans of St. Paul's Rondo neighborhood and north Minneapolis, whose history is described in section 6; Mexican farmworkers, of whom MNopedia notes about 7,000 seasonal sugar beet workers as early as 1928 ([MNopedia, Land, Labor, the Market, and Politics](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/land-labor-market-and-politics)); and the women whose butter making and egg money MNopedia says often kept early farms afloat. A culture's self-image is a selection, and noticing what it leaves out is part of seeing it clearly.

## 4. Minnesota nice, decoded

"Minnesota nice" is a reputation, not a measured trait. No statewide study measuring Minnesotans' friendliness, indirectness or welcome to newcomers was found. What exists are interviews, essays, humor, journalism and a few pieces of regional research. Read together, they describe a recognizable set of habits, with a warm side and a guarded side.

### The warmth

The kind side of the reputation is real. Star Tribune culture reporter Rachel Hutton told NPR in 2024 that Minnesotans are "literally nice," meaning kind and polite, the sort of people who will help push a stranger's car out of a snowbank ([NPR, Aug. 9, 2024](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5065632)). There is data behind the civic part of that picture. In 2023, 40.3 percent of Minnesotans 16 and older said they had volunteered in the past year, third among the states, against 28.3 percent nationally ([America's Health Rankings, volunteerism, from the Census Bureau's Current Population Survey, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.americashealthrankings.org/explore/measures/volunteerism_d/MN)). Samuel Myers Jr., a University of Minnesota economist, describes a "sustained and vigorous corporate giving culture" in which the state's nonprofits are among the best known in America ([Humphrey School, The Minnesota Paradox, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.hhh.umn.edu/research-centers/roy-wilkins-center-human-relations-and-social-justice/minnesota-paradox)). And Minnesotans vote: 76.35 percent of eligible voters cast a ballot in 2024, second only to Wisconsin, and 62 percent of Minnesotans 18 to 29 voted, first in the nation ([KSTP, citing the Minnesota Secretary of State](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/local-news/minnesotans-ranked-2nd-in-voter-turnout-1st-among-young-voters/)).

Even critics grant the warmth. An immigration attorney who moved to Minnesota told MPR in 2012 that she had seen the state's "collective good will" toward people in need: "I know someone will write the check." Her next sentence was the harder one: she was not sure how good Minnesotans were at making refugees "really feel Minnesotan" ([MPR News, March 12, 2012](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2012/03/12/outsiders1-is-minnesota-nice-to-newcomers)). That pairing of generosity and distance runs through much of what follows.

### The indirectness

The best-known guide to Minnesota's way of talking is a work of humor. Howard Mohr, a writer for *A Prairie Home Companion* and an English professor in Marshall, published *How to Talk Minnesotan: A Visitor's Guide* in 1987, explaining the subtle meanings of phrases like "You bet" and "That's different" ([MPR News, Sept. 6, 2022](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2022/09/06/author-howard-mohr-known-for-how-to-talk-minnesotan-dies)). It should be read as humor, but as humor by a close observer. A Kirkus review summed up its picture of a place where things "could be worse," where poetry should rhyme, and where an offer of help is refused three times before it is accepted ([Kirkus Reviews](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/howard-mohr/how-to-talk-minnesotan-a-visitors-guide/)). Writing after Mohr's death in 2022, Jay Gabler recalled the book's lessons: "That's different" as "a blanket reply on neutral ground," a lesson titled "The Power of the Negative," the rule that a guest must refuse food and the host must insist, and the "Minnesota Long Goodbye," which ends with the guests in the car and the hosts bent at the driver's window ([Duluth News Tribune via Echo Press, Sept. 6, 2022](https://www.echopress.com/lifestyle/arts-and-entertainment/front-row-seat-remembering-howard-mohr-and-how-to-talk-minnesotan)). Gabler also noted the book's limits: it described how "a certain, then culturally dominant, strain" of Minnesotans talked, not all Minnesotans.

Journalists describe the same habits today. Hutton told NPR that if you disliked someone's hotdish at a potluck, you might call it "different" or "interesting" while quietly scraping it into the trash, and that in Minnesota speech calling someone "weird" is a harsh statement. Corey Bonnema, co-author of a transplant's guide, described a relative who asks "is it hot in here" rather than asking for the air conditioning ([NPR, Aug. 9, 2024](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5065632)).

**A framework: high and low context.** The anthropologist Edward T. Hall's distinction between high-context and low-context communication offers a way to think about this. In high-context communication, much of the meaning is carried by the setting, the relationship and what goes unsaid, and the listener is expected to decode it. In low-context communication, meaning is put into explicit words. A widely used open college textbook, summarizing Hall, places the United States and most of Northern Europe at the low-context end, with the guiding principle "say what you mean," and adds that in the United States or Scandinavia "yes" usually means yes ([Kirkwood Community College open textbook, Cultural Dimensions – Hall, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://kirkwood.pressbooks.pub/emcworthy/chapter/cultural-dimensions/)). The business researcher Erin Meyer, in her book *The Culture Map*, goes further and calls the United States "the lowest-context culture in the world" on her communicating scale, as summarized in a 2020 article on her work ([Welcome to the Jungle, June 1, 2020](https://www.welcometothejungle.com/en-GB/articles/keys-managing-multicultural-teams)).

Here Minnesota is interesting. By these national labels, Minnesotans belong to one of the most explicit cultures in the world. Yet "that's different," "is it hot in here," and a refusal that means "ask me again" are high-context moves: the speaker expects the listener to read the setting. One way to put the question, offered as a question rather than a finding, is this: does mainstream Minnesota speech run more high-context among insiders than the national label suggests, and does that help explain why newcomers, including newcomers from other parts of the United States, find it hard to read? Two cautions belong here. A 2011 systematic review of research using Hall's concept found that most country classifications rest on "seemingly less-than-adequate evidence" and are "flawed or, at best, very limited" ([Kittler, Rygl and Mackinnon, 2011, University of Stirling repository](https://www.stir.ac.uk/research/hub/publication/818365)). And no study measuring context in Minnesota speech was found.

**Feedback and disagreement.** Meyer's other scales, again as national background, place the United States in the middle on giving negative feedback: Americans, in her account, are trained to wrap negative messages in positive ones, softer than Northern Europeans such as the Dutch or Danes and more direct than many Asian cultures. On disagreeing, she places the United States in the middle as well, between confrontational cultures such as France, Germany and Israel and non-confrontational cultures such as Japan, Indonesia and Thailand ([Welcome to the Jungle, summarizing Meyer](https://www.welcometothejungle.com/en-GB/articles/keys-managing-multicultural-teams)). Meyer's own book page uses the example of American managers who precede negative feedback with three nice comments ([Erin Meyer, The Culture Map](https://erinmeyer.com/books/the-culture-map/)). If Minnesota's habits lean further toward the gentle end than the national average, as local humor and reporting suggest, then a Minnesota supervisor's criticism may be wrapped so softly that a colleague from a more direct culture does not hear it as criticism at all, and a colleague from a less direct culture may still hear it as harsh. That is a hypothesis to test in one's own workplace, not a finding.

### Conflict avoidance and passive aggression

The guarded side of Minnesota nice is the one Minnesotans joke about most. Hutton described "a habit of understatement" and a tendency, "instead of being direct," to be passive-aggressive, and Bonnema's co-author Jerilyn Veldof told NPR that passive aggressiveness gives the phrase its double meaning ([NPR, Aug. 9, 2024](https://www.npr.org/transcripts/nx-s1-5065632)). Danielle Lavin-Loucks, a sociologist who grew up in Stillwater and now teaches at Valparaiso University, wrote in 2020 that "avoidance of conflict is central." In her description, a smile, a nod and a change of subject, often to the weather, "appears like agreement" to others, while visible conflict, open disagreement, un-nice topics and complaints are what Minnesotans avoid ([The Society Pages, July 3, 2020](https://thesocietypages.org/specials/rethinking-minnesota-nice/)). That is one person's reflective essay, but the observation that silence can be mistaken for agreement matters a great deal inside public agencies, and section 10 returns to it.

### The newcomer freeze

MPR's 2012 "Outsiders" series heard from dozens of people who had moved to Minnesota and found the locals friendly but hard to befriend. Some said their only friends were other transplants. A newcomer from Fargo recalled being told that Minnesotans "will give you directions to anywhere except their own house." Todd Klingel, then president of the Minneapolis Regional Chamber of Commerce and a lifelong Minnesotan, said the chamber's focus groups with newcomers had told stories he found horrifying, and that feelings of isolation were even more common among professionals of color. He suggested a self-test: who have I invited into my house in the last year that I did not know five years ago? MPR added that Seattle has a similar reputation, so the pattern is not unique to Minnesota ([MPR News, March 12, 2012](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2012/03/12/outsiders1-is-minnesota-nice-to-newcomers)). The same story cited a 2003 report for the region's visitors association that concluded most transplants of color left the Twin Cities within two years. That report itself was not opened, so it is reported here only as MPR described it.

Vieth, the University of Minnesota researcher, explained the pattern in 2023 as a preference for long-term friendships: locals are willing to be friendly but often do not follow up, because existing friends already meet their social needs ([University of Minnesota, 2023](https://cla.umn.edu/psychology/news-events/story/vieth-navigating-social-isolation-newcomer-minnesota)). None of these sources measured friendship statewide. They describe a reputation that many newcomers recognize.

### What regional personality research says

The most systematic evidence on regional personality comes from psychologists who mapped average personality traits by state. In a 2013 study in the *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, Peter Rentfrow and colleagues analyzed five samples totaling more than 1.5 million people and found three regional profiles. One, which they called "Friendly & Conventional," was marked by high extraversion, agreeableness and conscientiousness and low openness, and was concentrated in the north central Great Plains and the South. The authors described the region as made up of people who are, on average, "sociable, considerate, dutiful, and traditional," socially and civically engaged, and likely to stay close to home ([Rentfrow et al., 2013, American Psychological Association](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/psp-a0034434.pdf)).

The study's appendix lists each state's average scores. Minnesota's agreeableness score, 61.6, is among the highest of the 48 states and District of Columbia listed (sixth by that table), and its openness score, 38.5, is among the lowest (44th of 49). Its extraversion and conscientiousness scores sit near the middle ([Rentfrow et al., 2013, appendix](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/psp-a0034434.pdf)). In plain terms, the data fit the "nice" part of the reputation (agreeable, considerate) and add something the reputation does not always mention: a lower average taste for novelty and change. The authors themselves caution that their labels give "only a broad sense" of the profiles, and the data came largely from people who volunteered to take online personality tests. State averages say nothing about any one Minnesotan.

## 5. How it looks from outside the majority

Many of the sharpest observations about Minnesota's majority culture come from people who stand partly outside it. What follows are specific voices and studies, each attributed. None of them speaks for a whole community.

**Black Minnesotans in the twentieth century.** In a 2002 MPR documentary on segregation in the North, Arthur McWatt, a St. Paul native and retired social studies teacher who writes about civil rights history, described employers who would accept a Black applicant's application and then throw it away, discrimination "done in a more subtle way than in the South." McWatt said Black Minnesotans learned that racist white people often had smiling faces and polite voices, and developed "an antenna" for insincerity. He also said Black Minnesotans generally felt safer here than in the South, and he attributed the state's relative tolerance partly to how few Black residents there were: the 1940 census showed African Americans as under 1 percent of Minneapolis and 1.5 percent of St. Paul ([MPR, "Up South," Feb. 12, 2002](https://archive.mpr.org/stories/2002/02/12/african-americans-jim-crow-north)). The same documentary noted that Minnesota law already prohibited job and housing discrimination, and that a 1943 state survey of employers found many justifying their refusal to hire Black workers.

**Professionals of color and newcomers.** MPR's 2012 series reported that professionals of color who relocated to the Twin Cities often described an environment in which locals "accept them, but lack a genuine understanding of other cultures." General Mills executive Ken Charles said the job gets people "the 9 to 5," and that "the harder part is the 5 to 9" ([MPR News, March 12, 2012](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2012/03/12/outsiders1-is-minnesota-nice-to-newcomers)). The same story also carried a refugee's very different view: that Minnesotans welcomed him, gave him a job when he did not yet speak English well, and "I think I can say that's nice." Both experiences are real.

**The Minnesota paradox.** Samuel Myers Jr., director of the Roy Wilkins Center at the University of Minnesota's Humphrey School, calls it "the Minnesota Paradox": the state is one of the best places to live in America, yet measured by racial gaps in unemployment, income, incarceration, arrests, homeownership, lending, test scores, child maltreatment reports, school discipline and even drowning, African Americans are worse off in Minnesota than in "virtually every other state" ([Humphrey School, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.hhh.umn.edu/research-centers/roy-wilkins-center-human-relations-and-social-justice/minnesota-paradox)). Myers told WCCO in 2020 that "the racism you see in Minnesota" is the kind "where people say there is no racism," and that saying "the only race is the human race" cannot explain who is unemployed, arrested or dying of COVID-19 ([CBS Minnesota, June 23, 2020](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/the-minnesota-paradox-why-the-land-of-10000-lakes-has-one-of-the-largest-racial-disparities/)). The WCCO report added that many indicators for Black Minnesotans are not worse than elsewhere; the gaps are wide partly because white Minnesotans do better than average. The Minnesota Spokesman-Recorder launched an essay series on the paradox in 2024 ([Minnesota Spokesman-Recorder, Nov. 14, 2024](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2024/11/14/minnesota-paradox-black-disparities/)).

**"Outside influence."** Myers makes a cultural point that is worth sitting with. He writes that in the 1990s, "in a characteristically Minnesotan manner," leaders blamed rising problems in the Black community on migrants from Chicago, Detroit and Gary, so that the problem was "not Minnesota" ([Humphrey School](https://www.hhh.umn.edu/research-centers/roy-wilkins-center-human-relations-and-social-justice/minnesota-paradox)). He sees the same move in claims, after George Floyd's murder in 2020, that outside agitators drove the unrest. Whatever one concludes about those events, the pattern he describes, locating a problem outside the group so the group's self-image stays intact, is a known feature of many dominant cultures, and a useful thing to watch for.

**Colorblindness.** Lavin-Loucks, growing up in Stillwater in the 1970s, wrote that her family emphasized colorblindness, the sense that "people are people," and that by not talking about race she did not really see it. She now sees colorblindness as discounting the experiences of people of color ([The Society Pages, July 3, 2020](https://thesocietypages.org/specials/rethinking-minnesota-nice/)). Waldner, the St. Thomas sociologist, put it bluntly in 2019: a white Minnesotan can say, in effect, "I'm polite to everyone, I'm Minnesota Nice so we don't have issues around race." She said she does not think the intent is malicious, but called it a form of denial that can sustain institutional racism in housing and education ([MPR News, Nov. 21, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/11/21/ask-a-sotan-revisiting-minnesota-nice)).

**Immigrant and refugee belonging.** In 2021, a Twin Cities planning effort on immigrant and refugee belonging, led by the two cities and their chambers of commerce, reported that community participants expressed frustration at "over-engagement": being asked for input again and again without seeing it reflected in a final product, and skepticism that the effort would bring real results ([The Journey to Belonging in the Twin Cities, City of Saint Paul, 2022](https://www.stpaul.gov/sites/default/files/2022-04/The%20Journey%20to%20Belonging%20in%20the%20Twin%20Cities%20Report.pdf)). That finding is directly relevant to public agencies that rely on community engagement, and section 10 returns to it.

**A new book.** In 2026 the journalist Justin Ellis published *The Cruelty of Nice Folks*, which Kirkus describes as a documented account of Minneapolis's history of racism behind its outward politeness, including subtler present-day forms such as opposition to denser, more affordable housing ([Kirkus Reviews, 2026](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/justin-ellis/the-cruelty-of-nice-folks/)).

These voices do not say that Minnesotans are unkind. Most of them say the opposite. What they describe is a culture in which kindness and avoidance can look the same from inside, and very different from outside.

## 6. Race and racism in Minnesota's history and present

Racism is not a separate topic from Minnesota culture. It is part of how the state's places, institutions and habits were made, and part of how the majority culture talks, or does not talk, about itself. This section sets out the documented history and present, and then returns to the question of Minnesota nice.

**Housing: covenants and segregation.** MNopedia records that Minneapolis real estate developers began writing racial covenants into property deeds in 1910, clauses barring sale or rental to people of Black, Asian and other descent. The company that introduced them inserted the same language into thousands of deeds across both cities, and by the 1930s most new residential developments in the Twin Cities carried such restrictions. The legislature banned them in 1953 ([MNopedia, Racial Housing Covenants in the Twin Cities, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/racial-housing-covenants-twin-cities)). MNopedia notes that covenants were "out of view" in county deed books, unlike the visible signs of Southern segregation, and that they reworked the relationship between race and land in places "imagined to be free from Jim Crow." The University of Minnesota's Mapping Prejudice project, which maps these deeds with the help of community volunteers, describes covenants as clauses meant "to prevent people who are not White from buying or occupying land" ([Mapping Prejudice, University of Minnesota](https://mappingprejudice.umn.edu/)). A 2019 Minnesota law lets homeowners record a formal "discharge" objecting to a covenant on their property; the project explains that this does not erase the record, which it calls "the receipts of structural racism" ([Mapping Prejudice, discharging racial covenants](https://mappingprejudice.umn.edu/discharging-racial-covenants)). Myers traces today's homeownership gaps in part to these policies, which he says became "baked in" to Minnesota life ([Humphrey School](https://www.hhh.umn.edu/research-centers/roy-wilkins-center-human-relations-and-social-justice/minnesota-paradox)).

> **Content note:** The next two paragraphs describe the 1920 lynchings in Duluth. Readers may pause or skip to "Rondo and Interstate 94."

**Duluth, 1920.** On June 15, 1920, three Black circus workers, Elias Clayton, Elmer Jackson and Isaac McGhie, were lynched by a mob in Duluth after a white couple accused six Black circus workers of robbery and rape. A doctor who examined the young woman that morning found no evidence of rape. MNopedia estimates the crowd at 10,000 people. A grand jury indicted 37 white men, eight were tried, and three were convicted of rioting; no one was convicted of murder ([MNopedia, Duluth Lynchings](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/duluth-lynchings)).

MNopedia notes that lynching "is widely believed to be something that happened only in the South." Minnesota passed an anti-lynching law on April 21, 1921, after lobbying by a St. Paul activist. The lynchings were then "mostly forgotten or ignored" until 1991, when the men's graves received headstones; a memorial followed, Duluth holds an annual day of remembrance, and Max Mason, a Black man convicted in a related case, was pardoned by the state in 2020 ([MNopedia, Duluth Lynchings](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/duluth-lynchings)). The long forgetting is part of the cultural story.

**Rondo and Interstate 94.** St. Paul's Rondo neighborhood was the center of Black life in the city. By the 1930s half of St. Paul's Black population lived there, its families were middle and working class, and it had its own clubs, a credit union and a cooperative ([MNopedia, Rondo Neighborhood](https://www3.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/rondo-neighborhood-st-paul)). Building I-94 between 1956 and 1968 cut it in half. MNopedia's comparison of four neighborhoods in the freeway's path is telling. In Rondo, 600 families lost their homes; neighborhood leaders won a design change but could not get a fair housing ordinance, and MNopedia concludes that a "lack of political clout, along with racial and class bias, left Rondo vulnerable." In mostly white, professional Merriam Park, by contrast, residents with connections to the archdiocese and elected officials used "insider and outsider tactics" and won several changes ([MNopedia, Neighborhood Resistance to I-94](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/neighborhood-resistance-i-94-1953-1965)). For anyone who works in public process, the lesson is pointed. A process can be open to everyone and still respond most to people who already know how it works. Rondo Days, first held in 1983, still celebrates the community ([MNopedia, Rondo](https://www3.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/rondo-neighborhood-st-paul)).

**Schools and opportunity.** Minnesota's high school graduation rate reached a record 85 percent for the class of 2025, with gains among Black, Asian and Native American students and students in special education, according to MPR's report of state data ([MPR News, April 3, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2026/04/03/minnesota-graduation-rates-hit-record-high-in-2025)). Hamline University political scientist David Schultz wrote in 2025 that, even so, graduation, college-going and test scores for students of color "are among the nation's worst" ([Duluth News Tribune, June 26, 2025](https://www.duluthnewstribune.com/opinion/columns/state-view-minnesota-exceptionalism-it-turns-out-was-a-myth)). Myers told WCCO that Minnesota's land was largely sold cheaply to white settlers, mostly Germans and Scandinavians, producing wealth handed down across generations ([CBS Minnesota, 2020](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/the-minnesota-paradox-why-the-land-of-10000-lakes-has-one-of-the-largest-racial-disparities/)). The same report noted disagreement among scholars about causes: Myron Orfield of the University of Minnesota Law School has argued that ending desegregation efforts made gaps worse, while Myers does not see housing segregation as the main cause.

> **Content note:** The next three paragraphs refer to the police killing of George Floyd in 2020 and to findings about police use of force. Readers may pause or skip to "How Minnesota nice shapes talk about race."

**George Floyd, 2020.** On May 25, 2020, George Floyd, a 46-year-old Black man, was killed by a Minneapolis police officer during an arrest ([Humphrey School](https://www.hhh.umn.edu/research-centers/roy-wilkins-center-human-relations-and-social-justice/minnesota-paradox)). Protests followed in Minneapolis and St. Paul and across the world. Myers opened his account of the Minnesota paradox by asking how this could happen in a state "known for being 'nice.'"

**The state's findings on the Minneapolis Police Department.** On April 27, 2022, the Minnesota Department of Human Rights released the findings of an investigation it opened after Floyd's murder. It found probable cause that the City of Minneapolis and its police department engaged in a pattern or practice of race discrimination, including higher rates of more severe force against Black people than white people in similar circumstances, more stops, searches, citations and arrests of Black people during traffic stops, and covert social media accounts used to surveil Black individuals and organizations without a public safety purpose ([Minnesota Department of Human Rights, Investigation into the City of Minneapolis and the Minneapolis Police Department, April 27, 2022](https://mn.gov/mdhr/assets/Investigation%20into%20the%20City%20of%20Minneapolis%20and%20the%20Minneapolis%20Police%20Department_tcm1061-526417.pdf)).

What makes the report relevant to an essay on culture is its main conclusion. The department wrote that the pattern was caused "primarily by an organizational culture," and that without fundamental culture change, reforming policies, procedures and trainings "will be meaningless." It added that organizational culture is created by the tone leaders set, how norms are taught and reinforced, and the consequences for breaking them, and that leaders had known of racial disparities but had not acted with "urgency, coordination, and intentionality" ([MDHR, 2022](https://mn.gov/mdhr/assets/Investigation%20into%20the%20City%20of%20Minneapolis%20and%20the%20Minneapolis%20Police%20Department_tcm1061-526417.pdf)). The report concerns one police department and nothing here suggests it describes DHS. But its idea, that culture can outlast policy, applies to any public institution.

**How Minnesota nice shapes talk about race.** Several of the voices in section 5 make the same point from different directions: the habits that keep everyday life smooth can make race hard to discuss. Myers says Minnesota's racism is the kind where people say there is none ([CBS Minnesota, 2020](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/the-minnesota-paradox-why-the-land-of-10000-lakes-has-one-of-the-largest-racial-disparities/)). Waldner describes politeness used as proof that race is not an issue ([MPR News, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/11/21/ask-a-sotan-revisiting-minnesota-nice)). Lavin-Loucks writes that "when it comes to racial injustice," avoidance means never calling out harmful words, actions and policies ([The Society Pages, 2020](https://thesocietypages.org/specials/rethinking-minnesota-nice/)). McWatt describes Black Minnesotans learning to read polite faces ([MPR, 2002](https://archive.mpr.org/stories/2002/02/12/african-americans-jim-crow-north)). In the terms of section 4, this is a low-conflict, indirect communication style meeting a topic that requires directness. None of these sources argues that Minnesotans should become less kind. Waldner and the MPR listener she spoke with said the state need not give up the phrase, but should use it as a reason for more honest conversations ([MPR News, 2019](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2019/11/21/ask-a-sotan-revisiting-minnesota-nice)).

## 7. Political culture

**A framework: Elazar's "moralistic" Minnesota.** The political scientist Daniel Elazar described three American political subcultures. In the "individualistic" culture, politics is a marketplace and a career. In the "traditionalistic" culture, an established elite governs. In the "moralistic" culture, politics is a shared search for the good society, government is "a positive instrument" for the general welfare, public service carries moral obligations, and corruption is not tolerated. Elazar called Minnesota "the archetypical example" of the moralistic culture, "more so than any other in the Union," and traced it to Yankee settlers reinforced by Scandinavian and, to a lesser degree, German immigrants ([Daniel J. Elazar, "Minnesota: The Epitome of the Moralistic Political Culture," Jerusalem Center for Public Affairs, Daniel Elazar online library, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://dje.jcpa.org/articles2/minn3.htm)). He wrote that Minnesotans debate "how large a role government should have," not whether it should have one, and that politics here has been "open to and dominated by amateurs," people driven by issues rather than careers.

This is an influential framework, but it is a classification by one scholar, built from settlement history rather than surveys of Minnesotans, and it describes a state's public life, not its residents' views. Elazar also noted a tension inside it that matters for this essay: the moralistic culture's "major difficulty with bureaucracy" is the conflict between communitarian ideals and large organizations, and where merit systems exist, "they tend to be rigidly maintained." A culture that sees government as a moral project can be generous and principled. It can also be slow to see when its own good intentions are not reaching everyone.

**Popular theory: "Yankeedom."** The journalist Colin Woodard's book *American Nations* divides North America into eleven regional cultures. In popular summaries of his map, Minnesota falls in "Yankeedom," a region he describes as valuing education, communal empowerment and citizen participation, with a "Utopian streak" ([6sqft, summarizing Woodard's map](https://www.6sqft.com/nyc-aka-new-netherland-mapping-the-11-different-cultural-nations-within-the-u-s/)). This is popular history, not peer-reviewed research. It points the same direction as Elazar, and shares the limit of explaining a whole region by its earliest settlers.

**The Farmer-Labor and Humphrey traditions.** The Farmer-Labor Party grew from farmers' and workers' movements, won the governorship with Floyd B. Olson in 1930, and in 1936 held the governorship, six of nine congressional seats and the state House ([MNopedia, Minnesota Farmer-Labor Party](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/minnesota-farmer-labor-party-1924-1944)). Its 1944 merger with the Democrats formed the DFL ([Elazar](https://dje.jcpa.org/articles2/minn3.htm)). Hubert Humphrey, as mayor of Minneapolis, set up the city's first fair-employment commission. In 1948 he told the Democratic National Convention that it was time for the party to "walk forthrightly in the bright sunshine of human rights," a speech MNopedia says won liberal acclaim and a walkout by segregationist delegates ([MNopedia, Hubert H. Humphrey](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/person/humphrey-hubert-h-1911-1978)).

**Bipartisan problem-solving.** Minnesota's civil rights law of 1967, which created the state Department of Human Rights, was sponsored by Conservatives in the legislature and passed with support from both parties. Sex was added in 1969, and disability and public assistance status in 1973. Sexual orientation was added in 1993 after six failed attempts ([MNopedia, Minnesota Human Rights Act](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/minnesota-human-rights-act)). The 1971 "Minnesota Miracle," which shifted most school costs from local property taxes to the state, took the longest special session in state history and was finally worked out in private meetings among key members of both caucuses at the governor's residence. Before it, MNopedia notes, the richest districts had both the lowest tax rates and the highest spending per pupil ([MNopedia, Minnesota Miracle](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/minnesota-miracle-legislation)). From 1913 to 1973, MNopedia adds, Minnesota legislators did not identify with a party when they caucused or campaigned. Process, consensus and the long negotiation are part of this inheritance.

**Turnout.** As noted in section 4, Minnesota's 2024 turnout of 76.35 percent of eligible voters was second in the nation, and its youth turnout was first ([KSTP](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/local-news/minnesotans-ranked-2nd-in-voter-turnout-1st-among-young-voters/)).

**Divides.** Schultz, the Hamline political scientist, argued in June 2025 that "the myth of Minnesota exceptionalism" no longer holds. He described Minnesota as a swing state with sharp regional divides, with only about a dozen of 87 counties consistently voting Democratic, a state Senate split 34 to 33 and a House that had been tied 67 to 67. He suggested that "the same moralistic spirit" that once defined the state's politics now "fuels passionate polarization" ([Duluth News Tribune, June 26, 2025](https://www.duluthnewstribune.com/opinion/columns/state-view-minnesota-exceptionalism-it-turns-out-was-a-myth)). That is one scholar's argument in an opinion column, but it is a useful corrective to any picture of Minnesota as a place of easy consensus. The rural profile in this series describes the urban and rural divide in more detail.

**Tests of civic culture, 2025 and 2026.** Several events in the past two years have tested the state's civic habits. They are reported here briefly, with their sources, and without judgment of any party.

> **Content note:** The next paragraph describes the 2025 shootings of state legislators, and the one after it refers to deaths during a federal enforcement operation. Readers may pause or skip to "Fraud investigations."

- *The lawmaker shootings.* On June 14, 2025, Rep. Melissa Hortman and her husband, Mark, were fatally shot in their Brooklyn Park home, and Sen. John Hoffman and his wife, Yvette, were wounded at their Champlin home, in what MPR News describes as politically motivated attacks allegedly carried out by Vance Boelter. A federal judge sentenced Boelter to two life sentences plus 40 years after he pleaded guilty in federal court; he has pleaded not guilty to state charges ([MPR News, Minnesota lawmaker shootings coverage, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.mprnews.org/crime-law-and-justice/minnesota-lawmaker-shootings-hortman-hoffman)). Schultz noted they were the first political assassinations in the state's history ([Duluth News Tribune, 2025](https://www.duluthnewstribune.com/opinion/columns/state-view-minnesota-exceptionalism-it-turns-out-was-a-myth)).
- *Operation Metro Surge.* A federal immigration enforcement operation in the Twin Cities grew, according to federal officials in a court document, to roughly 3,000 federal officers at its height. Federal officers fatally shot two people in Minneapolis, Renee Good on January 7 and Alex Pretti on January 24, 2026, and their deaths set off protests. Border official Tom Homan declared the operation over after about 10 weeks, citing more than 4,000 arrests; Gov. Walz and Minneapolis Mayor Jacob Frey said they would ask the federal government to help pay for its financial impact ([FOX 9, Feb. 2026](https://www.fox9.com/news/operation-metro-surge-how-many-federal-agents-have-left-minnesota)). The Minnesota Department of Education later reported that three out of four schools saw higher absences during the operation, about 440,000 fewer school meals were served, and 80 percent of public school leaders said it hurt students' learning ([Minnesota Department of Education, Sept. 2026](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/MNMDE/bulletins/42d0cae)).
- *Fraud investigations.* Investigations of fraud in publicly funded programs, beginning with 2022 federal indictments in the Feeding Our Future case, became a central political issue. In spring 2025, under the House's power-sharing agreement, Republicans established a House Fraud Prevention and Oversight Committee. On January 5, 2026, Gov. Tim Walz announced he would not seek a third term, citing attention to fraud allegations, and criticized "Republican opportunists" and President Trump for "demonizing our Somali neighbors" ([Northern News Now, Jan. 5, 2026](https://www.northernnewsnow.com/2026/01/05/walz-ends-re-election-bid-amid-fraud-allegations-timeline/)). Some of these investigations concern programs run by the Department of Human Services, and they are discussed in section 10.

Each of these events raises a question for the majority culture's self-image. Can a state that prides itself on civility absorb political violence? How does a culture that values welcome respond when federal enforcement and political rhetoric single out particular communities? How does a culture that trusts its institutions respond when some of those institutions are found to have failed at oversight? These are open questions, and Minnesotans disagree about the answers.

## 8. Anti-racism and equity work, and the debate about it

Minnesota's response to racial disparities includes state policy, community-led work and a lively public argument. This section describes each briefly and fairly.

**State government.** The governor's office has built an equity structure through executive orders rather than a single statute. Executive Order 23-12, signed September 29, 2023, continued the One Minnesota Council on Diversity, Inclusion, and Equity and the Governor's Community Council on Inclusion and Equity, noted the appointment of the state's first Chief Equity Officer on March 29, 2023, and directed agencies to carry out a long-range equity plan ([Executive Order 23-12, via Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/archive/execorders/23-12.pdf)). An earlier order said an individual's opportunities "should not depend on their race, identity, ability," place of origin, place of worship or zip code ([Executive Order 21-13](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/archive/execorders/21-13.pdf)). The state's Office of Inclusion, in Minnesota Management and Budget, marked ten years of the Chief Inclusion Officer role in April 2026 ([Office of Inclusion, 2025 Annual Report](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/other/261275.pdf)). Its 2024 report lists a listening session it held for DHS and DHS's own Equity Week among its activities ([Office of Inclusion, 2024 Annual Report](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2026/other/261274.pdf)). The Department of Human Rights, created by the 1967 law, is the state's civil rights enforcement agency, and its 2022 police findings are described in section 6.

**Repair of the record.** Some efforts address history directly. The 2019 law allowing homeowners to discharge racial covenants, the Mapping Prejudice project's volunteer mapping, the 2020 pardon of Max Mason, and the long work of Duluth's memorial committee are examples ([Mapping Prejudice](https://mappingprejudice.umn.edu/discharging-racial-covenants); [MNopedia, Duluth Lynchings](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/duluth-lynchings)). Rondo's residents have kept their neighborhood's memory alive for more than forty years through Rondo Days ([MNopedia, Rondo](https://www3.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/place/rondo-neighborhood-st-paul)).

**Community-led work.** Much of the most important equity work is led by communities themselves, as the community profiles in this series document: culturally specific nonprofits, parent groups, faith communities and newsrooms such as the Minnesota Spokesman-Recorder's 2024 series on the paradox ([Minnesota Spokesman-Recorder, 2024](https://spokesman-recorder.com/2024/11/14/minnesota-paradox-black-disparities/)).

**The debate.** Equity work has critics, and their arguments are part of the state's political culture. At a 2021 stop of the Center of the American Experiment's statewide "Raise Our Standards" tour, policy fellow Catrin Wigfall argued that "equity" is easily confused with "equality": in her view, equality means equal opportunity, while equity means guaranteeing equal outcomes, which requires favoring one group over another and is, "at its core, discrimination." She also argued that the focus distracts from core academics, noting that Black and Hispanic students in Mississippi outperform those in Minnesota in math and reading. At the same event, Kendall Qualls of TakeCharge Minnesota spoke about family structure, and state Sen. Carla Nelson stressed local school board control ([Post Bulletin, June 8, 2021](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/local/center-for-the-american-experiment-presenters-criticize-use-of-critical-race-theory-in-minnesota-schools)). By 2025, federal executive orders had ended diversity, equity and inclusion programs across the federal government. In that setting, Minnesota State Chancellor Scott Olson defended his system's equity work, saying "Minnesota needs everybody in the workforce" and describing equity in terms of first-generation and low-income students as well as students of color ([Post Bulletin, Jan. 25, 2025](https://www.postbulletin.com/news/local/dei-is-being-banished-in-government-and-business-but-not-in-higher-education-in-minnesota)).

For a public servant, the point is not to pick a side in this essay. It is to notice that people of good faith disagree about the words "equity" and "equality," and that the majority culture's strong attachment to "treat everyone the same," discussed next, sits at the center of that disagreement.

## 9. Values under the surface

Values are the beliefs about what is good that sit underneath behavior. Researchers have tried to measure them by country and, more rarely, by state. Those measures are useful as background, with care.

**Individualism and interdependence.** Geert Hofstede's model of national culture rates countries on six dimensions. On his website, he defines individualism as the extent to which people feel independent rather than interdependent members of larger wholes, and adds that individualism "does not mean egoism" and collectivism "does not mean closeness" ([Geert Hofstede, the 6-D model of national culture, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://geerthofstede.com/culture-geert-hofstede-gert-jan-hofstede/6d-model-of-national-culture/)). In the data set published with his books, the United States scores 91 on individualism, among the highest of any country, with power distance 40, masculinity 62, uncertainty avoidance 46 and indulgence 68 ([Hofstede dimension data, 2015 version, geerthofstede.com](https://geerthofstede.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/6-dimensions-for-website-2015-08-16.csv); [dimension data matrix page](https://geerthofstede.com/research-and-vsm/dimension-data-matrix/)). The Culture Factor, the consultancy that now publishes Hofstede-based country profiles, lists the United States at 60 on individualism and 50 on long-term orientation (the data set has 26), with its other scores the same, and describes a loosely knit society in which people are expected to look after themselves ([The Culture Factor, country comparison tool, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.theculturefactor.com/country-comparison-tool?countries=united+states)). The two sources disagree, and both are national averages.

The same data set includes the European countries that shaped Minnesota's majority culture. Sweden scores 5 and Norway 8 on Hofstede's masculinity dimension, among the lowest in the world, compared with 62 for the United States. Denmark scores 18 on power distance, compared with 40 for the United States ([Hofstede dimension data](https://geerthofstede.com/wp-content/uploads/2016/08/6-dimensions-for-website-2015-08-16.csv)). Hofstede describes low-masculinity societies as ones where competing is less openly endorsed and there is "sympathy for the underdog" ([Hofstede, 6-D model](https://geerthofstede.com/culture-geert-hofstede-gert-jan-hofstede/6d-model-of-national-culture/)). It is tempting to see Jante law and Minnesota modesty in those numbers. That would be a stretch. These scores describe Scandinavia today, not the descendants of nineteenth-century emigrants, and no study links them.

**State-level individualism.** The most relevant research is at the state level. In 1999, Joseph Vandello and Dov Cohen built an eight-item index ranking the 50 states on collectivism and individualism. They found collectivism strongest in the Deep South and individualism strongest in the Mountain West and Great Plains ([Vandello and Cohen, Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 1999, abstract via University of South Florida](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/2290)). The index used things like the share of people and of older people living alone, households with grandchildren, the ratio of divorces to marriages, people with no religious affiliation, Libertarian voting, carpooling and self-employment; scores ranged from 31 for Montana, the most individualist, to 91 for Hawaii ([Cambridge University Press supplementary material describing the index](https://static.cambridge.org/content/id/urn:cambridge.org:id:article:S0140525X11000021/resource/name/S0140525X11000021sup001.pdf)). Minnesota's own score could not be found on any page that could be opened, so it is not reported here. The items themselves are worth noticing, though. They measure household arrangements such as older people living alone and grandparents in the home. Those are exactly the patterns that differ between Minnesota's majority culture and many of its newer communities, and that shape how families use disability and aging services.

**Tight and loose.** Jesse Harrington and Michele Gelfand's 2014 study ranked the 50 states on "tightness," meaning many strongly enforced rules and little tolerance for deviance, versus "looseness." Minnesota ranked 28th, with a score of 47.84, near the middle; Mississippi was the tightest and California the loosest ([Harrington and Gelfand, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2014, via PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4050535/)). The index was built from items such as corporal punishment in schools, executions, marijuana penalties, dry counties, same-sex civil union law, religiosity and the share of residents born abroad. The authors found tightness only moderately related to collectivism, which they treat as a separate dimension. In other words, by this measure, Minnesota is neither especially rule-bound nor especially permissive. Its social norms may be enforced more by quiet disapproval than by law, which an index of laws and punishments would not capture. That is a question, not a finding.

**Egalitarianism and "treat everyone the same."** Meyer places Scandinavian countries among the most egalitarian on her leading scale, quoting her view that downplaying individual achievement has been part of Scandinavian society "for centuries" ([Welcome to the Jungle, summarizing Meyer](https://www.welcometothejungle.com/en-GB/articles/keys-managing-multicultural-teams)). Minnesota's civic tradition also prizes fairness. The difficulty is that fairness can be defined two ways: as identical treatment, or as treatment that gets people to the same place. Bennett's model, discussed in section 14, describes how an emphasis on sameness can hide real differences, and the debate in section 8 turns on the same question.

**Modesty and privacy.** Jones's essay on Jante law and Mohr's humor both describe a culture that discourages standing out ([MPR News, 2009](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2009/12/14/syljones); [Duluth News Tribune via Echo Press, 2022](https://www.echopress.com/lifestyle/arts-and-entertainment/front-row-seat-remembering-howard-mohr-and-how-to-talk-minnesotan)). Mohr's section on money, in which a neighbor refuses payment for hours of work out in the cold, describes a reluctance to talk openly about money and need. A culture that keeps its troubles private and dislikes asking for help may also find it awkward to ask others about theirs.

**Work ethic and self-reliance.** The farm history described in section 3, and Rentfrow's finding of relatively high conscientiousness across the region, point to a culture that values steady work and handling one's own problems ([Rentfrow et al., 2013](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/psp-a0034434.pdf)). At the same time, cooperatives, church aid societies and high volunteering show a strong habit of helping. The two fit together in a particular way: help is often organized through institutions, and given more easily than it is asked for.

**Trust in institutions, process and consensus.** Elazar's account describes Minnesotans as seeing government as a positive force and expecting clean, principled public service ([Elazar](https://dje.jcpa.org/articles2/minn3.htm)). The Minnesota Miracle's long negotiation and the bipartisan human rights law show a culture that trusts process to produce fair results ([MNopedia, Minnesota Miracle](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/minnesota-miracle-legislation)). Meyer, on her deciding scale, notes that consensus decisions "take much longer to reach" but are harder to change, and that people from top-down cultures must learn patience with them ([Welcome to the Jungle, summarizing Meyer](https://www.welcometothejungle.com/en-GB/articles/keys-managing-multicultural-teams)). Trust in institutions is a strength. It can also be a blind spot, for people whose own or family experience of institutions, including the ones described in sections 6 and 11, gives them reason to trust less.

**What these measures cannot tell us.** Every measure in this section is an average across millions of people, and most are national. None describes a particular staff member, family or client. Researchers warn against moving from a group average to an individual, a mistake sometimes called a stereotype. The value of these frameworks is in the questions they raise, not in the labels they offer.

## 10. Culture inside institutions

Culture does not stop at the office door. The habits described above, indirect speech, conflict avoidance, faith in process, "treat everyone the same," and comfort with long-standing relationships, can shape how a public agency runs its meetings, gives feedback, makes decisions and listens to communities. This section sets out what public evidence exists about the state workforce and DHS, and says plainly what is not documented.

### Who works in state government

Minnesota Management and Budget's workforce report for fiscal year 2023 (July 2022 to June 2023) counts about 37,100 executive branch positions across 87 agencies, boards and commissions. DHS is the largest agency, with about one in five executive branch employees ([MMB, State of Minnesota Workforce Planning Report FY 2023, via Legislative Reference Library](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2024/other/240792.pdf)).

- **Race and ethnicity.** About 15.9 percent of executive branch employees identified as Black, Indigenous or People of Color (BIPOC), 78.5 percent as white, and about 5 percent did not report race. The report compares this with 20.4 percent BIPOC in the Minnesota labor force. It finds the state close to the labor force for Indigenous, Black and Asian workers, and says Hispanic and Latino workers are the most underrepresented, at 2.9 percent of state workers against 5.4 percent of the labor force. BIPOC employees were 21 percent of new hires in FY 2023, up from 11 percent in FY 2011 ([MMB FY 2023](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2024/other/240792.pdf)).
- **DHS.** The report's agency table lists 7,360 DHS employees, of whom 20.0 percent were people of color and 78.1 percent white, with 12.1 percent reporting a disability ([MMB FY 2023, appendix table](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2024/other/240792.pdf)). DHS is therefore somewhat more racially diverse than the executive branch as a whole, and close to the state's labor force, though the report does not compare DHS's staff with the people DHS serves.
- **Disability.** Employees with disabilities were 11.6 percent of the executive branch, about twice their share of the state labor force (5.9 percent) in the report's comparison. Turnover among employees with disabilities, and among BIPOC employees, ran higher than average ([MMB FY 2023](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2024/other/240792.pdf)).
- **Age.** The median age of state workers was 46, and about 40 percent were 50 or older. The median age of new hires was 36 ([MMB FY 2023](https://www.lrl.mn.gov/docs/2024/other/240792.pdf)). Set beside Compass's finding that 42 percent of Minnesota's youngest children are children of color, the age gap between state staff and the state's future population is clear.

What this does not show matters as much. No published source found says where state or DHS employees grew up, how many were born in Minnesota, what languages they speak, or what religious or cultural backgrounds they come from. Race is not the same as culture: a white employee may have grown up in a recent immigrant family, and an employee of color may have grown up entirely inside Minnesota's majority culture. The numbers above describe who is in the room, not how anyone in it thinks.

### How state employees describe their workplace

Minnesota Management and Budget's 2025 Employee Engagement and Inclusion Survey was the fourth round of the survey, open to most executive branch employees, and 48 percent of those invited took part ([MMB, Statewide Employee Engagement and Inclusion Survey, High-Level Employee Statement Results, August 2025](https://mn.gov/mmb-stat/communications/toolkit/2025/employeesurvey/Employee%20Engagement%20and%20Inclusion%20Survey%20Results.pdf)). A few statewide results speak directly to the themes of this essay:

- 58 percent agreed, and another 24 percent somewhat agreed, that they feel comfortable sharing their ideas and perspectives at work "even when they differ from others'."
- 62 percent agreed, and 16 percent somewhat agreed, that they did not need to "conceal or distort valued parts" of their identity to fit in at work.
- 64 percent agreed, and 24 percent somewhat agreed, that they felt equipped to look at situations from other cultural perspectives in their jobs.
- 54 percent agreed, and 21 percent somewhat agreed, that their agency makes decisions in line with improving equity.

Read alongside section 4, the first result invites a question rather than a conclusion. In a culture that tends to avoid open disagreement, roughly four in ten state employees did not fully agree that they feel comfortable voicing a differing view. The published summary does not break results down by agency, race, disability or other group, so it cannot say whose comfort is lower, or whether DHS differs from other agencies. Those breakdowns, if they exist, were not found in public form.

### Meetings, feedback, silence and consensus

No published study was found that observed how Minnesota's cultural habits play out in state agency meetings. What follows connects the documented patterns above to ordinary workplace moments, as questions to test against one's own experience.

- **Silence read as agreement.** Lavin-Loucks's observation that a smile and a nod can look like agreement ([The Society Pages, 2020](https://thesocietypages.org/specials/rethinking-minnesota-nice/)) applies to meetings. When no one objects, has the group agreed, or have some people decided objecting is not worth the cost? The IDI's developers note that members of non-dominant groups sometimes "go along to get along," minimizing parts of their own identity to navigate a dominant culture's rules ([IDI, the Intercultural Development Continuum](https://idiinventory.com/idc)).
- **Feedback that is not heard.** If Minnesota's majority culture softens criticism more than the American average, as local humor and reporting suggest, then a supervisor's gentle suggestion may be missed by a colleague from a more direct culture, while a colleague from a less direct culture may still find it pointed ([Welcome to the Jungle, summarizing Meyer](https://www.welcometothejungle.com/en-GB/articles/keys-managing-multicultural-teams)).
- **Consensus and who is in it.** Minnesota's civic tradition values long, inclusive process ([MNopedia, Minnesota Miracle](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/minnesota-miracle-legislation)). The I-94 history shows that process tends to reward people who already know how to use it ([MNopedia, Neighborhood Resistance to I-94](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/neighborhood-resistance-i-94-1953-1965)). Who is present when consensus forms, and who learns of it afterward?
- **Community engagement fatigue.** The 2021 Twin Cities belonging effort heard frustration at "over-engagement," input given repeatedly without seeing it reflected in the result ([The Journey to Belonging in the Twin Cities](https://www.stpaul.gov/sites/default/files/2022-04/The%20Journey%20to%20Belonging%20in%20the%20Twin%20Cities%20Report.pdf)). A culture that trusts process can mistake holding a listening session for having listened.

### Oversight, trust and the fraud investigations

Recent oversight findings touch DHS directly, and they bear on culture as well as rules. On March 17, 2026, the Office of the Legislative Auditor reported that DHS officials had asserted that, before 2025, the department lacked authority to investigate alleged kickbacks in the Early Intensive Developmental and Behavioral Intervention (EIDBI) program, the autism benefit, unless the allegations also involved other misconduct. The auditor found that DHS "has long had legal authority" to address such allegations, and identified a decades-old error in the department's rules that may limit its ability to suspend payments during kickback investigations ([Office of the Legislative Auditor, special review, March 17, 2026](https://www.auditor.leg.state.mn.us/sreview/2026/dhs-oig-kickbacks.htm)). Separately, DHS ended its Medical Assistance Housing Stabilization Services program on October 31, 2025, citing widespread fraud. A program launched in 2020 to help older adults and people with disabilities find and keep housing was closed even though, in the department's words, it "truly filled an important gap" for many participants ([DHS bulletin, Oct. 31, 2025](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/MNDHS/bulletins/3f98793)).

These findings raise questions any reflective public servant might ask without assuming the answers. How does a culture of trust in providers and partners, which serves many people well, notice when that trust is being abused? How does an agency tighten oversight without placing suspicion on whole communities, given that the political debate described in section 7 has at times focused on Somali Minnesotans? The Somali community profile in this series reports these debates in more detail. This essay does not attempt to judge them.

## 11. Disability history as part of Minnesota culture

Disability is not only a service area. It is part of Minnesota's cultural history, and some of that history shapes how disabled people and families, including families from immigrant communities, view public agencies today.

> **Content note:** This section describes abuse, neglect, forced sterilization and deaths in state institutions. Readers may pause or skip to section 12.

**The institutions.** Minnesota built a system of state institutions for people with disabilities. The Minnesota Historical Society's library guide lists Faribault State School and Hospital, which began in 1879 as an experimental department of the state's institute for deaf and blind students; the Colony for Epileptics, which opened at Cambridge in 1925 and closed as Cambridge State Hospital in 1997; and Gillette State Hospital for Crippled Children, established in 1897, which the guide calls the first hospital in the United States for children with physical disabilities ([Minnesota Historical Society Library, Institutions for People with Disabilities, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://libguides.mnhs.org/institutions/disabilities)). MNopedia describes how, nationally and in Minnesota, such institutions shifted from education to lifelong custodial care, and by the 1960s were overcrowded and understaffed, with families worried about chemical and physical restraints ([MNopedia, Welsch v. Likins](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/welsch-v-likins)).

**Eugenics and the reform spirit.** Minnesota's eugenics history shows how a reform-minded, scientific, do-good culture can cause great harm. A 1917 law let county probate judges commit people judged "feeble minded" without their parents' or guardians' consent. Because unusual behavior and lower economic class were treated as signs of feeblemindedness, most of those committed were working-class women, and MNopedia notes that an unmarried mother could be so labeled, as could an immigrant for whom English was a second language who failed an English-language IQ test. Charles Dight, who founded the Minnesota Eugenics Society in 1923, was, in MNopedia's words, "consistently interested in social reform and utopian politics." A 1925 law authorized sterilization, and at least 2,204 people were sterilized between 1925 and 1945, 77 percent of them women; the true number is unknown because no central board kept records ([MNopedia, Eugenics in Minnesota](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/eugenics-minnesota)). Elazar's "moralistic" culture, which sees government as a tool for the good society, is visible here too, turned to a terrible end. The lesson for public servants is not that reform is dangerous. It is that confidence in one's own good intentions is not a safeguard.

**Families who sued, and won.** The parents of Patricia Welsch, a resident of Cambridge, obtained help from the Legal Aid Society of Minneapolis, and with support from the Arc of Minnesota their lawyers filed a class action. On February 15, 1974, U.S. District Judge Earl Larson ruled that residents had a constitutional right to treatment. A 1987 settlement aimed to reduce restraints, add direct care staff and improve discharge to the community, and the case was dismissed in 1989 ([MNopedia, Welsch v. Likins](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/welsch-v-likins)).

**Parents organizing.** The Arc in Minnesota began when parents first gathered in Minneapolis in 1946. Its history credits members with winning special education classes in Minneapolis schools eleven years before national law required them, with pressing for the state's Developmental Disabilities Waiver as an alternative to large congregate settings, and with helping negotiate the plan to close Minnesota's state institutions and replace them with community services ([The Arc Minnesota, History of The Arc in Minnesota](https://arcminnesota.org/about/why-the-arc/history-of-the-arc-in-minnesota/)).

**Self-advocates and remembrance.** Around 13,000 people who died in Minnesota's state hospitals were buried in graves marked only with numbers, or nothing. In 1994 disability self-advocates formed Remembering With Dignity to replace them with named stones. A first effort to win a state apology failed in 1996 after legislators removed references to the cemeteries over fears of liability. The group dedicated its first new markers at Faribault in 1999, and in 2010 helped draft an apology resolution that Gov. Tim Pawlenty signed ([MNopedia, Remembering With Dignity](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/remembering-dignity)).

**From charity to rights.** Minnesota's helping institutions have deep roots in its majority culture. Lutheran Social Service traces its roots to a children's home founded in 1865 by a Swedish Augustana Synod pastor, and Swedish immigrants also founded hospitals and a home for the aged ([MNopedia, Swedish Immigration](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/swedish-immigration-minnesota)). That tradition of care built much of the service system. The disability rights movement asked a different question: not only how to care for disabled people, but who decides. The story above moves from institutions run for people, to parents suing on their behalf, to self-advocates speaking for themselves. A culture that is proud of helping can find that shift uncomfortable, because it means sharing control. It is also the shift on which today's policy rests.

## 12. Culture inside disability services

Today's disability policy in Minnesota is built around values that come partly from the disability rights movement and partly from the wider American and Minnesotan emphasis on the individual. Seeing those values as cultural, rather than simply as correct, is not a criticism of them. It helps explain why some families embrace them and others find them strange.

**Values written into law.** Minnesota law states that it is the policy of the state that adults with disabilities, and children with disabilities with support from their families, may make "informed choices" about services, and that they must be offered an informed decision-making process. That process must provide information "in a manner that is culturally and linguistically appropriate," identify the person's cultural needs and access to culturally responsive providers, and offer information about "the benefits of inclusive and individualized services" ([Minn. Stat. 256B.4905, checked Oct. 31, 2026](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.4905)). The same statute states a policy that working-age people with disabilities can work in competitive, integrated jobs. The licensing law for home and community-based services requires person-centered planning that identifies what is important "to the person" as well as "for the person," and that "respects each person's history, dignity, and cultural background," along with self-determination and "the most integrated setting" ([Minn. Stat. 245D.07](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/245D.07)). Minnesota's Olmstead Plan, required by the Jensen settlement, aims for disabled Minnesotans to live full, integrated lives in communities of their choosing, as MCIL summarizes the state Olmstead office's description ([Metropolitan Center for Independent Living](https://mcil-mn.org/mcil-ada-minnesota-submits-comments-on-education-and-employment-to-minnesota-olmstead-plan/)).

These are strong protections, won by disabled people and families against the history in section 11. They also carry assumptions that match the majority culture: that the individual is the main decision-maker, that independence is a central goal, and that the most integrated setting is usually the best one. The law itself already recognizes the tension, by requiring cultural needs to be identified and choices to be informed in culturally appropriate ways.

**Where families may see it differently.** The community profiles in this series document how some families describe disability, care and decision-making. A few examples, from sources reopened for this essay, show the range. They describe particular studies at particular times and should not be read as describing all members of any community.

- In a 2014 Minnesota Department of Health study of families of children with autism, Hmong parents described stigma tied to a belief that families may be "genetically flawed or cursed" for having a child with a disability, which could make families less likely to seek early services. A Somali parent described a fear that people would say "it's because of the mother," and a Latino parent described the fear that a child would be seen as "crazy" ([Minnesota Department of Health, report to the Legislature, Feb. 15, 2014](https://www.leg.mn.gov/docs/2014/mandated/140404.pdf)).
- In 2015, the Somali American Parent Association, working with the Health Department, held 25 community conversations with a total of 220 Somali participants about mental health. Participants described intense stigma, fear of being labeled "crazy," and a tendency to turn first to the mosque, the Quran or spiritual leaders ([Minnesota Department of Health, Somali Mental Health Project](https://www.health.mn.gov/communities/equity/reports/mentalhealth.pdf)). Sahan Journal reported that a group of Somali parents, professionals and autistic people has proposed the term *maangaar*, "unique mind," to replace words for autism that carried negative meanings ([Sahan Journal, updated April 7, 2026](https://sahanjournal.com/health/autism-somali-new-terminology-community-acceptance-minnesota/)).
- A 2021 study for DHS by researchers at Purdue University and the University of Minnesota compared how racial and ethnic groups in Minnesota's Medicaid population used home and community-based services. Among older adults, white people were least likely to use community-based Elderly Waiver services (17 percent) and most likely to use them in a residential setting such as assisted living (16 percent). Under 65, white people were most likely to be on a disability waiver (46 percent) and least likely to use personal care assistance without a waiver (6 percent), while Asian and Pacific Islander people (38 percent) and Black people (31 percent) used personal care assistance without a waiver most often. The authors state plainly that they "do not attempt to draw conclusions" about causes ([Arling and colleagues, report to DHS, March 26, 2021](https://developtoolmn.org/dhs/assets/racial-and-ethnic-comparison-report-quantatitive_tcm1053-663962.pdf)).
- A 2017 survey for the Governor's Council on Developmental Disabilities repeated a 1962 survey of Minnesotans' attitudes and oversampled five racial and ethnic communities. It mapped attitudes into groups along lines such as capability and integration, with labels including "independence" and "inclusion," and reported, for example, that African American respondents leaned toward close supervision of people with developmental disabilities "for their own protection and wellbeing" ([Governor's Council on Developmental Disabilities, 2017](https://mn.gov/mnddc/extra/customer-research/MN-Racial-Ethnic-Communities-Survey2017.txt)). Its community samples were small, roughly 50 to 93 people each.

None of these findings shows that any community "values independence less." They show differences in where families go first for help, how disability is named, who in the family decides, and which services are used. Vandello and Cohen's state index, described in section 9, counted elders living alone and grandparents in the household as markers of individualism and collectivism ([Cambridge supplementary material](https://static.cambridge.org/content/id/urn:cambridge.org:id:article:S0140525X11000021/resource/name/S0140525X11000021sup001.pdf)). A service system designed around the individual who lives on their own, makes their own choices and works in the community will fit some households more naturally than others.

**Questions this raises for practice.** Several questions follow, offered as questions:

- When a family member answers for an adult with a disability, is that a rights problem, a cultural norm the person has chosen, or both? How would a planner tell the difference, and whose definition of "the person's choice" applies?
- When a family prefers care at home by relatives, does the planning process treat that as a valid choice, or as a step toward "more independent" options?
- When an assessment asks about independence in daily living, does it also capture interdependence, such as who in the household helps and is helped?
- When a family is reluctant to accept a label, is that denial, a reasonable fear of stigma, a different explanation of the condition, or distrust earned by history?
- When the system offers "choice," do families know the options well enough, in their own language, for the choice to be real, as the informed choice statute requires?

## 13. Other threads: welcome, faith, gender and class

**The refugee-welcome tradition, and its tensions.** Minnesota has a long tradition of welcoming refugees. Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, writing in December 2021 as Afghan evacuees arrived, credited Catholic Charities and Lutheran Social Service with paving the way through sponsorships and support, noted about 15,000 Korean adoptees who came to Minnesota after the Korean War, and quoted a Hmong scholar saying of early refugees, "we naturally gravitate toward each other." The same article noted that "there have also always been strains of anti-immigration ideology in Minnesota," including discrimination that drove some early Norwegian immigrants back to Norway ([Mpls.St.Paul Magazine, Dec. 19, 2021](https://mspmag.com/arts-and-culture/looking-back-at-minnesotas-refugee-history/)). The tradition of church-led welcome comes straight from the majority culture's institutions. The tension, as the immigration attorney told MPR in 2012, lies between helping newcomers and making them feel they belong ([MPR News, 2012](https://www.mprnews.org/story/2012/03/12/outsiders1-is-minnesota-nice-to-newcomers)).

**Religion's decline and change.** Lutheran and Catholic churches shaped the majority culture, but their hold is weakening. Pew Research Center's 2023 to 2024 survey finds 22 percent of Minnesota adults in mainline Protestant churches, including 12 percent in the Evangelical Lutheran Church in America, and 18 percent Catholic, while 5 percent are atheist, 9 percent agnostic and 15 percent "nothing in particular," with a margin of error of plus or minus 5.7 points ([Pew Research Center, Religious Landscape Study, Minnesota](https://www.pewresearch.org/religious-landscape-study/state/minnesota/)). A 2018 Star Tribune report, as summarized by Patch, found the ELCA had nearly 200,000 fewer Minnesota members than in 2000 and about 80 Catholic churches had closed ([Patch, July 2018](https://patch.com/minnesota/southwestminneapolis/minnesota-churches-are-steep-decline-report)). The cultural habits these churches formed, the potluck, the aid society, the quiet duty to help, may outlast the churches themselves. Meanwhile, newer Minnesotans bring Muslim, Buddhist, Hindu and other faiths, and many immigrant Christians worship in congregations of their own.

**Gender and LGBTQ history.** Minnesota's record here is mixed and contested, like its record on race. The 1967 civil rights law did not include sex until 1969, and sexual orientation was added only in 1993 after six failed attempts ([MNopedia, Minnesota Human Rights Act](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/minnesota-human-rights-act)). In 1970 Jack Baker and Michael McConnell became the first same-sex couple in the United States to apply for a marriage license, and were refused ([MNopedia, Baker v. Nelson](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/baker-v-nelson)). In 2012 Minnesota voters rejected a constitutional amendment limiting marriage to a man and a woman, the first state to reject such a ban at the ballot box ([MNopedia, Minnesota Amendment 1](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/minnesota-amendment-1)). Women's history appears through this essay in less visible ways: in farm women's labor, in the women who were most often committed and sterilized under eugenics laws, and in the parents who organized disability advocacy.

**Class.** Minnesota's majority culture often presents itself as middle class and modest. Yet the 1971 Minnesota Miracle was needed because the richest school districts paid the lowest tax rates and spent the most per pupil ([MNopedia, Minnesota Miracle](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/minnesota-miracle-legislation)), and the eugenics era treated poverty itself as a sign of defect ([MNopedia, Eugenics](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/eugenics-minnesota)). Among white non-Hispanic Minnesotans, about 312,000 people lived below the poverty line in 2024 ([ACS 2024, table B17001H](https://api.censusreporter.org/1.0/data/show/latest?table_ids=B19013,B19013H,B19013B,B17001,B17001H,B17001B&geo_ids=04000US27)). A culture of modesty can make class differences, like racial ones, hard to name.

## 14. Frameworks for reflection

This section offers two related tools for self-reflection. They are background, not a diagnosis of Minnesotans. No study measuring the intercultural development of Minnesotans, of Minnesota state employees or of DHS staff was found.

**Bennett's Developmental Model of Intercultural Sensitivity.** Milton Bennett's model describes six stages in how people experience cultural difference. The first three, Denial, Defense and Minimization, are "ethnocentric": one's own culture is experienced as more central to reality. The last three, Acceptance, Adaptation and Integration, are "ethnorelative": all cultures are experienced as alternative ways of organizing reality ([Bennett, 2017, Intercultural Development Research Institute](https://www.idrinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/DMIS-IDRI.pdf)). In brief, in Bennett's words and summary:

- *Denial:* other cultures are perceived only vaguely, if at all. In organizations, there are no structures for recognizing cultural difference.
- *Defense:* difference is seen in "us and them" terms, often with "us" as superior. A reversed form idealizes the other culture while criticizing one's own.
- *Minimization:* differences are downplayed in favor of similarities that are, in fact, based on one's own worldview. Bennett writes that at this stage organizations "tend to exaggerate the benefits of unbiased equal opportunity," masking "the continued operation of dominant culture privilege."
- *Acceptance:* people become curious about cultural difference and see other cultures as equally complex. Bennett warns of a "naïve and paralytic" stance, "it's not bad or good, it's just different," and says the task is to make judgments that are not ethnocentric.
- *Adaptation:* people can shift perspective and behavior to communicate across cultures.
- *Integration:* people move in and out of cultural worldviews as part of their identity.

Bennett offers example questions for reflection. For Minimization, he suggests asking people how much cultural difference matters in their relationships with people of other cultures, compared with human similarity. For an agency at the Integration stage: "How often is intercultural competence used as a criterion for hiring or advancement?" ([Bennett, 2017](https://www.idrinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/DMIS-IDRI.pdf)). He also notes the model's main criticism, that its assumption of linear progress may reflect a "Western" or "male" bias.

The fit with Minnesota's majority culture is worth considering, as a question. A culture that values treating everyone the same, prefers to emphasize what people share, avoids open conflict and is proud of being fair may find Minimization comfortable and natural. Myers's report of Minnesotans saying "the only race is the human race" ([CBS Minnesota, 2020](https://www.cbsnews.com/minnesota/news/the-minnesota-paradox-why-the-land-of-10000-lakes-has-one-of-the-largest-racial-disparities/)) and Lavin-Loucks's account of colorblindness ([The Society Pages, 2020](https://thesocietypages.org/specials/rethinking-minnesota-nice/)) sound like what Bennett describes. But that is an observation about a cultural style, not a measurement, and many individual Minnesotans will recognize themselves in other stages entirely.

**What the Intercultural Development Inventory has found.** The Intercultural Development Inventory (IDI) is a commercial questionnaire based on Bennett's model. Bennett writes that its research supported the stages from Denial through Adaptation, and that it does not measure Integration ([Bennett, 2017](https://www.idrinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/DMIS-IDRI.pdf)). The IDI's developers describe Minimization as taking two forms: one, more common among dominant-group members, comes from limited cultural self-understanding; the other, more common among non-dominant-group members, is a strategy for navigating a dominant culture, often "go along to get along." They add that where Minimization exists in organizations, diversity often feels "not heard" ([IDI, the Intercultural Development Continuum](https://idiinventory.com/idc)).

Aggregate results are rarely published in full because the instrument is proprietary. One openly published example is a 2014 study at a nursing college in Ohio, where most students, faculty and staff scored in Minimization and 98 percent of students overestimated their own level of intercultural competence. The authors also wrote that IDI results in the general population follow a bell curve, with about 68 percent falling in Minimization, citing an IDI training session in 2011 rather than a published study ([Kruse and colleagues, SpringerPlus, 2014, via PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4112197/)). These findings are background only. They do not describe Minnesotans, and no Minnesota-specific IDI results were found. The most useful lesson in them may be the gap between how people see their own skill and how they score.

**Using the frameworks well.** Each framework in this essay, Hall's context, Hofstede's dimensions, Meyer's scales, Elazar's political cultures, Rentfrow's regions, Vandello and Cohen's index, Harrington and Gelfand's tightness and Bennett's stages, describes averages or ideal types. They are most useful as mirrors: ways of noticing one's own default settings. They are least useful as labels for other people.

## 15. Strengths to build on

Seeing a culture clearly includes seeing what it does well. Minnesota's majority culture brings real strengths to culturally responsive public service.

- **A habit of showing up.** High volunteering, high voter turnout and a dense nonprofit sector reflect a culture that believes ordinary people should take part in public life ([America's Health Rankings](https://www.americashealthrankings.org/explore/measures/volunteerism_d/MN); [KSTP](https://kstp.com/kstp-news/local-news/minnesotans-ranked-2nd-in-voter-turnout-1st-among-young-voters/)).
- **Public service as a moral calling.** Elazar's account of a culture that sees government as a positive instrument and expects clean, principled service is, at its best, exactly the ethic public agencies need ([Elazar](https://dje.jcpa.org/articles2/minn3.htm)).
- **Cooperation and patience.** The cooperative creameries and the long negotiation of the Minnesota Miracle show a culture that can pool resources and keep talking until a fair deal emerges ([MNopedia, Clarks Grove](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/clarks-grove-cooperative-creamery); [MNopedia, Minnesota Miracle](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/minnesota-miracle-legislation)).
- **Kindness and courtesy.** Politeness, helpfulness and reluctance to embarrass others are real assets in hard conversations with families, if they are paired with honesty.
- **Modesty.** A culture that discourages showing off can make room for listening, and for the humility that intercultural work requires.
- **A record of rights.** Minnesota passed bipartisan civil rights protections early, its families and self-advocates helped close the institutions, and its laws now require that informed choice be culturally and linguistically appropriate ([MNopedia, Minnesota Human Rights Act](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/minnesota-human-rights-act); [The Arc Minnesota](https://arcminnesota.org/about/why-the-arc/history-of-the-arc-in-minnesota/); [Minn. Stat. 256B.4905](https://www.revisor.mn.gov/statutes/cite/256B.4905)).
- **A capacity for honest reckoning.** Remembering With Dignity's named gravestones, the state's apology, Mapping Prejudice's volunteers and Duluth's memorial show Minnesotans facing hard history in public ([MNopedia, Remembering With Dignity](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/group/remembering-dignity); [Mapping Prejudice](https://mappingprejudice.umn.edu/discharging-racial-covenants); [MNopedia, Duluth Lynchings](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/event/duluth-lynchings)).
- **A culture that has always been changing.** The majority culture itself was built by immigrants, and it keeps absorbing new people. MNopedia notes that Minnesota's congressional hotdish contest gave Rep. Ilhan Omar second place in 2019 for her "Little Moga-Hot-dishu," and that chef Yia Vang published a Hmong hotdish recipe in 2021 ([MNopedia, Hotdish](https://www.mnhs.org/mnopedia/search/index/thing/hotdish-mnopedia)). A culture that can do that with a casserole can do it with its institutions.

## 16. Questions for reflection

These questions are meant for private reflection or small-group discussion. There are no right answers.

1. What did "being polite" mean in the home I grew up in? What was I taught not to talk about?
2. When I say "that's interesting" or "that's different" at work, what do I mean, and would a colleague from another background hear it that way?
3. When a meeting ends with no objections, how do I know whether people agreed?
4. Whose feedback do I find hardest to hear: feedback that is too blunt, or too gentle? What does that tell me about my own cultural settings?
5. Who have I invited into my home, or my circle at work, in the last year whom I did not know five years ago?
6. When I think "I treat everyone the same," what is the "same" based on? Whose way of doing things is the default?
7. What did I learn growing up about race in Minnesota, and what did I learn later? Who taught me each?
8. When a problem arises in a community, do I look first for causes inside our systems, or outside them?
9. How do I picture a "good outcome" for a person with a disability: living independently, working, making their own choices? Where did that picture come from, and would every family share it?
10. When a family wants to make decisions together, or care for someone at home, how do I respond? How does the planning process respond?
11. What do I know about the history of Minnesota's state institutions, eugenics laws and racial covenants? How might that history shape the trust a family gives me?
12. When our agency "engages the community," what do people see change afterward?
13. Which of the frameworks in this essay felt most true of me, and which least? Which made me uncomfortable, and why?
14. What strengths of Minnesota culture do I bring to my work, and how could I use them more deliberately with people whose culture differs from mine?
15. What is one habit I could practice this month to notice the water I swim in?

- **Sites that were not available.** The State Demographic Center, the Olmstead Implementation Office, the Department of Human Rights' web pages (its 2022 report PDF was available), Minnesota Management and Budget's inclusion page, the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences site (the article was read through PubMed Central), Minnesota Lawyer, Europe PMC, and Sahan Journal's topic pages could not be opened.
