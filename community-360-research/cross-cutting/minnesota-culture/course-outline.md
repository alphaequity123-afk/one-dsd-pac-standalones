# The Water We Swim In: course outline
Draft: October 31, 2026. Gary approved all six modules on October 31, 2026.

A voluntary e-learning course for staff of the Disability Services Division and the wider Department of Human Services, to be hosted on the Railway site. It is built from the essay `essay.md` in this folder, and every fact it shows comes from a source cited there. Six modules of about 20 minutes each, about two hours in all. Staff can take them in order or one at a time.

## Approach

- **Purpose.** Help staff see Minnesota's mainstream culture, which most of them share, as a culture: its real strengths and its blind spots. Then ask how it shapes their work with Minnesotans of other cultures and with disabled people and their families.
- **Tone.** Affectionate and honest. Open each module with recognition, often Minnesotans' own humor about themselves, before harder material. Strengths and blind spots appear together.
- **Evidence.** Facts come only from the essay's sources and are linked. People's views are attributed by name. Where research cannot say something about Minnesota, the course says so.
- **Frameworks.** Hall's high- and low-context communication, Hofstede's and Meyer's dimensions, Elazar's political cultures, Rentfrow's regional personality study, Harrington and Gelfand's tightness rankings, and Bennett's Developmental Model of Intercultural Sensitivity. Each is presented as a mirror for self-reflection, never as a score or label for anyone. The course does not use or mention the Intercultural Development Inventory, which is covered elsewhere in the app.
- **Reflection is private.** Prompts invite notes the learner keeps to themselves. Nothing is collected or graded.
- **Scenarios are labeled as examples.** Practice scenarios are composites written for teaching, clearly marked as examples, and never presented as real cases or real people.
- **No icons.** Gary's rule: the course pages use no icons, emoji, symbols or pictograms of any kind. Progress and navigation are shown in words.
- **Content notes.** Modules 4 and 5 carry content notes before the passages that describe racial violence, deaths and abuse in state institutions, following the rule in `RESEARCH-BRIEF.md`.

## Module structure (each about 20 minutes)

1. Opening (2 minutes): a recognizable Minnesota moment.
2. Learn (8 to 10 minutes): short text sections, real voices, one framework.
3. Try it (5 minutes): one example scenario with choices and feedback.
4. Reflect (3 minutes): two or three private prompts.
5. Take away (1 minute): one habit to practice.

## Modules

### 1. The water we swim in
**Goal.** Recognize that the majority culture is a culture, and why it is hard to see from inside.
- The fish-and-water idea (David Foster Wallace, 2005) and Bennett's point that people see their own culture in more detail than anyone else's.
- Who Minnesotans are now: the state's racial and ethnic makeup and how it is changing, with exact figures from the essay.
- Who works in state government: the fiscal 2023 workforce figures for the executive branch and DHS.
- **Try it.** An everyday office moment, read two ways.
- **Reflect.** Essay questions 1 and 15.
- **Take away.** Notice one moment this week when "normal" was really "how we do it here."

### 2. Minnesota nice, decoded
**Goal.** Understand the warmth, the indirectness and the newcomer freeze, and how each looks to people from other cultures.
- The warmth: helpfulness, giving and civic generosity.
- The code: "that's different," "interesting," the Minnesota no, the long goodbye, offering three times. Uses Minnesotans' own humor, labeled as humor.
- Conflict avoidance and passive aggression, from local reporting and commentary.
- The newcomer freeze: friendly but hard to befriend.
- What research shows: Minnesota's high agreeableness and low openness scores, and its middle rank on tightness. Hall's high and low context, as a question.
- **Try it.** A family member's direct complaint, and a staff reply that sounds polite but does not answer.
- **Reflect.** Essay questions 2, 3 and 4.
- **Take away.** Say one "no" or one concern plainly and kindly this week.

### 3. Where it comes from
**Goal.** Trace mainstream Minnesota culture to its roots.
- Scandinavian and German settlement; Lutheran and Catholic institutions; the "Jante law" against standing out.
- Farm and small-town interdependence, climate and cooperatives.
- Political culture: Elazar's "moralistic" Minnesota, the Progressive and Farmer-Labor traditions, high voter turnout and the "Minnesota Miracle."
- Values under the surface: self-reliance, egalitarianism and "treat everyone the same," modesty, privacy, consensus.
- **Try it.** Match values to everyday workplace habits.
- **Reflect.** Essay questions 6 and 14.
- **Take away.** Name one value you hold, and the history it came from.

### 4. Seen from the other side
*Carries content notes before the passages on racial violence.*
**Goal.** Hear how Minnesota's majority culture is experienced by Minnesotans of color, immigrants and newcomers, and face the state's racial history honestly.
- Voices from published sources describing Minnesota nice from outside the majority.
- The "Minnesota paradox": strong averages and some of the nation's widest racial gaps.
- History: racial covenants (Mapping Prejudice), Rondo and I-94, the 1920 Duluth lynchings, school and housing segregation.
- 2020 and after: George Floyd's murder, the Human Rights Department's 2022 findings on organizational culture, and the anti-racism and equity work since then, with the public debate about it shown fairly.
- **Try it.** A colleague of color describes a meeting differently than you saw it.
- **Reflect.** Essay questions 5, 7 and 8.
- **Take away.** Seek out one perspective you would not usually hear.

### 5. Culture in the system we run
*Carries a content note before the passage on state institutions.*
**Goal.** See how majority-culture values are built into disability policy and practice.
- Disability history as Minnesota history: Faribault and Cambridge, eugenics laws, Welsch v. Likins, the move out of institutions, the Olmstead plan.
- Values written into law: independence, self-determination and individual choice.
- How families from more interdependent cultures may see these differently, using examples documented in the community profiles.
- Data that raises questions: the 2021 DHS study of waiver use by race and ethnicity.
- Inside the agency: the 2025 state employee survey on voicing different views; silence read as agreement; the Legislative Auditor's 2026 findings, presented factually.
- **Try it.** A planning meeting where the family wants decisions made together and care kept at home.
- **Reflect.** Essay questions 9, 10 and 11.
- **Take away.** In your next plan, ask what a good life means to this person and family, in their words.

### 6. Seeing across difference
**Goal.** Use the frameworks as mirrors and choose practices to keep.
- Bennett's stages, explained in plain words, with the point that "treating everyone the same" can hide whose "same" it is. Offered as a mirror, not a test.
- Using frameworks well: averages, not people.
- Strengths of Minnesota culture to build on: fairness, follow-through, civic commitment and care.
- **Try it.** Revisit the scenario from Module 2 or 5 with new choices.
- **Reflect.** Essay questions 12 and 13.
- **Take away.** Choose one habit to practice for a month, and a way to notice whether it helps.

## Decisions for Gary

1. The course title: "The Water We Swim In" or another name.
2. Whether staff should see an optional, private progress tracker or none at all.

4. Who reviews the example scenarios before they go live.
5. Whether the course links to the essay and to the community profiles.
